Compound Interest

Compound Interest is the interest you earn not only on your original principal but also on the interest already added to it, so your money grows on a growing base. It is the single most powerful force behind long-term wealth creation, because each rupee of interest starts earning its own interest.

What is Compound Interest?

Compound Interest is interest calculated on the initial principal and on all the interest that has accumulated in earlier periods. Unlike Simple Interest, which pays a flat amount only on the original sum, compound interest keeps folding earned interest back into the base, so growth accelerates over time. Investors sometimes call this "interest on interest," and Einstein is popularly said to have called it the eighth wonder of the world.

Here is a relatable Indian example. Suppose you put ₹1,00,000 into an instrument paying 10% per annum. In year one you earn ₹10,000. In year two, you earn 10% not on ₹1,00,000 but on ₹1,10,000, which is ₹11,000. That extra ₹1,000 is compounding at work. Leave it untouched for 20 years and the same ₹1 lakh becomes roughly ₹6.7 lakh without adding a single extra rupee. This is exactly why a long-horizon SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) in a Mutual Fund can turn modest monthly savings into a large corpus.

  • P is the principal, the amount you invest at the start.
  • r is the annual interest rate written as a decimal (10% becomes 0.10).
  • n is the number of times interest is compounded per year (annually = 1, quarterly = 4, monthly = 12).
  • t is the time in years.

The interest earned alone is simply A minus P. Two levers matter most: the rate and the time. Because t sits in the exponent, time is astonishingly powerful. A quick mental shortcut is the Rule of 72: divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate the years needed to double your money. At 12%, money doubles in about 6 years; at 8%, about 9 years.

Compounding frequency: why "how often" matters

The more frequently interest is compounded, the more you earn, because interest starts working sooner. A bank Fixed Deposit in India usually compounds quarterly, while a Recurring Deposit also compounds quarterly. Worked example
Priya, a 30-year-old software engineer in Pune, invests a lump sum of ₹1,00,000 at 10% per annum compounded annually for 10 years. How much does she have at maturity, and how does compound interest compare with simple interest over the same period?

Step 1 — Set up the formula. Using A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) with P = ₹1,00,000, r = 0.10, n = 1, t = 10:

A = 1,00,000 × (1 + 0.10)^10 = 1,00,000 × (1.10)^10.

Step 2 — Compute the growth factor. (1.10)^10 = 2.5937. So A = 1,00,000 × 2.5937 = ₹2,59,374.

Step 3 — Find the interest earned. Interest = A − P = 2,59,374 − 1,00,000 = ₹1,59,374.

Step 4 — Compare with simple interest. Simple interest = P × r × t = 1,00,000 × 0.10 × 10 = ₹1,00,000, giving a maturity of just ₹2,00,000. The year-by-year build-up shows where the extra ₹59,374 comes from.

So what? Under simple interest Priya earns a flat ₹10,000 every year. Under compound interest, her annual interest climbs from ₹10,000 in year one to nearly ₹23,579 in year ten, because each year she earns interest on a larger base. That single behaviour — reinvesting interest and staying invested — hands her an extra ₹59,374, or roughly 59% more interest, on the exact same deposit. Stretch the horizon to 25 or 30 years and the gap becomes enormous. The lesson for Indian investors is blunt: start early, stay invested, and let time do the heavy lifting. Remember that Inflation eats into these returns, so compare against the RBI's approximately 4% CPI target (with a 2–6% tolerance band) to judge your real, inflation-adjusted gain.

Where you meet compound interest in India

Compounding works both for you and against you. It builds your Index Fund corpus and your PPF balance, but the same maths inflates the cost of unpaid EMI dues and, brutally, credit-card debt at 36–48% per annum. Bank FDs and RDs are protected by DICGC insurance up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank. Mismanaging debt hurts your Credit Score (CIBIL), which ranges from 300 to 900. The rule is simple: be the one earning compound interest, not the one paying it.

Check yourself
If you invest ₹50,000 at 8% per annum compounded annually, roughly how many years until it doubles?
About 9 years. Using the Rule of 72, 72 ÷ 8 = 9. So ₹50,000 becomes roughly ₹1,00,000 in about 9 years, without any additional investment.

Key takeaways

  • Compound interest pays interest on both principal and accumulated interest, so growth accelerates over time.
  • The formula is A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt); time (t) matters most because it sits in the exponent.
  • More frequent compounding (quarterly, monthly) produces a higher maturity value than annual compounding.
  • The Rule of 72 quickly estimates doubling time: 72 divided by the annual rate.
  • Starting early beats investing more later; a long horizon is the biggest advantage.
  • Compounding also works against you on credit-card and loan dues, so avoid high-cost debt.

FAQs

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal, so you earn the same amount each period. Compound interest is calculated on principal plus previously earned interest, so your returns grow faster over time. Over long horizons the difference becomes very large.

How does compounding frequency affect returns?

The more often interest is compounded, the sooner interest starts earning its own interest, so you finish with more. On the same rate and principal, monthly compounding beats quarterly, which beats annual. Most Indian bank FDs compound quarterly.

Is a SIP an example of compound interest?

Broadly yes. A SIP benefits from compounding when returns on your invested units are reinvested and themselves earn returns over time. The long-term power of a SIP comes largely from staying invested and letting gains compound, though market returns fluctuate year to year unlike a fixed rate.

What is the Rule of 72?

It is a shortcut to estimate how long money takes to double. Divide 72 by the annual return rate. At 12% money doubles in about 6 years, and at 9% in about 8 years. It is an approximation, not an exact figure.

Does inflation reduce the benefit of compound interest?

Yes. Your real return is the compounded return adjusted for inflation. With the RBI targeting around 4% CPI within a 2–6% band, an 8% FD delivers a real return closer to 4% before tax. Aim for investments that comfortably beat inflation over the long run.

Can compound interest work against me?

Absolutely. Credit-card balances and overdue loans compound against you, often at 36–48% per annum, so debt can balloon quickly. Paying dues on time protects both your wallet and your CIBIL score.
