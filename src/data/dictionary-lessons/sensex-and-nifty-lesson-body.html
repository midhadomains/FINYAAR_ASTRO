Sensex and Nifty

Sensex and Nifty are India's two headline stock market indices: the BSE Sensex tracks 30 large, established companies listed on the Bombay Stock Exchange, while the Nifty 50 tracks 50 large companies on the National Stock Exchange. Both are free-float market-capitalisation-weighted benchmarks that compress the mood of the entire Indian equity market into a single, live number.

What is Sensex and Nifty?

Sensex and Nifty are the two most quoted numbers in Indian finance. The Sensex (short for "Sensitive Index") is a basket of 30 blue-chip stocks on the BSE, launched with a base value of 100 for the base year 1978-79. The Nifty 50 is a basket of 50 large stocks on the NSE, launched with a base value of 1,000 on 3 November 1995. When you hear "the market is up 400 points today," someone is almost always talking about one of these two.

Think of it like a class result. Instead of tracking every one of India's 5,000+ listed equity shares individually, the index picks the biggest, most-traded "toppers" and reports their combined performance as one score. If most heavyweights rise, the index rises. Because both indices are dominated by giants like Reliance, HDFC Bank, TCS and Infosys, they move in near-lockstep, day after day.

How the index is calculated

Both indices use the free-float market-capitalisation method. A company's weight depends not on its share price but on the market capitalization of the shares that are actually available to the public, excluding promoter and government holdings. A stock with a bigger free-float market cap pushes the index more.

Where:

  • Free-float Market Cap = Share Price × Shares Outstanding × Free-float Factor, summed across all constituents. The free-float factor (0 to 1) is the fraction of shares freely tradeable in the market.
  • Base Market Cap = the total free-float value fixed at the starting date, used as a constant divisor.
  • Base Index Value = 100 for Sensex, 1,000 for Nifty.

Because prices update continuously through the trading day (9:15 am to 3:30 pm), the index ticks live. The heavier a stock's weight, the more its own volatility is transmitted into the headline number.

The Nifty's wider net makes it a slightly better proxy for the large-cap market, which is why most ETFs and derivatives are built on it. Both are reconstituted periodically to drop weak names and add rising ones.

Example

Worked example
A simplified 3-stock "Mini Nifty" starts with a base index value of 1,000 and a base market cap of ₹2,30,000 crore. Compute today's index level, then find the new level after one stock rises 10%.

Step 1 - Free-float market cap of each stock (Price × Shares × Free-float factor):

StockPriceSharesFree-floatFF Market CapWeight
A₹2,000100 crore0.50₹1,00,000 cr21.7%
B₹1,500200 crore0.80₹2,40,000 cr52.2%
C₹500400 crore0.60₹1,20,000 cr26.1%
Total free-float market cap₹4,60,000 cr100%

Stock A: 2,000 × 100 × 0.50 = ₹1,00,000 crore. Stock B: 1,500 × 200 × 0.80 = ₹2,40,000 crore. Stock C: 500 × 400 × 0.60 = ₹1,20,000 crore. Total = ₹4,60,000 crore.

Step 2 - Today's index level:

The Mini Nifty stands at 2,000, double its base level of 1,000.

Step 3 - Stock B rises 10%, from ₹1,500 to ₹1,650. Its new free-float market cap = 1,650 × 200 × 0.80 = ₹2,64,000 crore. A and C are unchanged, so the new total = 1,00,000 + 2,64,000 + 1,20,000 = ₹4,84,000 crore.

Step 4 - The "so what": the index jumped from 2,000 to 2,104, a gain of 104 points (+5.2%). Notice that Stock B rose 10% but the index rose only 5.2%, because B carries a 52.2% weight: 52.2% × 10% ≈ 5.2%. This is the core lesson of an index. A stock moves the headline number in proportion to its weight, not its percentage move. That is exactly why a single 4% swing in Reliance or HDFC Bank can drag the real Sensex hundreds of points, while a mid-cap doubling barely registers.

Why they matter and how to invest

Sensex and Nifty are barometers, benchmarks and building blocks all at once. Fund managers measure their skill against them, foreign investors read them as India's economic pulse, and ordinary savers use them as a low-cost way to own the whole market. You cannot buy the index directly, but you can buy an index fund or ETF that mirrors it for a tiny expense ratio.

A monthly SIP into a Nifty 50 index mutual fund is one of the most common ways Indians build long-term wealth, harnessing compound interest and instant diversification across 50 companies. Historically these indices have delivered roughly 11-12% annualised over long horizons, comfortably ahead of a fixed deposit and of inflation, though with far more risk and return swings along the way. Valuations are often gauged using the index P/E ratio and dividend yield.

Check yourself
If a stock with a 20% index weight falls 15% while everything else stays flat, roughly how much does the index move?
About -3%. The index change equals weight × price change = 0.20 × (-15%) = -3%. The stock's own drop is 15%, but the index only feels its weighted share.

Key takeaways

  • Sensex = 30 BSE stocks (base 100, 1978-79); Nifty 50 = 50 NSE stocks (base 1,000, 3 Nov 1995).
  • Both use free-float market-cap weighting, so bigger companies move the index more.
  • A stock changes the index in proportion to its weight, not its raw percentage move.
  • You invest through index funds and ETFs that track them for a low expense ratio.
  • They are benchmarks for fund performance and a proxy for India's economic health.

Frequently asked questions

Which is better, Sensex or Nifty?

Neither is objectively better; they track almost the same large-cap market and move together. Nifty 50 is broader (50 vs 30 stocks) and hosts most ETFs and derivatives, so it is often preferred as a benchmark. For a long-term investor the practical difference is negligible.

Can I buy the Sensex or Nifty directly?

No. An index is just a number, not a security. You gain exposure by buying an index fund or ETF that replicates it, or through index futures and options. A Nifty 50 or Sensex index fund via SIP is the simplest route for most people.

Why do Sensex and Nifty move together?

Because the same heavyweight companies dominate both baskets. Reliance, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, TCS and Infosys carry large weights in each index, so when they move, both indices move nearly identically, usually within a few tenths of a percent of each other.

How are companies chosen for the index?

Stocks are selected on criteria like large free-float market capitalisation, high liquidity, and a solid listing history. Index committees review the list periodically, dropping companies that shrink or turn illiquid and adding rising large-caps. This keeps the index representative of the current market leaders.

What returns have Sensex and Nifty given historically?

Over multi-decade periods both have delivered roughly 11-12% annualised, including price gains and reinvested dividends. Returns are lumpy, though: some years are strongly positive, others sharply negative. Equity indices reward patience, not short-term timing.

Does the index include dividends?

The headline Sensex and Nifty are price indices, so they capture only price changes, not dividends. Separate "Total Return Index" (TRI) versions add reinvested dividends and are the fair benchmark for comparing a fund's performance.
