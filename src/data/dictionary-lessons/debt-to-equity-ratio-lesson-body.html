Debt-to-Equity Ratio

Debt-to-Equity Ratio measures how much borrowed money a company uses for every rupee its shareholders put in or left behind. It is total borrowings divided by shareholders' funds, meaning share capital plus reserves and surplus. A ratio of 1.5 says the business runs on one and a half rupees of debt for each rupee of owner capital, which sharpens returns while the good years last and shortens the fuse when they do not. A comfortable level is set by the industry, the steadiness of the cash flows and the loan terms, never by one figure that travels everywhere.

What the numerator is allowed to count

Debt here means borrowed money carrying an explicit interest cost and a fixed repayment date. That pulls in term loans, working capital borrowings, debentures, commercial paper and the portion of long-term loans due within twelve months, a line many readers miss because it sits among current liabilities. Since Ind AS 116, lease liabilities belong there too, a long lease committing the company to dated payments as a loan does.

Supplier credit stays out because it costs no stated interest and unwinds as stock is sold. A firm stretching its payables is under strain all the same.

Setting the two sides down

Long-term borrowings are loans repayable after twelve months, short-term borrowings those due sooner, including current maturities of the long-term loans. Share capital is the face value of shares issued and paid for. Reserves and surplus, shown as other equity under Ind AS, is everything built up afterwards: retained profits, securities premium, general reserve and any revaluation surplus. Those two together make shareholders' funds, or net worth, the quantity behind book value per share.

Two variants recur. Net debt to equity subtracts cash and liquid investments from the top, on the argument that money available to repay tomorrow is not really a burden. Lenders often insist on tangible net worth below, stripping out intangibles so the covenant rests on assets that could be realised.

Comfortable is an industry judgement

Cash flow stability decides how much borrowing a business can carry. A road or transmission project with a long concession and contracted revenue services heavy debt through a downturn, because next year's receipts are already written down somewhere. A capital goods maker whose order book empties in a recession cannot.

Banks and non-banking financial companies sit outside the comparison altogether, since borrowing is their raw material and deposits fund the loans they make. Their safety is regulated through capital adequacy requirements set by the RBI. Compare a company with its own industry and its own past, and treat any threshold offered as a universal rule with care, because the right level moves with interest rates, asset lives and the predictability of demand.

The ratio never travels alone

Leverage measured on the balance sheet says how much is owed, not whether the company can pay. Interest cover, operating profit divided by the interest bill, gets closer, and net debt to EBITDA says how many years of operating earnings the borrowings represent. Beside those sit the maturity profile, since a wall of repayments in one year is dangerous at any ratio, and the interest rate itself.

Hold on to the link with returns. Borrowing lifts shareholder returns only while the business earns more on its capital than the loan costs, which is what ROCE measures against the interest rate. Once that gap closes, every rupee of debt works against the owners, and because losses reduce reserves, the ratio climbs by itself. Lenders holding collateral and covenants react faster than shareholders do.

Worked example
Anjali Deshmukh is finance head at an unnamed packaging company in Aurangabad. The business needs ₹100 crore of capital either way. Structure A raises ₹80 crore from shareholders and borrows ₹20 crore. Structure B raises ₹40 crore and borrows ₹60 crore. Loans cost 10 per cent and tax is 25 per cent. A good year brings operating profit of ₹18 crore, a bad year ₹6 crore. What does the choice actually do?

Step 1, the two ratios. Structure A carries ₹20 crore of debt against ₹80 crore of equity, a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.25. Structure B carries ₹60 crore against ₹40 crore, a ratio of 1.5. Interest is ₹2 crore a year under A and ₹6 crore under B, fixed whatever the year brings.

Step 2, the good year. On ₹18 crore of operating profit, A pays ₹2 crore interest, is taxed ₹4 crore on the ₹16 crore left and keeps ₹12 crore, or 15 per cent on its ₹80 crore of equity. B pays ₹6 crore interest, is taxed ₹3 crore on ₹12 crore and keeps ₹9 crore, which on ₹40 crore of equity is 22.5 per cent. Less profit, better return.

Step 3, the bad year. On ₹6 crore, A pays its ₹2 crore, is taxed ₹1 crore on ₹4 crore and still earns ₹3 crore, a thin but positive 3.75 per cent. B pays ₹6 crore and has nothing left. Its owners earn zero and its interest cover falls to exactly 1.0.

So what? Leverage bought 7.5 extra points of return in the good year and cost the whole of it in the bad one. The pivot is the 10 per cent loan rate: ₹18 crore of operating profit is 18 per cent on capital, so borrowing pays, while ₹6 crore is 6 per cent, so it does not. Below ₹6 crore, Structure B runs a loss that eats into reserves, and the ratio climbs past 1.5 without a rupee being borrowed.

Check yourself
Q
A company holds ₹300 crore of borrowings, ₹120 crore of cash and ₹200 crore of net worth. What are its gross and net debt-to-equity ratios?
A: Gross is ₹300 crore over ₹200 crore, which is 1.5. Net debt of ₹180 crore over the same net worth gives 0.9. The gap matters only if that cash is genuinely free rather than pledged or already committed.

Key takeaways

  • It divides total borrowings by shareholders' funds, that is share capital plus reserves and surplus.
  • Interest-bearing borrowings and lease liabilities count; trade payables, provisions and deferred tax do not.
  • Leverage raises shareholder returns only while the return on capital employed exceeds the interest rate.
  • A safe level depends on how predictable the cash flows are, so judge it within an industry.
  • Losses shrink reserves and push the ratio up by themselves, which is how leverage compounds trouble.

Frequently asked questions

Is there an ideal debt-to-equity ratio?

No figure works across industries. Utilities and infrastructure projects with contracted revenue support far more debt than cyclical manufacturers, while many service businesses carry almost none. Compare with close peers and with the company's own record.

Do trade payables count as debt in this ratio?

Not in the standard calculation, because payables arise from trading and carry no stated interest. A broader total-liabilities-to-equity measure exists, but that is a different ratio.

What is the difference between gross and net debt to equity?

Gross uses all borrowings. Net subtracts cash and liquid investments first, on the view that money available to repay a loan offsets it, which is fairer for cash-rich companies.

Why do banks and finance companies show very high ratios?

Borrowing is their business model rather than a funding choice, since deposits and market borrowings become the loans they advance. Their solvency is governed by RBI capital adequacy rules instead.

Do lease liabilities have to be included?

Under Ind AS 116 most leases appear as a right-of-use asset and a matching liability that behaves like debt. Including it makes retailers and airlines comparable with firms that bought such assets on loan.

Can the ratio be negative?

Yes, when accumulated losses exceed share capital and reserves. The number then stops describing leverage and becomes a solvency alarm.
