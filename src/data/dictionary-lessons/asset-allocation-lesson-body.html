Asset Allocation

Asset Allocation is the decision about how your money is divided across asset classes, chiefly equity, debt, gold and cash, rather than which particular share or scheme you buy inside each class. It is driven by the goal you are funding, the years before you need the money, and the fall in value you can tolerate without abandoning the plan. An Indian portfolio usually carries its debt sleeve through EPF, PPF, deposits and debt funds, and its equity sleeve through shares or mutual funds. Over long periods this split explains far more of the variation in results than the individual securities picked inside it.

The mix decides more than the picks

Two investors can hold the same twelve stocks and end up in completely different places, because one keeps 80 per cent of the portfolio in those stocks and the other keeps 30 per cent. Equity delivers the growth and also the drawdowns. Debt supplies predictability. Gold moves on its own logic, often firming when equities are under stress. Cash earns least and does the job the others cannot: being available the day you need it.

This is where diversification and allocation part company. Diversification spreads money within a class so one company cannot sink you. Allocation decides how much of the portfolio faces that class at all, the larger lever on risk and return.

Fitting the mix to the goal and the horizon

Allocation is decided goal by goal, not for a lump of money in the abstract. A down payment due in eighteen months and a retirement corpus due in twenty-five years should not sit in the same mix. The shorter the horizon, the less the portfolio can afford to be waiting on a recovery.

Age matters, but capacity matters more. A 45-year-old on a stable government salary can carry more equity than a 30-year-old freelancer.

Strategic, tactical and the drift in between

Your strategic allocation is the long-run target you commit to, say 60 per cent equity, 30 per cent debt and 10 per cent gold. Tactical allocation is a deliberate short-term deviation of a few points, taken because valuations or rates look unusual. Drift is neither: it happens while you do nothing.

V subscript i is the rupee value of one asset class, the denominator is the portfolio total across all n classes, and w subscript i is that class as a percentage of the whole. Run it after a strong year and the answer is rarely the number you set: a bull run lifts the equity weight simply because that sleeve grew fastest, so the portfolio now takes more risk than you agreed to.

Rebalancing, and what it costs here

Rebalancing means selling a little of what has run up and buying what has lagged, until the weights match the target again. Done on a fixed date each year, or whenever a sleeve strays beyond a band such as five percentage points, it removes the guesswork and imposes the discipline instinct resists: trimming the winner.

The friction is real and worth planning around. Redeeming equity mutual fund units can trigger capital gains tax and, inside the exit-load window, a small charge; ELSS units cannot be moved during the statutory lock-in. A cheaper route is to rebalance with new money, pointing the next few SIP instalments at the underweight sleeve instead of selling the overweight one.

Worked example
Deepa Iyer, a dentist in Coimbatore, holds ₹20,00,000 across three sleeves at her target of 60 per cent equity, 30 per cent debt and 10 per cent gold. Over the next year equity gains 35 per cent, her debt funds return 7 per cent and gold rises 12 per cent. What has happened to her plan, and what should she do about it?

Step 1, the starting position. The target puts ₹12,00,000 in equity, ₹6,00,000 in debt and ₹2,00,000 in gold. She has not bought or sold anything since setting it, and she checks the mix once a year in April.

Step 2, what a good year did to the mix. Equity grows to ₹16,20,000, debt to ₹6,42,000 and gold to ₹2,24,000. The portfolio is now worth ₹24,86,000, a gain of ₹4,86,000 on the year, and it is very tempting to stop reading at that line.

Step 3, the drift nobody chose. Applying the weight formula, equity is 16,20,000 ÷ 24,86,000, or 65.2 per cent, against a target of 60. Debt has slipped to 25.8 per cent and gold to 9.0. Deepa never decided to raise her equity exposure by more than five percentage points; the market decided it for her, and the next correction will now hurt more than the plan ever allowed for.

Step 4, the trades that restore it. Applying the target percentages to the new total of ₹24,86,000, rather than to the old total, gives the amount each sleeve should now hold. The sales and the purchases net to zero, so no fresh money is required.

Step 5, the cheaper version. Selling ₹1,28,400 of equity may attract capital gains tax and, if any units are inside the exit-load window, a small charge as well. If Deepa instead points her monthly SIP entirely at debt and gold for the next several months, fresh money closes most of the gap, nothing is redeemed and no tax event is triggered at all.

So what? Rebalancing is not a market call, it is maintenance. Left alone, a rising market quietly converts a moderate portfolio into an aggressive one, and the investor usually discovers this only when the fall arrives and the loss is larger than the plan ever allowed for.

Check yourself
Q
A portfolio set at 70 per cent equity is now at 78 per cent after a strong year. Nothing was bought or sold. What changed?
A: The equity sleeve grew faster than the others, so it now forms a larger share of a bigger total. The risk profile rose without any decision being taken, which is what rebalancing corrects.

Key takeaways

  • Asset allocation is the split across equity, debt, gold and cash, and it drives most of the outcome.
  • Set the mix goal by goal, since the horizon decides how much equity is sensible.
  • Strategic allocation is the committed target, tactical a deliberate tilt, drift the unplanned change.
  • A sleeve's weight is its value divided by the portfolio total, so a fast-growing sleeve raises risk on its own.
  • Rebalance on a date or a band, using fresh contributions first to keep tax and exit loads down.

Frequently asked questions

Is the rule of 100 minus your age a good allocation?

It is a starting point, not an answer, because it uses only age and ignores income stability, dependents and the goal date. Two people of the same age with different job security should not carry the same weight in equity.

How often should I rebalance?

Once a year on a fixed date suits most household portfolios, or whenever a sleeve drifts beyond a set band. More frequent rebalancing adds cost and tax for little gain.

Does allocation replace choosing good funds?

No, it comes first and the fund choice follows. Get the equity share right and a reasonable index fund will do; get it wrong and no fund selection rescues you.

How much gold belongs in an Indian portfolio?

Investors commonly hold a modest share as a diversifier, since gold tends not to move with equities. Jewellery is not the investment sleeve, because making charges and resale losses eat the return.

Do EPF and PPF count towards my allocation?

Yes. They are debt holdings and often the largest a salaried Indian owns, so leaving them out makes a portfolio look far more equity-heavy than it is.

Should I change my allocation when markets fall?

Changing the target because of a fall usually locks in losses at the worst moment. Rebalancing back to the existing target means buying equity cheaper, a different action altogether.
