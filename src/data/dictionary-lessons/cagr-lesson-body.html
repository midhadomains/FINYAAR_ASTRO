CAGR

CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate) is the single constant annual rate that would carry a starting value to an ending value over a stated number of years. It answers one narrow question: at what steady speed did this investment travel, ignoring everything that happened between the first day and the last. Fund factsheets, Sensex charts and property brochures across India quote it because one clean percentage compares things that grew over different periods. That convenience is also its limitation, since the smoothing throws away the path.

An imaginary steady rate, fitted afterwards

No investment in India actually grows at its CAGR. A flexi-cap fund reporting 14 per cent over ten years delivered 14 per cent in none of those years and probably lost money in two or three. The rate is fitted to the endpoints afterwards, the way a line is drawn through the first and last dots and everything between is rubbed out.

What survives is still useful. Because the rate compounds, it respects the arithmetic of compound interest instead of dividing total growth by the number of years, and it sets a five-year property gain beside a three-year mutual fund return on comparable terms.

Two values and a count of years

Three inputs are needed, and none describes the middle of the journey.

V with subscript n is the value at the end, V with subscript zero the value at the start, and n the number of years between them. Dividing the two values gives the total growth multiple, raising that multiple to the power one over n asks what yearly multiple repeated n times would produce it, and subtracting one converts the multiple into a rate. Multiply by 100 to express it as a percentage.

A holding that moved from ₹4,00,000 to ₹9,00,000 in six years has a growth multiple of 2.25, and 2.25 raised to the power one-sixth is roughly 1.1447, so the CAGR is about 14.5 per cent a year. Note that n counts years, so eighteen months enters as 1.5.

Where the number quietly misleads

Two failures matter for an Indian household. The first is psychological: a fund quoting a handsome long-run CAGR may have halved somewhere along the way, and whoever sold at that low never received the advertised rate. The figure describes an investor who held from the first day to the last and never flinched, a smaller group than it suggests. Read it alongside the year-by-year table and some measure of volatility.

The second failure is mechanical. CAGR takes one inflow at the start and one value at the end, so money arriving in instalments defeats it. Feed it a SIP and you must pretend the whole amount went in on day one. The correct tool for dated, irregular cash flows is XIRR.

Worked example
Sneha Kulkarni, a dentist in Pune, put ₹10,00,000 into a large-cap fund in April 2020 and another ₹10,00,000 into a small-cap fund on the same day. Five years later both holdings are worth ₹20,00,000. Her advisor says the two funds performed identically. Was that a fair thing to say?

Step 1, the rate itself. Both holdings doubled, so the growth multiple is 2 and the period is five years. Two raised to the power one-fifth is 1.148698, which makes the CAGR 14.87 per cent a year for each fund. On that measure the advisor is right, and the two lines in her portfolio statement are indistinguishable.

Step 2, the doubling check. The rule of 72 divides 72 by 14.87 and suggests about 4.8 years to double. The money actually doubled in five. That is close enough to confirm the arithmetic, and a reminder that the shortcut runs slightly fast at this speed. Both funds pass the check identically, because both doubled over the same five years, which is the only information the CAGR formula was ever given.

Step 3, opening the years back up. Reconstructing the value at each anniversary tells a very different story.

Step 4, what she actually lived through. The large-cap fund never had a down year and its worst twelve months still added 13.9 per cent. The small-cap fund showed her ₹6,20,000 at the first anniversary, a loss of ₹3,80,000 on a statement she opened at her clinic, and it was still below her purchase price three years in. Its best year and worst year sit 121.5 percentage points apart.

Step 5, the exit that did not happen. Had she sold the small-cap holding at the end of year three, her realised CAGR would have been roughly negative 7.6 per cent a year, not 14.87. The advertised rate existed only for an investor who sat through the third year without selling.

So what? Identical CAGRs describe identical destinations, never identical journeys. Use the figure to compare endpoints and to size a long-term goal, then insist on the year-by-year table before deciding how much of your money can survive the ride. And if Sneha had been adding ₹25,000 every month instead of investing once, CAGR would have been the wrong instrument entirely.

Check yourself
Q
A fund quotes a five-year CAGR of 18 per cent. Your friend says she therefore earned 18 per cent last year in that fund. What is wrong with that?
A: CAGR is an average speed fitted to the first and last values, so no single year need resemble it. Last year could have been a loss. Only the year-wise returns tell you what any particular twelve months delivered.

Key takeaways

  • CAGR is the constant annual rate that links a starting value to an ending value over n years.
  • It compounds properly, so it beats dividing total growth by the number of years.
  • It deliberately erases the path, so two investments with the same CAGR can feel nothing alike.
  • Read it beside the year-wise returns and the deepest fall before trusting it.
  • With money going in at different dates, CAGR breaks down and XIRR is the right measure.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good CAGR for an Indian equity mutual fund?

There is no fixed benchmark, and any number quoted applies to a particular past period. Compare against the scheme's own benchmark index and its category over the same window, not against a figure remembered from another decade.

Can CAGR be negative?

Yes. If the ending value is lower than the starting value, the growth multiple is below one and the formula returns a negative rate. A holding that fell from ₹5,00,000 to ₹4,00,000 over three years has a CAGR of roughly negative 7.2 per cent a year.

How is CAGR different from absolute return?

Absolute return states total growth without reference to time, so 60 per cent could have taken two years or twelve. CAGR spreads that growth across the years in a compounding way, which makes different holding periods comparable.

Should I use CAGR for my SIP returns?

No. Each instalment has been invested for a different length of time, and CAGR assumes one lump sum on day one. Applying it to a SIP typically understates the real return substantially. XIRR handles dated instalments correctly.

Is CAGR the same as annualised return?

For a single inflow and a single outflow the two terms mean the same thing, and factsheets often say annualised return where they mean CAGR. Where cash flows are irregular it is computed as XIRR instead.

Does CAGR account for inflation or tax?

Neither, unless you build them in yourself. A quoted CAGR is gross, pre-tax and nominal. Subtract inflation for a real rate, and subtract the capital gains you will owe for what you keep.
