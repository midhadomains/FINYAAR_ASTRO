Depreciation

Depreciation is the accounting method of spreading the cost of a long-lived physical asset over the years it is expected to be useful, so that each year carries a fair share of the asset’s wear and tear rather than the whole cost hitting the books in the year of purchase. In an Indian company’s accounts, depreciation turns a one-time cash outflow on a machine, building or vehicle into a series of yearly non-cash expenses that reduce reported profit and slowly lower the asset’s value on the balance sheet.

Why assets lose value on paper

When a business buys a fixed asset — a lathe, a delivery van, an office fit-out — it pays cash today but expects the asset to earn revenue for many years. Accounting matches this reality using the matching principle: the cost should be recognised in the same periods that the asset helps generate income. Depreciation is how that matching happens. A van bought for ₹12,00,000 that lasts eight years is not a ₹12,00,000 expense in year one; it is roughly ₹1,50,000 of expense each year, reflecting the slice of the van “used up” that year.

Physical assets lose value for real reasons — wear from use, ageing, rust, and obsolescence as newer models arrive. Land is the usual exception; it is generally not depreciated because it does not wear out. Everything else that is tangible and has a limited life gets depreciated.

Straight-line versus written-down value

Indian companies mainly use two methods. The straight-line method (SLM) charges the same amount every year, computed from cost, salvage (residual) value and useful life.

The written-down-value method (WDV), also called the reducing-balance method, applies a fixed percentage to the asset’s remaining book value each year. Because the base shrinks, the rupee charge is high early and falls over time.

WDV suits assets that lose most value early — laptops, phones, vehicles — while SLM suits assets that wear evenly, such as buildings and furniture. The Income Tax Act in India prescribes WDV on blocks of assets for tax, whereas companies often use SLM in their published accounts under the Companies Act, so the same asset can carry two different depreciation figures for two different purposes.

Effect on the income statement and balance sheet

Depreciation appears as an expense on the income statement, reducing operating profit and therefore taxable income and net profit. On the balance sheet, accumulated depreciation piles up and is subtracted from the asset’s original cost to give its net book value. The table below shows how one machine looks under both methods.

Because depreciation lowers profit, it also feeds into ratios investors watch, such as return on equity. A heavier early charge under WDV depresses early-year profit and can temporarily flatter later-year ROE.

A non-cash expense in the cash flow statement

Here is the crucial subtlety: depreciation is a non-cash expense. No rupee leaves the bank when depreciation is booked — the cash already went out when the asset was purchased. So in the cash flow statement prepared by the indirect method, depreciation is added back to net profit when computing cash from operations. It reduced profit on paper, but it did not reduce cash, so it must be reversed to arrive at the true cash position.

Worked example
Anjali Deshpande runs a printing press in Pune. On 1 April she buys a digital printing machine for ₹6,00,000. She estimates a useful life of 5 years and a salvage value of ₹1,50,000. She wants to compare straight-line depreciation with written-down value at 25% per year for the first three years.

Step 1 — Straight-line charge. Using the SLM formula, annual depreciation is (₹6,00,000 − ₹1,50,000) ÷ 5 = ₹4,50,000 ÷ 5 = ₹90,000 every year. This figure never changes across the five years.

Step 2 — Written-down-value charge. Under WDV she applies 25% to the opening book value each year. Year 1: 25% of ₹6,00,000 = ₹1,50,000. Year 2: opening value is ₹6,00,000 − ₹1,50,000 = ₹4,50,000, so charge is 25% of ₹4,50,000 = ₹1,12,500. Year 3: opening value is ₹3,37,500, so charge is ₹84,375.

Step 3 — Compare the two paths.

YearSLM expenseSLM closing valueWDV expenseWDV closing value
1₹90,000₹5,10,000₹1,50,000₹4,50,000
2₹90,000₹4,20,000₹1,12,500₹3,37,500
3₹90,000₹3,30,000₹84,375₹2,53,125

Step 4 — Read the profit impact. In year 1, WDV charges ₹60,000 more than SLM (₹1,50,000 versus ₹90,000). That extra expense lowers Anjali’s reported profit and her tax in the first year, but by year 3 the WDV charge has fallen below the SLM charge, so profit is higher later.

So what? The machine is the same and the cash paid is the same ₹6,00,000 either way. Depreciation only decides the timing of the expense on paper. If Anjali wants to defer tax and match the machine’s fast early wear, WDV is attractive. If she wants steady, predictable profits to show a lender, SLM looks smoother. In both cases no cash moves when depreciation is booked, so when she prepares her cash flow statement she will add these amounts back to profit. Understanding this lets her plan tax and reassure her banker without being misled by a dip in book profit that never touched her bank balance.

Check yourself
Q
A company reports net profit of ₹8,00,000 after charging ₹2,00,000 depreciation. Roughly how much did depreciation reduce its cash this year?
A: Zero. Depreciation is a non-cash expense, so it reduced reported profit by ₹2,00,000 but did not reduce cash. In the cash flow statement it is added back to profit.

Key takeaways

  • Depreciation spreads a fixed asset’s cost across its useful life so each year bears a fair share of wear and tear.
  • Straight-line method charges an equal amount yearly; written-down value charges a fixed percentage on a shrinking book value, so it is front-loaded.
  • It reduces profit on the income statement and cuts the asset’s net book value on the balance sheet through accumulated depreciation.
  • It is a non-cash expense, added back to profit in the cash flow statement because no cash leaves when it is booked.
  • Indian firms often keep two figures — WDV on asset blocks for income tax and SLM in Companies Act accounts — for the same asset.

Frequently asked questions

Is depreciation the same as the asset losing market value?

No. Depreciation is an accounting allocation based on cost, useful life and method chosen. The asset’s real resale price in the market can be higher or lower than its book value at any point. A five-year-old car’s book value and its actual second-hand price rarely match.

Why is land not depreciated in Indian accounts?

Land is treated as having an unlimited useful life — it does not wear out or become obsolete the way a machine does. Since depreciation exists to spread cost over a limited life, land has no life to spread over. Buildings on the land are depreciated, but the land itself is not.

Can a company switch from SLM to WDV or back?

A change in depreciation method is treated as a change in accounting estimate under Indian accounting standards and is allowed only if it gives a more appropriate presentation. The company must disclose the change and its effect. It cannot be switched casually year to year just to manage reported profit.

How does depreciation lower my tax bill in India?

Depreciation is a deductible expense, so it reduces taxable business income. The Income Tax Act prescribes WDV rates on blocks of assets, and because WDV is front-loaded, businesses get larger deductions in an asset’s early years, deferring tax to later years.

What is the difference between depreciation, amortisation and depletion?

All three spread cost over time. Depreciation applies to tangible fixed assets like machines and vehicles. Amortisation applies to intangible assets such as patents and software. Depletion applies to natural resources like mines and quarries as they are extracted. The idea is identical; only the asset type differs.

What happens to depreciation when an asset is fully written down?

Once an asset reaches its salvage value or zero book value, no further depreciation is charged, even if it is still in use. It may continue to appear on the balance sheet at its residual value until it is sold or scrapped, at which point any gain or loss on disposal is recorded.
