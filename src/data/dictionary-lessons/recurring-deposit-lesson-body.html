Recurring Deposit

Recurring Deposit is a bank or post-office savings product in which you deposit a fixed sum every month for a chosen tenure, and the bank pays a pre-agreed rate of interest, compounded quarterly, so you receive a lump-sum maturity amount at the end. It is designed for people who want the discipline of monthly saving with the assured returns and capital safety of a term deposit.

What is a Recurring Deposit?

A Recurring Deposit (RD) lets you park a fixed instalment, say ₹5,000, every month into a bank or post-office account for a fixed tenure, usually 6 months to 10 years. In return the bank pays interest at a locked-in rate and, unlike a savings account, compounds it every quarter. When the RD matures you get back the total of all instalments plus the accumulated interest as a single lump sum.

Think of a Bengaluru software engineer who cannot commit a big amount in one shot but can spare ₹5,000 a month. An RD forces that discipline, much like a SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) does for mutual funds, but with a guaranteed rate and no market risk. It is the middle ground between a plain savings account and a Fixed Deposit: a Fixed Deposit needs one large amount upfront, while an RD builds the corpus in small monthly steps.

RDs are offered by every scheduled bank, small finance banks, and India Post. Deposits with banks are insured up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank by the DICGC (a subsidiary of the RBI), which is why RDs are considered a very low risk vs return instrument.

How the maturity value is calculated

The power of an RD comes from Compound Interest: interest earns interest each quarter. This is very different from Simple Interest, where only the original instalments earn interest. Over long tenures the gap between the two widens sharply, and the Rule of 72 gives a quick feel for how long money takes to double at a given rate.

RD vs FD vs SIP: how to choose

An RD is often confused with a Fixed Deposit and a SIP. They differ in how you invest, the return profile, and the risk taken.

Rule of thumb: use an RD when you have a steady monthly surplus and a short-to-medium goal (a trip, a gadget, an emergency buffer). Use a Fixed Deposit when a lump sum is already sitting idle. Use a SIP when the goal is 5+ years away and you can tolerate volatility in exchange for potentially higher returns that beat inflation.

If interest across all her RDs at one bank crossed ₹40,000 in a year, the bank would deduct 10% TDS; she would still owe tax at her slab, so a high earner keeps less than ₹2,324 after tax.

Check yourself
If two people invest the same total amount over a year, one via a ₹60,000 lump-sum FD and one via a ₹5,000-a-month RD at the same rate, who earns more interest and why?
A: The FD investor earns more. The full ₹60,000 in the FD stays invested for all 12 months, whereas in the RD only the first instalment gets a full year and later instalments earn interest for progressively fewer months. Because the average time invested in an RD is roughly half the tenure, the same total contribution earns less interest than a lump-sum FD held for the whole period.

Taxation, premature closure and eligibility

Tax: RD interest is fully taxable and added to your income under "Income from Other Sources," taxed at your slab. Banks deduct 10% TDS if total interest on your deposits at that bank exceeds ₹40,000 in a year (₹50,000 for senior citizens). Unlike a 5-year tax-saver FD, a normal RD gives no Section 80C deduction.

Premature closure: Most banks allow it after a lock-in of a few months but levy a penalty of 0.5% to 1% on the applicable rate. Missing an instalment usually attracts a small penalty and can lead to account closure after repeated defaults.

Eligibility: Any resident individual, minors (through a guardian), HUFs, and firms can open an RD; NRIs can open them through NRE or NRO accounts. Opening one has no bearing on your Credit Score (CIBIL), though banks may let you take a loan against the RD balance instead of breaking it, similar in spirit to how a lender sizes an EMI against your income.

Key takeaways

  • An RD builds a lump sum through fixed monthly deposits at an assured rate, usually compounded quarterly.
  • Bank RDs are insured up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank by the DICGC, making them very low risk.
  • Because early instalments stay invested longer, the effective one-year return is below the quoted rate; longer tenures compound better.
  • Interest is fully taxable at your slab, with 10% TDS above the ₹40,000 threshold, and there is no 80C benefit on a normal RD.
  • Choose an RD for disciplined short-term goals; consider a SIP for long-term, inflation-beating growth.

Frequently asked questions

Is a Recurring Deposit better than a Fixed Deposit?

Neither is universally better; they suit different situations. An FD earns more on the same total amount because the full sum stays invested for the whole term, so if you already have a lump sum, choose an FD. An RD is better when you can only save monthly and want to build the corpus gradually with discipline.

Is RD interest taxable in India?

Yes. RD interest is added to your total income and taxed at your slab rate. Banks deduct 10% TDS once interest across your deposits at that bank crosses ₹40,000 a year (₹50,000 for seniors). You can submit Form 15G/15H to avoid TDS if your income is below the taxable limit.

What happens if I miss a monthly RD instalment?

You will usually be charged a small default penalty for the missed month, and the maturity date or amount may be adjusted. Repeated defaults (often six consecutive missed instalments) can lead the bank to close the account prematurely and pay only the applicable interest with penalty.

Can I withdraw money from an RD before maturity?

Full premature withdrawal is generally allowed after a short lock-in, but attracts a penalty of about 0.5% to 1% on the rate. Partial withdrawal is usually not permitted; instead, many banks offer a loan or overdraft of up to 80–90% against the RD balance so you keep the deposit intact.

Does an RD beat inflation?

Often only marginally. With RBI targeting inflation at 4% (within a 2–6% band) and RDs paying around 6.5–7.5%, the real return after tax can be close to zero for high earners. For long-term goals, market-linked options like index funds may offer better inflation-adjusted returns, at the cost of higher risk.

Is a bank RD safe?

Yes, RDs are among the safest instruments. Bank deposits are insured up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank by the DICGC, and the rate is locked at opening, so market swings do not affect your maturity value. Post-office RDs carry a sovereign guarantee.
