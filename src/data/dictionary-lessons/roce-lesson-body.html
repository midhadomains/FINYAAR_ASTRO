FINYAAR DICTIONARY · RATIOS AND VALUATION

ROCE

ROCE (Return on Capital Employed) is the operating profit a company earns on every rupee of long-term capital put to work in the business. It divides earnings before interest and tax by capital employed, which an Indian annual report lets you read as total assets minus current liabilities, or as shareholders' funds plus long-term borrowings. Because the profit figure sits above the interest line, the ratio grades the entire capital base, borrowed money included. That makes it a check on return on equity, a number cheap debt can lift without any operation improving.

Which rupees count as capital employed

Capital employed is the money a business has tied up for the long haul: the funds shareholders left in, plus whatever lenders committed for years rather than weeks. Current dues come out first, since they revolve with the operating cycle and carry no stated interest. Two routes reach the same pool from opposite sides of the balance sheet.

Operating profit over the money it took to earn it

On top sits earnings before interest and tax, the line the income statement reaches after every operating cost, including depreciation, but before finance cost and tax.

EBIT is the profit the operations threw off before anything went to lenders or to the government. Total assets is everything the company owns at the reporting date, at the values its books carry. Current liabilities covers dues payable within twelve months, chiefly trade payables, provisions and short-term borrowings.

Keeping interest out of the numerator is the whole design: the denominator already counts the lender's money, so deducting the payment to that lender would mismatch the two halves. Putting EBITDA on top instead, as some do for capital-heavy firms, ignores the wearing out of the assets being judged.

Where it parts company with return on equity

Return on equity answers a narrower question: what the owners got on the owners' money. Debt sits outside it except through the interest it costs, so a company borrowing at nine per cent and employing that money at fourteen keeps the spread for shareholders.

ROCE refuses the trick. Borrowed rupee and owner rupee both sit in the denominator, and profit is struck before interest, so the ratio reports how the assets were run, not how they were paid for. Rising return on equity alongside flat ROCE and a climbing debt-to-equity ratio is a borrowing story, not an efficiency story. The spread also runs in reverse, and a business earning eleven per cent while paying twelve on its loans destroys value with every rupee raised.

Reading the number without being fooled

Capital employed is a snapshot at one date while EBIT accrues over twelve months, so the tidier practice is to average opening and closing figures, particularly after a large fund raise. A plant under construction sits in capital work in progress, swelling the denominator while adding nothing on top.

Age flatters too, since depreciated assets carry low written-down values and a mature factory can post a figure a competitor building that capacity today could never match. Acquisition goodwill pushes the other way, and one-off gains on land sales belong nowhere near EBIT. Judge the result against the company's own cost of capital and its own record, across a full cycle, since commodity producers swing from thirty per cent to single digits without management changing anything.

Worked example
Sandeep Vaidya, a research trainee in Indore, is comparing two unnamed manufacturers of similar size. Each employs ₹200 crore of capital and each reports a return on equity of 15 per cent. Company A is funded entirely by shareholders. Company B is funded half by shareholders and half by long-term debt costing 9 per cent. Tax runs at 25 per cent for both. Which is the better business?

Step 1, what Company A earns. With no borrowings, its EBIT of ₹40 crore passes untouched to profit before tax. Tax takes ₹10 crore and ₹30 crore remains, which on equity of ₹200 crore is the reported 15 per cent. Capital employed is also ₹200 crore, so ROCE is ₹40 crore over ₹200 crore, or 20 per cent.

Step 2, working backwards through Company B. Its equity base is only ₹100 crore, so 15 per cent means a profit after tax of ₹15 crore. Gross that up at 25 per cent tax and profit before tax is ₹20 crore. Add back interest of ₹9 crore, being 9 per cent on ₹100 crore of debt, and EBIT is ₹29 crore. Capital employed is once again ₹200 crore, so ROCE is 14.5 per cent.

Step 3, reading the gap. The same ₹200 crore produced ₹40 crore of operating profit at A and ₹29 crore at B. B matched A on return on equity by borrowing at 9 per cent and running that money at 14.5 per cent, keeping the 5.5 point spread for its owners. Take the debt away and B would have returned about 10.9 per cent on equity.

So what? Return on equity said these were twins. ROCE says one converts capital into operating profit far more efficiently and the other is renting that appearance from its lenders. Sandeep should also weigh the margin for error, because B earns only 5.5 points more on its capital than it pays on its debt, and a rise in borrowing costs or a soft year for EBIT lands on its shareholders first.

Check yourself
Q
A company reporting ROCE of 11 per cent plans to fund its expansion with a loan at 12 per cent. What should you ask?
A: Whether the new project can earn more than the existing base does, because at 11 per cent on capital against 12 per cent on debt, each borrowed rupee earns less than it costs. Leverage helps only while the return stays above the cost of the debt.

Key takeaways

  • ROCE is EBIT divided by capital employed, shown as a percentage.
  • Capital employed is total assets minus current liabilities, or shareholders' funds plus long-term borrowings.
  • Measuring profit before interest stops borrowing from inflating the ratio, as it does return on equity.
  • Leverage creates value only while ROCE exceeds the interest rate on the debt.
  • Capital work in progress, old assets and goodwill distort the denominator, so read several years together.

Frequently asked questions

What counts as a good ROCE?

There is no universal figure. The tests that matter are whether the ratio comfortably exceeds the cost of capital, whether it holds up across a cycle, and how it sits against the same industry.

How is ROCE different from return on equity?

Return on equity measures profit after interest and tax against shareholders' funds alone. ROCE measures profit before interest against equity and long-term debt together, so it reports operating efficiency instead.

Is ROCE the same as ROIC?

They are close cousins. Return on invested capital puts after-tax operating profit on top and nets surplus cash out of the base, while ROCE stays pre-tax and leaves cash in.

Why does ROCE fall when a company builds a new factory?

Money spent on a project under construction enters capital work in progress, so the denominator grows at once while the factory earns nothing. Many analysts recompute the ratio with that item excluded.

Can ROCE be compared across industries?

Only loosely. Software and consumer services employ little capital and post high ratios, while steel, cement and power carry enormous asset bases by necessity.

Should exceptional items stay inside EBIT?

No. A gain on selling land or a one-time impairment says nothing about the ongoing return on capital, so strip such items out and recompute.
