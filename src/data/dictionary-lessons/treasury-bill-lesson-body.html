Treasury Bill

Treasury Bill is a short-term instrument through which the Government of India borrows for up to one year, issued by the Reserve Bank of India in tenors of 91 days, 182 days and 364 days. It carries no coupon: it is sold at a discount to face value and redeemed at full face value on maturity, and the difference between the two is the investor's entire return. The minimum investment is ₹10,000, in multiples of that amount. Because the borrower is the sovereign and the tenor is short, treasury bills are the benchmark for safety among rupee instruments.

No coupon, only a discount

Buy a 91-day bill with a face value of ₹100 for ₹98.45 and nothing further happens for three months. No interest lands in the account, no reinvestment decision arises. On the maturity date the Reserve Bank pays ₹100, and the ₹1.55 that appeared from nowhere is the interest, delivered as a price concession at the start rather than as cash along the way. A bond with a stated coupon rate does the opposite, drip-feeding interest and repaying face value intact.

That structure has a convenient consequence. With only one future cash flow there is no coupon reinvestment risk before maturity; rolling the proceeds into a new bill exposes the investor to future rates. The price reacts to rate moves far less violently than a long G-Sec does. Held to maturity, a bill returns exactly what was knowable on the day it was bought.

Wednesdays at the Reserve Bank

All three tenors are generally auctioned on Wednesdays, usually settling the next working day; holidays and RBI notifications can change the schedule. An indicative quarterly calendar tells the market roughly how much borrowing is coming and in which maturities, and a specific notification about a week ahead names the amount for each tenor. Bids are quoted in price terms and allotted on a multiple price basis.

Individuals are not expected to compete with treasuries and primary dealers on price. A non-competitive window, capped at five percent of the notified amount, lets a retail bidder name an amount and take allotment at the weighted average price of the successful competitive bids. It is reachable through RBI Retail Direct, a bank or a primary dealer.

Turning a price into a rate you can compare

A discount of ₹1.55 means little until it is annualised, because 91 days of return is not comparable to a year on a fixed deposit. The Reserve Bank annualises on an actual over 365 basis.

Y is the annualised yield in percent, P is the price paid for every ₹100 of face value, D is the number of days to maturity, and 365 is the assumed length of the year. The first fraction is the return over the holding period; the second scales it up to a full year. The denominator is the price paid, not face value. This is a simple annualised yield, not a guaranteed one-year return or an effective compounded rate.

Who lines up to buy them

Banks hold bills because they count towards the statutory liquidity ratio and because a parking space maturing in weeks suits money that cannot be locked away. Liquid mutual fund schemes buy them for the same reason, corporate treasuries to hold quarter-end cash without credit risk. Retail participation has grown since Retail Direct opened the auction to individuals.

Worked example
Arvind Nambiar runs a small architecture practice in Kochi and has received ₹4,92,250 as an advance on a project. The money is committed to a site purchase roughly three months away, so it cannot be risked and cannot be locked beyond that. He places a non-competitive bid for a 91-day treasury bill through RBI Retail Direct and is allotted ₹5,00,000 of face value at a price of ₹98.45 per ₹100. What does he earn in rupees, what is that as an annual rate, and how does it compare with a 91-day bank deposit at 6.00 percent?

Step 1, what he pays and what he gets back. Face value of ₹5,00,000 divided by 100 gives 5,000 units. At ₹98.45 each, the outgo is 5,000 × ₹98.45, or ₹4,92,250, exactly his advance as the final purchase cost. The auction price is unknown when he bids, and Retail Direct can require funding based on an indicative price plus a markup, with excess funds refunded after allotment. He therefore needs sufficient upfront funds beyond this illustrative final cost. On the maturity date the Reserve Bank credits the full ₹5,00,000. His rupee gain is ₹5,00,000 − ₹4,92,250, which is ₹7,750.

Step 2, the return over the 91 days. Measured against the money he actually parted with, ₹7,750 on ₹4,92,250 is 1.5744 percent. That is the whole of it for the period, and it is fixed the moment the allotment is confirmed.

Step 3, scaling it to an annual figure. Applying the yield relation, 1.55 divided by 98.45 gives 0.015744, and multiplying by 365 divided by 91 gives 0.063149, an annualised yield of 6.31 percent. The multiplier of 4.011 is simply how many 91-day stretches fit into a year.

Step 4, the same money in a short deposit. Assuming simple interest on an actual/365 basis and ignoring tax and charges, a 91-day deposit at 6.00 percent on ₹4,92,250 pays ₹4,92,250 × 0.06 × 91 ÷ 365, or ₹7,364. The bill hands him ₹386 more on the identical sum over the identical period, and without bank credit risk, since the payer is the Government of India. To match it, the bank would have to quote 6.31 percent.

So what? For money with a known short deadline, a treasury bill turns an idle current account balance into a sovereign obligation with a certain payoff date. The extra ₹386 matters less than the two structural points: Arvind knows his exact rupee outcome in advance, and he is not relying on any bank staying solvent for three months.

Check yourself
Q
A 182-day bill is bought at ₹96.90 per ₹100 of face value. Is the annualised yield higher or lower than 3.20 percent?
A: Higher. The ₹3.10 discount on a price of ₹96.90 is 3.20 percent for 182 days, and annualising by 365 divided by 182 roughly doubles it, to about 6.42 percent. Always check whether a quoted return covers the tenor or a year.

Key takeaways

  • The Reserve Bank issues treasury bills for the Government of India in 91-day, 182-day and 364-day tenors only.
  • They carry no coupon; the return is the gap between the discounted price and the face value repaid.
  • Auctions are generally on Wednesdays with next-working-day settlement; the issue notification overrides the indicative calendar.
  • Individuals bid non-competitively through RBI Retail Direct, with retail allotment capped at five percent of the notified amount.
  • Annualise the discount on an actual over 365 basis before comparing a bill with a deposit or fund.

Frequently asked questions

What tenors do Indian treasury bills come in?

Three: 91 days, 182 days and 364 days. Nothing longer exists, since borrowing beyond twelve months goes through dated securities. Cash management bills of other short maturities appear occasionally for temporary mismatches.

Can I sell a treasury bill before maturity?

Yes. Bills trade in the secondary market, and a Retail Direct holder can place a sell request on the platform. Execution depends on available buyers and the market price. An early sale can realise a loss; the maturity payoff does not guarantee an early-sale price.

Are treasury bills safer than a fixed deposit?

In credit terms, yes. A bill is a direct obligation of the Government of India; a deposit is an obligation of a bank, insured only up to the deposit insurance limit per depositor per bank.

How is the return on a treasury bill taxed?

The discount is not automatically tax-free. Treatment depends on the applicable law, the holder and whether the bill is held as an investment or trading asset. Confirm the classification and rate for redemption or sale with the Income Tax Department or a tax adviser before calculating a post-tax return.

What is the minimum I can invest?

₹10,000 of face value, and multiples of ₹10,000 above that. Because bills sell at a discount, the cash you pay is a little less than the face value you bid for.

Why do treasury bill yields move with the repo rate?

Bills sit at the shortest end of the government yield curve, so they track the cost of very short-term money closely. Policy expectations, banking-system liquidity and demand also affect auction yields, so the response is not a fixed or guaranteed timetable.
