G-Sec

Government securities (G-Secs) are tradeable debt instruments issued by the central or state governments. They include Treasury Bills, dated Government of India securities and State Development Loans. This lesson focuses on dated central-government securities, commonly paying half-yearly coupons and redeeming face value on a stated maturity date. Individuals can own securities through RBI Retail Direct or gain indirect exposure through gilt funds. RBI describes rupee government securities as carrying no domestic credit risk, but their market prices remain exposed to interest-rate and liquidity risk.

Lending to the sovereign, in writing and for a long time

When the central government spends more than it collects, the shortfall is funded largely by selling dated securities to the market. A security named 7.10 percent GS 2034 identifies its coupon and maturity year: it pays 7.10 percent of face value a year in two instalments, and hands the face value back in 2034. It trades freely in between, which is what separates a G-Sec from a small savings scheme.

Dated securities are auctioned weekly, ordinarily on Fridays, settling the next working day, under an indicative half-yearly calendar. Sovereign borrowing for a year or less goes through the treasury bill route instead.

Two doors open to an individual investor

The direct door is RBI Retail Direct. An individual with a rupee savings account, a PAN and valid KYC opens a Retail Direct Gilt account free of charge, then bids non-competitively in the weekly auctions. The minimum is ₹10,000 of face value, the non-competitive bid ceiling for dated central-government securities ₹2 crore per security per auction, and allotment comes at the weighted average price of the successful competitive bids. Holdings sit in a gilt account at the Reserve Bank, not a demat account.

The indirect door is a gilt fund, a category of mutual fund SEBI requires to keep at least 80 percent of assets in government securities. A manager picks the maturities and units redeem on any business day, but the investor pays an expense ratio and holds a floating net asset value, not a fixed redemption date.

Safe from default, exposed to rates

Two risks are routinely collapsed into one word, and separating them is most of the skill of owning a G-Sec. Credit risk is the chance the borrower does not pay, negligible for rupee obligations of a government that controls both taxation and the currency the debt is written in. Interest-rate risk is the chance your security loses value because yields elsewhere have risen. A G-Sec is immune to the first, exposed to the second.

Why price and yield sit on a seesaw

For a plain fixed-rate dated security, the price is the present value of its promised payments. The equation below values it on a coupon date with whole half-yearly periods remaining; between coupon dates, accrued interest and settlement conventions also matter.

P is the market price per ₹100 of face value and C is the annual rupee coupon fixed by the coupon rate, so C divided by 2 is each half-yearly payment. F is the face value repaid at the end, n is the number of half-yearly periods left, and y is the market yield to maturity as a decimal. Notice that y sits only in the denominators: raise it and every term shrinks, which is the arithmetic behind the seesaw.

Worked example
Farida Merchant, a textile exporter in Surat, bought ₹10,00,000 of face value in a 7.10 percent dated security at par through her Retail Direct account when it had ten years to run. Immediately after the fourth half-yearly coupon, with eight years remaining, market yields on comparable government paper have risen by one percentage point to 8.10 percent. Her statement now shows a lower value and she wants to know what she has actually lost.

Step 1, what has not changed. Her coupon. The security pays 7.10 percent of face value, so ₹71,000 a year on ₹10,00,000, arriving as ₹35,500 on two dates six months apart. Market yields do not touch that obligation, and it runs unchanged until the government repays her ₹10,00,000 in full at maturity.

Step 2, what the market now pays for that stream. With sixteen half-yearly periods left and a required yield of 8.10 percent a year, or 4.05 percent per half-year, the present value of ₹3.55 sixteen times plus ₹100 at the end comes to ₹94.1953 per ₹100 of face value. Multiplying by the 10,000 units she holds gives a market value of ₹9,41,953.

Step 3, sizing the fall. Against the ₹10,00,000 she paid, the drop is ₹58,047, or 5.80 percent. A buyer today will not accept 7.10 percent when fresh issues offer 8.10 percent, so the price falls until the discounted coupons plus the ₹100 redemption deliver that higher rate.

Step 4, what the loss is and is not. It is real if she sells this week, but she is not forced to sell. With an unchanged yield and promised payments, the discount narrows towards par as maturity approaches. Changing yields can interrupt that path, and at maturity she receives exactly ₹10,00,000 plus the final coupon, with total coupons of ₹5,68,000 over those eight years, including the final coupon. She has already received ₹1,42,000 over the first two years, so the price decline alone is not her total investment return.

So what? Farida's coupon column never moves while her value column swings by more than ₹1.7 lakh across the range. That is interest-rate risk in one picture, and it is why a sovereign security can be free of default risk and still lose money for someone forced to sell early. Matching maturity to a known cash need reduces the risk of having to sell at an unfavourable price. Inflation, coupon reinvestment and opportunity costs still matter.

Check yourself
Q
If G-Secs carry no credit risk, how can a gilt fund post a negative return?
A: The fund marks its holdings to market daily. If yields rise, the prices of the securities it owns fall, and that fall can exceed the coupon income collected. No borrower defaulted; the portfolio was simply repriced.

Key takeaways

  • G-Sec covers central and state government securities; conventional dated central-government bonds usually pay coupons twice a year.
  • Dated securities are auctioned weekly, usually on a Friday, under a half-yearly calendar.
  • An individual can buy through a free Retail Direct Gilt account, with a ₹10,000 minimum face value and a dated central-government non-competitive bid limit of ₹2 crore per security per auction, or via a gilt fund.
  • Rupee sovereign credit risk is effectively nil; interest-rate risk is substantial and grows with maturity.
  • Price and yield move in opposite directions. Holding to contractual redemption avoids an early-sale price, but does not remove inflation or reinvestment risk.

Frequently asked questions

How do I buy a G-Sec as an individual in India?

Register on RBI Retail Direct with a rupee savings account, PAN and KYC, open a Retail Direct Gilt account at no cost, and bid non-competitively in a weekly auction. Banks and primary dealers route retail bids too.

How does a G-Sec differ from a state development loan?

State Development Loans are themselves government securities, issued by state governments through RBI. Dated Government of India securities are issued by the central government. Both are available through Retail Direct, and their yields can differ.

Can I lose money on a government security?

RBI describes rupee G-Secs as carrying no domestic credit risk, but selling before maturity can realise a loss. Redemption returns face value, which may be less than a premium purchase price. Inflation and coupon reinvestment rates also affect the realised outcome.

Is there a lock-in on a Retail Direct holding?

No. Securities can be sold through the secondary market window on Retail Direct. Liquidity varies sharply, so recently issued benchmark tenors are easier to exit than older ones.

How is G-Sec income taxed?

Coupon income is generally taxable at the holder's applicable rate. Sale or redemption gains and gilt fund units can follow different rules depending on the instrument, acquisition date and investor. Verify current treatment with the Income Tax Department before comparing post-tax returns.

Why do G-Sec yields move when the RBI changes policy?

The policy rate anchors the short end of the curve while expectations about future policy and inflation shape the long end. Prices respond to changes in expectations, which may occur before or after an announcement; different maturities need not move together.
