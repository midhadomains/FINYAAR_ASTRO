Dividend

Dividend is the portion of a company's profit that it distributes to its shareholders, usually in cash, as a reward for owning its shares. In India it is declared by the board, approved by shareholders, and paid per share held on a fixed record date.

What is a Dividend?

A dividend is a cash payout that a profitable company shares with the people who own it. When you buy an equity share, you become a part-owner of the business, and a dividend is your slice of the profit that the board decides not to reinvest. It is one of the two ways shareholders make money from stocks, the other being a rise in the share price.

Think of a well-run Indian company like ITC, TCS or Coal India. After paying all costs, taxes and interest, suppose the company has ₹10,000 crore of net profit left. The board may decide to keep ₹6,000 crore inside the business for growth and hand back ₹4,000 crore to shareholders. That ₹4,000 crore, divided across all shares, is the dividend. If you hold 500 shares, you simply receive your proportionate amount straight into your bank account, with no need to sell anything.

Dividends are not guaranteed. A company can raise, cut, or skip them depending on profits, cash flow and future plans. Fast-growing firms often pay little or nothing, choosing instead to reinvest, while mature, cash-rich companies tend to be generous, steady payers.

In India, dividends are often announced as a percentage of face value (the nominal value of a share, commonly ₹1, ₹2, ₹5 or ₹10), not of market price. A "300% dividend" on a ₹2 face value means ₹6 per share (300% of ₹2), which can look confusing next to a ₹500 market price. Two other ratios matter to investors:

The dividend yield tells you the cash return relative to the current price, letting you compare a stock against a fixed deposit. The payout ratio uses EPS (earnings per share) to show what fraction of profit is returned versus retained for growth.

Timing is driven by the record date: only investors on the register that day receive the dividend. Because Indian settlement is now T+1, the ex-dividend date is typically the same day as the record date, and the share usually falls by roughly the dividend amount when it goes ex-dividend.

Example

Worked example

Priya, a Pune-based investor, owns 500 shares of a fictional company, Ganga Cements Ltd. The share has a face value of ₹2 and currently trades at ₹400. For the year, Ganga Cements reports EPS of ₹25 and the board declares a final dividend of 600%. Work out Priya's dividend income, the dividend yield, the payout ratio, and what it all means for her.

Step 1 - Convert the percentage into rupees. A 600% dividend is 600% of the face value of ₹2. So DPS = 6.00 × ₹2 = ₹12 per share. This is the number that actually matters, not the scary-looking 600%.

Step 2 - Priya's total dividend. She owns 500 shares, so her cash dividend = 500 × ₹12 = ₹6,000, credited directly to her bank account. She keeps all 500 shares.

Step 3 - Dividend yield. Using the market price of ₹400: yield = (₹12 ÷ ₹400) × 100 = 3.0%. So on the money she has invested at today's price, the cash return is 3% per year.

Step 4 - Payout ratio. With EPS of ₹25: payout = (₹12 ÷ ₹25) × 100 = 48%. Ganga Cements returns 48% of its profit and retains 52% (₹13 per share) to reinvest in the business.

Step 5 - Tax. Since FY 2020-21, dividends are taxable in the investor's hands at her income-tax slab. Because her ₹6,000 is above ₹5,000 from a single company in a year, the company deducts TDS at 10% (₹600), crediting ₹5,400; the ₹600 is adjusted when she files her return.

So what? Priya's 3% cash yield is lower than a bank fixed deposit paying around 7%, so dividends alone are not why she owns the stock. The real prize is the 52% of profit the company keeps and reinvests, which can grow future earnings and the share price. A healthy 48% payout signals a company that rewards owners while still funding growth. If Priya reinvests every dividend into more shares, she also harnesses compound interest-style growth over the years.

Why dividends matter to Indian investors

Dividends give a stock two engines of return: regular cash plus potential capital appreciation, a useful blend when you think about risk versus return. For retirees and conservative savers, a portfolio of steady dividend payers can act like a rising income stream that, unlike a fixed deposit, can grow over time and help offset inflation, which the RBI targets at 4% CPI within a 2-6% band.

Consistent dividends are also a quality signal. A company that has paid and raised dividends for a decade is telling you its profits are real cash, not just accounting EBITDA on paper. That is one reason many index heavyweights on the Sensex and Nifty are large, cash-generating dividend payers. Just remember a very high yield can be a red flag, sometimes it reflects a falling price rather than a generous board.

Check yourself
A company with a ₹5 face value declares a 200% dividend. You hold 300 shares at a market price of ₹250. What is your gross dividend and the dividend yield?
DPS = 200% of ₹5 = ₹10 per share. Gross dividend = 300 × ₹10 = ₹3,000. Dividend yield = (₹10 ÷ ₹250) × 100 = 4.0%.

Key takeaways

  • A dividend is a share of company profit paid to shareholders, usually in cash, per share held on the record date.
  • In India it is often quoted as a percentage of face value, so always convert it into rupees per share.
  • DPS = total dividend ÷ shares; yield compares it to price, and payout ratio compares it to EPS.
  • Dividends are taxed at your slab rate, with 10% TDS above ₹5,000 from one company in a year.
  • They are optional and can be cut; steady, growing dividends signal a healthy, cash-rich business.

Frequently asked questions

Are dividends taxable in India?

Yes. Since FY 2020-21, the earlier Dividend Distribution Tax was abolished and dividends are now taxed in the investor's hands at their income-tax slab rate. If your dividend from a single company exceeds ₹5,000 in a financial year, the company deducts TDS at 10% before paying you.

Do I need to sell shares to get a dividend?

No. A dividend is paid on shares you hold on the record date and lands directly in your linked bank account. You keep all your shares and can receive dividends year after year without selling a single one.

Why do some companies pay no dividend at all?

Fast-growing firms often reinvest every rupee of profit to expand faster, judging that this will lift the share price more than a cash payout would. Investors in such companies expect their return from capital appreciation rather than dividends.

What is the difference between dividend and dividend yield?

The dividend is the rupee amount paid per share, while the dividend yield expresses that amount as a percentage of the current market price. Yield lets you compare income across stocks and against products like fixed deposits.

Does the share price fall after a dividend?

Typically yes. On the ex-dividend date the price usually drops by roughly the dividend amount, because that cash has left the company. This is normal and does not mean you have lost money, since you receive the dividend separately.

Is a very high dividend yield always good?

Not necessarily. An unusually high yield can occur because the share price has crashed, not because the payout is generous. Always check whether the dividend is sustainable from real profits and cash flow, and look at the payout ratio and the company's track record.
