FINYAAR DICTIONARY · STOCK MARKET AND EQUITIES

Face Value

Face Value is the nominal value fixed on a share or a bond at issue and recorded in the company's books. Indian equity shares commonly carry a face value of ₹10, ₹5, ₹2 or ₹1, and that figure is the building block of paid-up share capital. It is not the market price, which the NSE and the BSE reset every second, and it is not book value, which comes off the balance sheet. Face value governs the arithmetic of a split, the reading of a dividend percentage and the sum a bond repays at maturity, yet it says nothing about worth.

Three numbers attached to the same share

All of these are quoted in rupees per share and only one is set by the market, which is where confusion starts.

A ₹1 face value share is not cheaper than a ₹100 one. Two companies of identical worth can carry different face values because one has split its shares and the other has not.

The arithmetic it actually drives

Face value exists because share capital must be denominated in something countable.

Face value per share is the nominal figure, the number of shares is the count issued and paid for, and the product is the paid-up share capital shown under equity. Anything paid above face value at issue does not enter that line; it goes to the securities premium account.

A company raising ₹500 crore by selling ₹10 face value shares at ₹250 adds only ₹20 crore to share capital, the rest being premium. Paid-up capital is therefore a poor measure of size: market capitalisation uses the market price.

A split moves it, a bonus issue leaves it alone

Both actions raise the number of shares you hold and neither makes you richer on the day, but they touch different parts of the balance sheet. In a split the face value is divided: a ₹10 share split into five becomes five shares of ₹2, and paid-up capital does not move because nothing new was created.

A bonus issue does the reverse. Face value stays where it was, new shares go free to existing holders, and share capital rises to match the larger count, funded by capitalising free reserves. Total equity is unchanged; only its composition shifts. Both actions cut the market price roughly in proportion, which is usually the point, since a lower quoted price improves liquidity.

Percentages on shares, par on bonds

Indian companies still announce dividends as a percentage, and it is always a percentage of face value, never of market price. A 250 per cent dividend on a ₹2 face value is ₹5 per share, thrilling until you notice the share trades at ₹170 and the yield is 2.9 per cent. Read the rupees-per-share figure in the dividend announcement.

Bonds follow the same logic more strictly. A debenture or government security is issued with a face value such as ₹100 or ₹1,000, the coupon rate applies to that face value rather than to the traded price, and unless the terms say otherwise the issuer repays the face value at maturity. That is redemption at par: a bond bought at ₹96 repays ₹100 and so does one bought at ₹104, which is why price and yield move in opposite directions.

Worked example
Meera Nadkarni, a pharmacist in Nashik, owns 400 shares of a listed company, illustrative only and not named. The face value is ₹10 and the market price is ₹850. The company has paid-up share capital of ₹20,00,00,000. It now splits each share into five and later declares a dividend of 250 per cent. What actually changes for her?

Step 1, the company before the split. Running the identity in reverse, the share count is ₹20,00,00,000 divided by ₹10, or 2,00,00,000 shares. Meera holds 400 of them, worth 400 × ₹850, that is ₹3,40,000, and her slice of share capital is ₹4,000.

Step 2, the split. Each ₹10 share becomes five shares of ₹2. The company now has 10,00,00,000 shares, and 10,00,00,000 × ₹2 is still ₹20,00,00,000, so paid-up capital has not moved by a rupee. Meera holds 2,000 shares. The market price adjusts to roughly ₹170, so her holding is worth ₹3,40,000 exactly as before.

Step 3, the dividend. A 250 per cent dividend means 250 per cent of face value: 2.5 × ₹2, or ₹5 per share, so on 2,000 shares she receives ₹10,000. Without the split the same declaration would have paid 2.5 × ₹10, that is ₹25 on each of 400 shares, and again ₹10,000.

Step 4, the bonus alternative. Had the board declared a one-for-one bonus instead, face value would have stayed at ₹10, the count would have doubled to 4,00,00,000, and paid-up capital would have risen to ₹40,00,00,000, funded by moving ₹20,00,00,000 out of free reserves. Meera would hold 800 shares at about ₹425, worth the same ₹3,40,000.

Step 5, the number that never moved. Her ownership stays at 0.002 per cent of the company in every version. Only the figures denominated in face value changed.

So what? Her ownership share, the value of her holding and her dividend cheque are identical through all of this. Face value is a unit of measurement, and changing the unit changes the reading, not the thing being measured. The 250 per cent headline is meaningless without knowing the face value, and even then it works out to 2.9 per cent of what the share actually costs.

Check yourself
Q
Company A declares a 400 per cent dividend on a ₹1 face value and Company B a 60 per cent dividend on a ₹10 face value. Which pays more per share?
A: Company B. Four hundred per cent of ₹1 is ₹4, while 60 per cent of ₹10 is ₹6. Neither figure means much until it is set against the market price.

Key takeaways

  • Face value is the nominal value fixed at issue, commonly ₹10, ₹5, ₹2 or ₹1 here.
  • Paid-up share capital equals face value multiplied by the number of shares issued.
  • A split cuts face value and leaves share capital unchanged; a bonus keeps face value and raises share capital from reserves.
  • Dividend percentages are always of face value, so convert them to rupees per share first.
  • Bonds pay a coupon on face value and normally redeem at par, whatever they traded at meanwhile.

Frequently asked questions

Is a low face value a sign of a cheap share?

No. Face value is an accounting convention carrying no information about worth: a ₹1 face value share can trade at ₹4,000 and a ₹10 one at ₹12.

Can a company change the face value of its shares?

Yes, by sub-dividing shares in a split or combining them in a consolidation, each requiring members to approve an alteration of the capital clause. Both are announced with a record date.

What is the difference between face value and book value?

Face value is fixed at issue and moves only in a split or consolidation. Book value is net assets divided by shares outstanding and changes at every reporting date.

Do shares always have a face value?

Under Indian company law shares carry a nominal value, so every listed Indian share has one. Some jurisdictions elsewhere permit shares of no par value.

Why is my dividend smaller than the percentage suggests?

Because the percentage applies to face value, not to what you paid. A 300 per cent dividend on a ₹2 face value is ₹6 per share, a one per cent yield at ₹600.

Does a bond always repay its face value?

Standard bonds redeem at par, meaning at face value, unless the terms provide for a redemption premium. That assumes the issuer can pay, since credit risk sits outside this arithmetic.
