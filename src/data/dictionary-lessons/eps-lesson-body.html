EPS (Earnings Per Share)

EPS (Earnings Per Share) is the portion of a company's net profit that is attributable to each outstanding equity share, calculated as net profit minus preference dividends divided by the weighted average number of equity shares. It is one of the most widely tracked profitability numbers in Indian equity markets and forms the denominator-partner of the P/E ratio.

What is EPS (Earnings Per Share)?

EPS (Earnings Per Share) tells you how much profit a company earned for every single share you own. If a company makes a net profit of ₹500 crore and has 24 crore shares, then each share "earned" roughly ₹20 during the year. That ₹20 is the EPS.

Think of it like a family business run by four cousins who split ownership equally. If the shop earns ₹4 lakh in a year, each cousin's "share of earnings" is ₹1 lakh. A listed company simply does the same maths across crores of equity shares. Higher EPS generally signals stronger per-share profitability, which is why it appears at the bottom of every Indian company's profit-and-loss statement, right below net profit, as mandated by Ind AS 33.

EPS matters because most valuation shortcuts start here. When a TV anchor says "the stock trades at 20 times earnings," that 20 is the share price divided by EPS. So before you can judge whether a stock is cheap or expensive, you first need a reliable EPS number.

How EPS is calculated

The basic formula subtracts any dividend owed to preference shareholders first, because that profit does not belong to ordinary equity holders. The remaining profit is then spread over the average shares outstanding during the year.

Reading the variables in plain words:

Net Profit (PAT) is profit after tax for the year, the final line of the P&L. Preference Dividend is the fixed dividend promised to preference shareholders, removed because it is not the ordinary shareholder's money. Weighted Average Equity Shares is the average number of shares outstanding through the year, weighted by how many months each block existed, so a fresh IPO or buyback mid-year is counted only for the period it was live.

Companies also report Diluted EPS, which assumes all potential shares (from ESOPs, convertible bonds or warrants) are converted. This gives a more conservative, "worst-case" per-share figure.

Types of EPS you will see

Not every EPS number means the same thing. Indian annual reports, brokerage notes and screeners often quote different versions, so knowing which one you are looking at is essential.

As a rule, diluted EPS is always equal to or lower than basic EPS. A large gap between the two is a warning that heavy future dilution could shrink your per-share stake.

Worked example: Bharat Manufacturing Ltd

Worked example
Bharat Manufacturing Ltd, a mid-cap listed on the NSE, reports net profit after tax of ₹500 crore for FY2025-26. It owes ₹20 crore as preference dividend. The weighted average number of equity shares outstanding is 24 crore. The company has also granted employee stock options that could add 2 crore new shares on conversion. The share currently trades at ₹400. Calculate Basic EPS, Diluted EPS and the resulting P/E ratio.

Step 1 — Profit available to equity holders. Remove the preference dividend, which does not belong to ordinary shareholders: ₹500 crore − ₹20 crore = ₹480 crore.

Step 2 — Basic EPS. Divide that profit by the weighted average equity shares: ₹480 crore ÷ 24 crore shares = ₹20.00 per share.

Step 3 — Diluted EPS. Assume the ESOPs convert, taking the share count to 24 + 2 = 26 crore: ₹480 crore ÷ 26 crore = ₹18.46 per share. The dilution knocks the EPS down by about ₹1.54, or roughly 7.7%.

Step 4 — P/E ratio. Using the basic figure, the P/E ratio is price ÷ EPS = ₹400 ÷ ₹20 = 20 times. On diluted EPS it stretches to ₹400 ÷ ₹18.46 = 21.7 times.

So what? Bharat Manufacturing earns ₹20 for every share you hold, but if the ESOPs vest, your real slice is closer to ₹18.46. A P/E of 20 sits roughly in line with the broad Nifty average, so the stock is neither obviously cheap nor a bargain. To decide, you would compare this EPS trend over five years, check whether profit growth is genuine and not a one-off, and weigh it against peers. EPS alone never tells the full story, but it is the honest starting point.

Why EPS matters and its limits

EPS feeds directly into the P/E ratio, into dividend yield comparisons and into how analysts set price targets. A rising EPS trend over several years is often a sign of durable earnings power, which is why long-term investors track it closely.

But treat it with care. EPS can be inflated by share buybacks (fewer shares, higher EPS) even when actual profit is flat. It can be distorted by one-time gains such as selling a factory. And because it uses net profit, it is affected by depreciation and tax choices, unlike EBITDA which strips those out. Two firms with the same EPS can have very different market capitalization depending on share count, so never compare EPS across companies in absolute rupee terms alone.

Check yourself
A company has net profit of ₹300 crore, no preference dividend, and 15 crore weighted average shares. What is its basic EPS, and if the share trades at ₹300, what is the P/E?
Basic EPS = ₹300 crore ÷ 15 crore = ₹20 per share. P/E = ₹300 ÷ ₹20 = 15 times.

Key takeaways

  • EPS is net profit (after preference dividends) divided by the weighted average equity shares.
  • Diluted EPS assumes all ESOPs and convertibles convert, and is always equal to or lower than basic EPS.
  • EPS is the denominator of the P/E ratio, the most common valuation shortcut in Indian markets.
  • Buybacks and one-time gains can flatter EPS, so check the trend and quality of earnings.
  • Never compare EPS in rupee terms across companies; always convert to a ratio like P/E.

Frequently asked questions

Is a higher EPS always better?

Generally, a higher EPS signals stronger per-share profitability, but not always. EPS can be pushed up by share buybacks or one-off gains rather than real business growth. Always look at the multi-year EPS trend and check whether profit growth is sustainable before drawing conclusions.

What is the difference between basic and diluted EPS?

Basic EPS uses only the shares currently outstanding. Diluted EPS assumes all potential shares from ESOPs, warrants and convertible bonds are issued, spreading profit over a larger share count. Diluted EPS is the more conservative figure and is always equal to or lower than basic EPS.

How is EPS related to the P/E ratio?

The P/E ratio is simply the share price divided by EPS. So EPS is the earnings half of that valuation multiple. If a stock trades at ₹400 with an EPS of ₹20, its P/E is 20 times, meaning you pay ₹20 for every ₹1 of annual earnings.

Where can I find a company's EPS in India?

Every listed Indian company must report basic and diluted EPS at the foot of its profit-and-loss statement under Ind AS 33. You will also find it on NSE and BSE company pages, in quarterly results filed with SEBI, and on stock screeners, usually as a trailing twelve-month (TTM) figure.

Can EPS be negative?

Yes. If a company makes a net loss, its EPS is negative and often shown as loss per share. Many young, fast-growing firms report negative EPS for years while they invest for scale, so a negative number is not automatically a red flag, but it does mean the P/E ratio becomes meaningless.

Does EPS account for dividends paid to shareholders?

EPS measures profit earned per share, not profit distributed. It only subtracts dividends owed to preference shareholders. What ordinary shareholders actually receive as dividend is a separate decision, captured by the payout ratio and dividend yield, not by EPS itself.
