FINYAAR DICTIONARY · TAXATION (INDIA)

Old vs New Tax Regime

Old vs New Tax Regime is the choice every Indian income-tax payer makes between two parallel systems of slabs under the Income Tax Act: the older regime that keeps higher slab rates but lets you claim a long list of deductions and exemptions, and the newer regime that offers lower slab rates but strips away most of those deductions. The new regime is the default option, so if you do nothing, your tax is computed under it. The old regime rewards people who save and spend in tax-favoured ways, while the new regime rewards simplicity and suits those with few deductions to claim.

Why two regimes exist

For decades India ran a single tax structure built on the idea of nudging citizens to save, insure themselves and buy homes. To make that happen, the law allowed you to subtract a range of amounts from your income before tax was charged — life insurance premiums, provident-fund contributions, tuition fees, home-loan interest, medical insurance and more. Over time this grew into a thicket of sections that rewarded the disciplined and the well-advised, but also made returns complicated and favoured those who could afford to lock money away.

The new regime was introduced to offer a cleaner alternative: pay a lower rate across most slabs, but give up the deductions. The government kept it optional at first and later made it the default. Today, one taxpayer can legitimately face two very different tax bills on the same income, depending purely on which regime they pick and how many deductions they actually use.

What each regime keeps and drops

The heart of the difference is deductions. The old regime lets you claim Section 80C (up to ₹1.5 lakh for investments such as PPF, EPF, life insurance and ELSS), Section 80D for health insurance premiums, House Rent Allowance (HRA) if you pay rent, and home-loan interest on a self-occupied property under Section 24(b). The new regime removes almost all of these. What both regimes now share is a standard deduction for salaried individuals, which is subtracted automatically without any proof.

The new regime also carries a stronger rebate under Section 87A, designed so that no tax is payable up to a specified income level. That threshold has been raised in recent Union Budgets, which is a large part of why the new regime has become attractive for middle-income earners with modest deductions. The exact slabs, the standard-deduction amount and the rebate ceiling are revised in each Budget, so always check the figures for the relevant financial year rather than assuming last year’s numbers still hold.

How the comparison is calculated

Comparing regimes is not about which has lower rates on paper; it is about which produces the lower final tax once your deductions are applied. Under the old regime your taxable income shrinks by every eligible deduction before the slabs are applied. Under the new regime your taxable income is much closer to your gross income because most deductions vanish.

The rule of thumb is a break-even idea: the old regime only wins if your deductions are large enough to more than offset its higher rates. If you fully use 80C, pay meaningful health-insurance premiums, claim HRA and service a home loan, the old regime often stays ahead. If your deductions are small, the new regime’s lower rates and bigger rebate usually leave you paying less.

Choosing and switching

Because the new regime is the default, salaried employees who want the old regime must actively opt for it, usually by declaring it to their employer at the start of the year and confirming it when filing. Salaried taxpayers can generally switch between regimes year to year, so a decision that suits you today is not permanent. Taxpayers with business or professional income face tighter rules on switching, so they should plan more carefully before opting.

The practical approach is to run the numbers both ways every year using the current slabs and rebate limit, because your own life changes — a new home loan, a child’s tuition, a jump in insurance — can flip the answer from one regime to the other.

Worked example
Priya Nair, a salaried marketing manager in Kochi, earns a gross salary of ₹12,00,000 in a financial year. She wants to know which regime leaves her with a smaller tax bill. She invests ₹1,50,000 in PPF and ELSS (fully using Section 80C), pays ₹25,000 in health-insurance premiums under Section 80D, and services a home loan on which she pays ₹2,00,000 of interest on her self-occupied flat. Both regimes give her a standard deduction, assumed here at ₹50,000 for illustration.

Step 1 — Compute taxable income under the old regime. Priya subtracts every eligible deduction from her gross salary: the standard deduction, 80C, 80D and home-loan interest under Section 24(b).

Step 2 — Compute taxable income under the new regime. Here only the standard deduction applies; 80C, 80D and home-loan interest are all disallowed.

Step 3 — Apply the slabs. The old regime charges higher rates, but Priya is taxed on only ₹7,75,000. The new regime charges lower rates, but on ₹11,50,000 — nearly ₹3.75 lakh more income exposed to tax. With deductions this large, the smaller taxable base under the old regime typically outweighs its steeper rates, so Priya is likely to pay less under the old regime. She should confirm this by plugging both taxable incomes into the current year’s slabs and the Section 87A rebate limit.

So what? Priya’s deductions total ₹3.75 lakh over and above the standard deduction, and every rupee of that is lost if she chooses the new regime. For a taxpayer who genuinely uses the full 80C limit, has a home loan and pays health-insurance premiums, the old regime frequently remains the cheaper choice. Her colleague who rents no home, has no loan and invests nothing in 80C would see the opposite result, because for that person the two taxable incomes would be almost identical and the new regime’s lower rates and larger rebate would win. The lesson is that the right regime is personal: it depends entirely on how many deductions you actually claim, so run both calculations each year before deciding.

Check yourself
A young professional in Pune rents a flat, has no home loan, and invests nothing under Section 80C. Which regime is she more likely to benefit from, and why?
A: The new regime. With no 80C investment and no home-loan interest, she has almost no deductions to lose, so her taxable income is nearly the same under both. The new regime’s lower slab rates and larger Section 87A rebate then leave her paying less. She would still verify with the current year’s figures.

Key takeaways

  • The new regime is the default; you must actively opt for the old regime if you want it.
  • The old regime has higher rates but allows 80C, 80D, HRA and home-loan interest; the new regime has lower rates but drops most deductions.
  • Both regimes now offer a standard deduction for salaried individuals, and the new regime carries a larger Section 87A rebate that recent Budgets have expanded.
  • The old regime tends to win for people with large deductions; the new regime tends to win for people with few.
  • Slabs, the standard deduction and the rebate limit change with each Union Budget, so recalculate both regimes every year with current figures.

Frequently asked questions

If I do nothing when filing, which regime applies to me?

The new regime applies automatically, because it is the default. To be taxed under the old regime you must actively choose it — typically by informing your employer at the start of the year and confirming the choice when you file your return.

Can I claim my Section 80C investments under the new regime?

No. Section 80C is one of the deductions the new regime removes, so your PPF, EPF, ELSS or life-insurance contributions do not reduce your taxable income there. They remain useful savings, but the tax benefit for the amount invested is only available under the old regime.

I have a home loan on my self-occupied flat. Does that change my choice?

It often does. Home-loan interest on a self-occupied property is deductible under Section 24(b) only in the old regime. A large annual interest amount can substantially shrink your old-regime taxable income and tip the comparison in the old regime’s favour, so run both calculations before deciding.

Can salaried people switch between the two regimes every year?

Generally yes. Salaried taxpayers can usually reassess each financial year and pick whichever regime suits their current situation. Taxpayers with business or professional income face stricter rules on switching, so they should plan carefully before opting out of the new regime.

Does the standard deduction exist in both regimes?

Yes. A standard deduction for salaried individuals is available under both the old and the new regime and is applied without any proof or investment. It is one of the few benefits the new regime retains, but its exact amount is set in the Union Budget and should be checked for the relevant year.

Why do people say no tax is payable up to a certain income under the new regime?

Because the new regime offers a fuller rebate under Section 87A, structured so that a resident individual whose income stays below a specified threshold ends up with zero tax. That threshold has been raised in recent Budgets, which is a major reason the new regime appeals to middle-income earners. The exact limit changes each year, so confirm the current figure.
