Budgeting

Budgeting is the process of planning how you will spend and save your income before the money actually arrives, so that every rupee has a purpose. To learn how to make a budget, you list your monthly income, group your expenses into needs, wants, and savings, and then set limits for each group so that spending never exceeds what you earn. A good budget turns vague money worries into a simple, repeatable plan you can follow every month.

What budgeting really is

A budget is simply a plan for your money over a fixed period, usually one month. It answers three questions: how much comes in, where it goes, and how much is left to save. Budgeting is not about restriction for its own sake — it is about giving yourself permission to spend on what matters while protecting your future. When you budget, you decide in advance rather than wondering at month end where your salary disappeared. The habit also makes it easier to build an emergency fund, clear high-cost debt, and grow your net worth steadily over time. Budgeting is the foundation skill of personal finance, and every other money decision becomes easier once it is in place.

The 50/30/20 rule

The most popular starting framework is the 50/30/20 rule. You divide your take-home pay into three buckets: 50% for needs such as rent, groceries, utilities, transport, and loan EMIs; 30% for wants such as dining out, subscriptions, travel, and shopping; and 20% for savings and investments such as a SIP, provident fund, or repaying extra on debt. The rule is popular because it is easy to remember and flexible. The split can be written as a simple formula of your monthly take-home income.

In high-cost Indian cities where rent alone can eat into the needs bucket, many people adapt the numbers to 60/20/20 or 50/20/30. The exact ratio matters less than the discipline of assigning every rupee a job.

How to build and stick to one

To build a budget, first total your monthly take-home income. Next, list fixed expenses (rent, EMIs, insurance, school fees) and variable expenses (food, fuel, entertainment). Then apply a framework such as 50/30/20, or use zero-based budgeting, where you assign every rupee of income to a category until income minus all allocations equals zero — nothing is left unplanned. Finally, automate savings on salary day so they leave before you can spend them.

Sticking to a budget depends on tracking. Review your spending weekly using a notebook, a spreadsheet, or a UPI statement. If you overshoot the wants bucket, trim it the following week rather than abandoning the plan. Avoid funding lifestyle on your credit card, which quietly converts wants into expensive debt.

Worked example
Ananya, a marketing executive in Pune, brings home ₹60,000 per month after tax and deductions. She wants to apply the 50/30/20 rule to bring order to her spending, which currently feels random. How should she split her salary, and what should she do with the savings portion?

Step 1 — Find the take-home figure. Ananya budgets on her in-hand salary of ₹60,000, not her gross package, because taxes and provident fund are already removed. This is the number the 50/30/20 rule divides.

Step 2 — Apply the three buckets. Needs are 50% of ₹60,000, which is ₹30,000. Wants are 30%, which is ₹18,000. Savings and investments are 20%, which is ₹12,000. She writes these targets down before the month begins.

Step 3 — Fill in real categories. Ananya lists her actual expenses against each bucket to check whether the targets fit her Pune lifestyle.

BucketCategoryAmount (₹)
Needs (50%)Rent, groceries, transport, utilities30,000
Wants (30%)Dining, OTT, shopping, weekend trips18,000
Savings (20%)SIP, recurring deposit, emergency fund12,000
Total60,000

Step 4 — Put the savings to work. Ananya does not leave the ₹12,000 idle. She directs ₹5,000 into a monthly SIP, ₹4,000 into building her emergency fund until it reaches six months of expenses, and ₹3,000 towards a short-term goal. She automates all three transfers on the first of the month, right after salary credit.

So what? Ananya has turned a ₹60,000 salary into a clear plan: ₹30,000 keeps her life running, ₹18,000 lets her enjoy the month without guilt, and ₹12,000 quietly builds her future. Because the savings leave first, she never has to rely on willpower at month end. If her rent rises next year, she simply revisits the buckets and trims wants rather than borrowing.

Key takeaways

  • A budget is a plan for your money made before the month begins, not a record made after.
  • The 50/30/20 rule splits take-home pay into 50% needs, 30% wants, and 20% savings.
  • Zero-based budgeting assigns every rupee a job until income minus allocations equals zero.
  • Automating savings on salary day removes the need for month-end willpower.
  • Weekly tracking is what keeps a budget alive; without it, plans quietly drift.

Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest budgeting rule for a beginner?

The 50/30/20 rule is the easiest starting point because it uses only three buckets: 50% needs, 30% wants, and 20% savings. It is simple to remember and flexible enough to adjust as your income or city changes.

Should I budget on my gross or take-home salary?

Always budget on your take-home (in-hand) salary, because taxes and provident fund contributions are already deducted. Budgeting on gross pay overstates what you can actually spend.

What is zero-based budgeting?

Zero-based budgeting means assigning every rupee of income to a specific category — needs, wants, savings, or debt — until income minus all allocations equals zero. Nothing is left unplanned, which gives very tight control over spending.

How do I stop overspending on wants?

Track spending weekly and cap the wants bucket. If you overshoot one week, trim the next week rather than abandoning the plan. Avoid funding wants on a credit card, which turns them into costly debt.

Where should the 20% savings go?

Split it between building an emergency fund, long-term investments such as a SIP, and any short-term goals. Automating these transfers on salary day makes the habit effortless.

How often should I review my budget?

Review spending weekly to catch small leaks, and revisit the full budget monthly. Rebuild the buckets whenever a major change occurs, such as a salary hike, a rent increase, or a new EMI.
