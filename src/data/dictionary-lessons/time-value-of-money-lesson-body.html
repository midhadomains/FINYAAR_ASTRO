Time Value of Money

Time Value of Money is the principle that a rupee available now is worth more than the same rupee promised later, because the rupee in hand can be put to work immediately. Turning a future amount into what it is worth now is discounting, and the answer is its present value; pushing a present amount forward is compounding, and the answer is its future value. The rate used to move money across time is the discount rate, and it reflects what the money could otherwise earn plus the risk the promise is not honoured. Almost every calculation in Indian personal finance sits on this idea, from an EMI schedule to bond pricing.

Why a rupee now outranks a rupee later

Money has a rental value. Handed ₹1,00,000 today, you can place it in a fixed deposit, repay costly debt or fund a business order, and by next year it is larger. The same ₹1,00,000 promised twelve months out buys none of those opportunities meanwhile.

Three forces push in the same direction. The first is that lost earning opportunity. The second is inflation, which erodes what a rupee purchases between now and then. The third is uncertainty: a promise for five years ahead may not be kept, and whoever waits deserves compensation for carrying that risk. Comparing sums that arrive on different dates without adjusting for all three is no comparison at all.

The two calculations that do all the work

Moving an amount backwards in time gives its present value.

Moving it forwards gives the future value, the same relationship rearranged.

PV is the value today, FV is the amount on the future date, r is the rate per period as a decimal so 9 per cent becomes 0.09, and n is the number of periods between the dates. The bracket raised to the power n is where compound interest lives: each period earns on the previous closing balance, not on the original sum. Divide by it and you discount, multiply by it and you compound, and a correctly built comparison gives the same verdict either way.

Choosing the rate, and what it does to the answer

The discount rate is not handed to you on a plate. It is the return you could genuinely earn on money of comparable risk over the same period: for a household often a safe deposit rate, for a business its cost of capital. Choose a higher rate and distant money shrinks harder.

Where it shows up in Indian money life

Open any loan statement and the machinery is already running. An EMI is the fixed monthly sum whose present value, discounted at the loan rate, equals the amount borrowed, which is why the interest and principal split shifts every month while the instalment stays put. A bond is priced the same way: discount each coupon and the maturity value at the yield the market demands and add them up. When yields rise, every discount factor falls and the price drops, with no change to the bond itself.

The same logic runs through a monthly investment plan, where each instalment compounds for a different number of months, so the earliest do the heaviest lifting. It also cuts through sales pitches: a guaranteed ₹50,00,000 payable after twenty years becomes comparable to a lump sum only once discounted back to today.

Worked example
Arvind Menon, a civil engineer in Kochi, is selling a plot of land. One buyer offers ₹18,00,000 in cash immediately. Another offers ₹26,00,000, but payable in a single instalment five years from now. Arvind can earn about 9 per cent a year on money of comparable safety. Which offer is genuinely larger?

Step 1, what the choice actually is. The headline gap of ₹8,00,000 is meaningless as it stands, because the two amounts arrive on different dates and a rupee in 2031 is not the same unit as a rupee today. They have to be restated at one common point in time before either can be called larger.

Step 2, fix the discount rate. Arvind uses 9 per cent, since that is what the money would realistically earn if he took the cash today and deployed it. The five-year factor is 1.09 raised to the power 5, which works out to 1.5386. Every year of waiting multiplies the gap between the two offers.

Step 3, discount the later offer. Present value is ₹26,00,000 ÷ 1.5386, which comes to about ₹16,89,800. The equivalent discount factor is 1 ÷ 1.5386, or 0.6499, so every rupee promised in year five is worth roughly 65 paise now. The larger offer has quietly become the smaller one.

Step 4, check it from the other end. Compound the cash offer forward instead: ₹18,00,000 × 1.5386 is about ₹27,69,500 in five years, comfortably above ₹26,00,000. Both methods agree, as they must.

Step 5, find the tipping point. The two offers are equal when 26 ÷ 18 raised to the power one-fifth equals one plus the rate, which gives about 7.6 per cent. Below that rate, waiting wins; above it, cash today wins. And this ignores the risk that a buyer promising money in 2031 simply does not pay.

So what? A bigger number arriving later is not automatically the better deal, and the discount rate decides the verdict rather than the size of the headline. Ask what the money would have earned in the meantime, run the division, and only then compare. The same test settles a lump sum against a deferred settlement, a maturity promise against a deposit, and an upfront discount against an instalment scheme.

Check yourself
Q
Why does a bond's market price fall when yields in the market rise, even though the coupon has not changed?
A: The price is the present value of the same fixed cash flows. A higher yield means a higher discount rate, so every coupon and the maturity amount are worth less today.

Key takeaways

  • A rupee now beats a rupee later because of lost earnings, inflation and the risk the promise fails.
  • Present value divides a future amount by one plus the rate raised to n; future value multiplies by it.
  • The discount rate is the return available on money of similar risk, and a higher rate shrinks distant amounts.
  • At 12 per cent, a rupee arriving in ten years is worth roughly 32 paise today.
  • EMI schedules, bond prices and investment plans all apply the same discounting arithmetic.

Frequently asked questions

Is time value of money the same as inflation?

No. Inflation is one reason future money is worth less, but the larger reason is the return you forgo by waiting. Even with zero inflation, money in hand could be earning.

What discount rate should an individual use?

Use the return you could realistically get on money of comparable risk over the same period, after tax. If the alternative is repaying a loan, the loan rate is a fair choice.

How is this different from compound interest?

They are two views of one mechanism. Compound interest describes money growing forward, while discounting runs the same growth backwards to value a future sum today.

Does it apply to monthly cash flows too?

Yes, and Indian loans work exactly this way. Convert the annual rate to a monthly rate and count n in months, which is how EMI schedules are built in practice.

Why do insurance and pension illustrations look so large?

Because they quote undiscounted future amounts spread over decades. Discount that maturity value back at a sensible rate and it usually shrinks a great deal, which is the fair way to compare options.

Where does risk enter the calculation?

Through the discount rate. A government security is discounted at a lower rate than payments promised by an unknown counterparty, and that higher rate is what shrinks their present value.
