Credit Card

Credit Card is a revolving line of unsecured credit issued by a bank that lets you spend up to a preset limit and repay later. If you clear the full statement balance by the due date, you pay no interest and enjoy a short interest-free period. If you carry an unpaid balance, the card charges very high interest, commonly about 3% to 3.75% per month, which works out to roughly 36% to 45% a year. It is a powerful payment and short-term convenience tool that becomes an expensive debt trap the moment you stop paying in full.

What a credit card is

A credit card is a plastic or virtual card linked to a revolving credit account. Unlike a debit card, which draws from your own bank balance, a credit card lets the bank pay the merchant on your behalf and bills you afterward. Each card carries a credit limit — the maximum outstanding amount the bank permits — set from your income, existing debt and repayment record. Because the borrowing is unsecured, no asset backs it, so banks price the risk with steep interest and rely heavily on your credit score (CIBIL) when approving you and setting the limit. Used with discipline, a card is a free 30-to-50 day float, a fraud-safe way to transact, and a record-builder. Used carelessly, it is one of the costliest forms of borrowing an Indian household can take on, dearer even than most a home loan or personal loan.

The billing cycle and interest-free period

A credit card works on a monthly billing cycle. Every purchase made during the cycle is totalled on a statement date, and you then get roughly 15 to 20 days before the payment due date. Spend early in the cycle and you can enjoy up to about 45 to 50 days before payment is due; spend late and the free window shrinks. This grace is the great advantage of a card — but it exists only if you pay the full statement balance by the due date. Pay in full and the bank charges no interest at all. The moment you leave even a rupee of the statement unpaid, that entire interest-free promise is withdrawn.

How interest and the minimum-due trap work

When you do not pay in full, the bank charges a finance charge on the outstanding amount, commonly about 3% to 3.75% per month. Because this compounds, the true annual cost is roughly 36% to 45% a year — a live example of compound interest working against you. Worse, once you revolve a balance, interest applies from the original transaction date with no grace, and fresh purchases start accruing interest immediately too. Banks show a tempting minimum amount due, often just 5% of the balance. Paying only this keeps the account from being marked overdue, but the remaining 95% keeps compounding. Clearing a large balance at the minimum can take many years and cost more in interest than the original spend. If you cannot pay in full, converting the balance to a structured EMI at a lower rate is almost always cheaper than revolving at the card rate.

Using a card well: utilisation and CIBIL

The single biggest driver of a healthy credit score (CIBIL) is on-time payment history, so never miss a due date. The second lever is credit utilisation — your balance divided by your total limit. Bureaus prefer to see this stay low, ideally well under 30%, because a card that is constantly maxed out signals stress. Pay in full, keep utilisation modest, avoid cash withdrawals on the card (they attract charges from day one), and treat the limit as a safety ceiling rather than a spending target. A card managed this way builds the record that later helps you qualify for a cheaper home loan. To see how cards sit within the wider borrowing landscape, visit the Loans, Credit and Debt hub.

Worked example
Ananya, a marketing executive in Pune, spends ₹60,000 on her credit card for a phone and some travel. When the statement arrives, she is short on cash and decides to pay only the minimum due of 5% each month. Her card charges 3.5% per month. How costly is this choice?

Step 1 — Understand the numbers. The outstanding balance is ₹60,000. The monthly interest rate is 3.5%, which is roughly 42% a year. The minimum due is 5% of the balance. By paying only the minimum, Ananya forfeits the interest-free period entirely, and interest applies on the full balance every month.

Step 2 — The first month. Interest for month one is ₹60,000 × 3.5% = ₹2,100. Her minimum payment is 5% of ₹60,000 = ₹3,000. Of that ₹3,000, ₹2,100 merely covers interest, so only ₹900 reduces the principal. The balance barely moves.

Step 3 — Watch the balance crawl. Because most of each minimum payment is eaten by interest, the principal falls painfully slowly in the early months, as the table shows.

Step 4 — The long-run cost. Continuing to pay only the shrinking minimum, Ananya would take several years to clear ₹60,000, and the total interest paid would run well beyond ₹30,000 — more than half the original spend again. Had she instead paid the full ₹60,000 by the due date, her interest cost would have been exactly zero.

So what? The minimum due is designed to keep you borrowing, not to help you clear debt. If Ananya cannot pay in full, converting the ₹60,000 into a fixed EMI at, say, 16% a year would slash her cost dramatically compared with revolving at 42%. The lesson is simple: a credit card is only free if the full statement is paid on time.

Check yourself
Q
You pay your full statement balance by every due date. How much interest does the card charge you?
A: Zero. Paying the full statement balance by the due date keeps the interest-free period, so no finance charge applies. Interest is levied only when you leave part of the balance unpaid.

Key takeaways

  • A credit card is unsecured revolving credit; you enjoy an interest-free period only if you pay the full statement balance by the due date.
  • Revolving an unpaid balance costs about 3% to 3.75% per month, roughly 36% to 45% a year, with interest charged from the transaction date and no grace.
  • Paying only the minimum due keeps you in debt for years and can cost more in interest than the original purchase.
  • Keep credit utilisation (balance divided by limit) low, ideally under 30%, and never miss a payment.
  • On-time payment history is the biggest driver of your credit score (CIBIL), and a well-run card builds the record that helps you borrow cheaply later.

Frequently asked questions

Is a credit card free to use?

It is interest-free only if you pay the full statement balance by the due date. Carry a balance and you pay very high interest, commonly about 3% to 3.75% per month.

What happens if I pay only the minimum due?

You avoid being marked overdue, but the rest of the balance keeps compounding at the card rate. Clearing it can take years and cost far more in interest than you spent.

How is a credit card different from a debit card?

A debit card spends your own bank balance instantly. A credit card borrows from the bank up to a limit, and you repay later.

Does a credit card affect my credit score?

Yes. On-time payments and low utilisation lift your credit score (CIBIL), while missed payments and a maxed-out card pull it down.

What is a good credit utilisation ratio?

Utilisation is your balance divided by your limit. Bureaus generally prefer it kept low, ideally well under 30% of the total limit.

Should I withdraw cash on my credit card?

Avoid it. Cash withdrawals usually attract a fee plus interest from the very first day, with no interest-free period at all.
