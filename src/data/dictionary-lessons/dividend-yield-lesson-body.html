Dividend Yield

Dividend Yield is the annual dividend a company pays per share expressed as a percentage of its current market price, showing how much cash income a shareholder earns each year for every rupee invested in the stock. It lets you compare the income from an equity share against alternatives like a fixed deposit.

What is Dividend Yield?

Dividend Yield tells you the cash return you get from a stock just from its dividend, before any change in the share price. If a share trades at ₹400 and pays ₹24 a year in dividends, its dividend yield is 6% — meaning you earn ₹6 of dividend income for every ₹100 you have parked in that stock.

Think of a retiree in Pune who owns shares of a large PSU like an energy or mining company. Every year the company transfers dividend straight into her bank account. Dividend yield is the number that lets her ask: "Am I earning more from this stock's dividends than I would from a bank FD?" That is why income-focused Indian investors watch it closely.

Crucially, yield moves inversely with price. When a stock falls, its dividend yield rises (same dividend, lower price); when it rallies, yield falls. So a very high yield is not always good news — it can signal a beaten-down price or a dividend the market expects to be cut.

Do not confuse dividend yield with dividend rate. Indian companies often declare dividends as a percentage of face value (say "150% dividend" on a ₹10 face value = ₹15 per share). That percentage is not the yield. To get yield you must divide the rupee dividend by the market price, which is usually far above face value. A related idea is the payout ratio, the share of net profit (or EPS) paid out as dividend — a company with high yield but a payout above 100% is paying more than it earns, which is unsustainable.

Types of dividend yield

The same formula can be measured against different reference points. Knowing which one you are looking at prevents costly mistakes.

TypeWhat it usesBest for
Trailing yieldDividends paid over the last 12 months ÷ today's priceWhat most screeners and websites show
Forward yieldExpected next-year dividend ÷ today's priceJudging future income if a change is expected
Yield on costCurrent dividend ÷ your original buy priceLong-term holders tracking income growth

Yield on cost is the most satisfying number for patient investors. If you bought at ₹400 and the dividend has since grown to ₹40, your yield on cost is 10% even though a new buyer at today's higher price earns much less. This reflects the power of compounding dividend growth over time.

Why it matters for Indian investors

Dividend yield matters most for income and for valuation. As an income tool, it lets you weigh a stock against a bank FD or a recurring deposit. As a valuation clue, a yield well above a stock's historical average may hint the share is cheap — a signal worth reading alongside its P/E ratio.

Remember the tax angle: since April 2020, dividends are taxed in the investor's hands at slab rate, and TDS applies above ₹5,000 of dividend in a year. So a headline 6% yield is a pre-tax figure. High-slab investors keep less of it. Also weigh inflation — a 6% yield barely beats the RBI's 4% CPI target (with its 2–6% band), so real income can be thin unless the dividend itself grows.

Example

Worked example
Ananya, a 30-year-old software engineer in Bengaluru, has ₹2,00,000 to invest for income. A large-cap PSU share trades at ₹400 and paid ₹24 in dividends over the last year. A bank FD offers 7%. She wants to compare the income and understand how yield behaves over time.

Step 1 — Current dividend yield. Using the formula, yield = 24 ÷ 400 × 100 = 6.0%. That is below the 7% FD, so on income alone the FD wins today.

Step 2 — Shares and income. With ₹2,00,000 she buys 2,00,000 ÷ 400 = 500 shares. Annual dividend = 500 × ₹24 = ₹12,000. The FD would pay 7% of ₹2,00,000 = ₹14,000. So far the FD gives ₹2,000 more.

Step 3 — But dividends grow. Suppose next year the company raises its dividend to ₹28 per share and the price rises to ₹460. Her income becomes 500 × ₹28 = ₹14,000, now matching the FD — and her yield on cost is 28 ÷ 400 = 7.0%, while a new buyer's current yield is only 28 ÷ 460 = 6.09%.

So what? The FD locks in a fixed 7% and returns exactly ₹2,00,000 at maturity — safe and predictable, protected by DICGC insurance up to ₹5 lakh. The stock started with lower income but its dividend and its price grew, lifting her capital to ₹2,30,000 and her yield-on-cost above the FD by Year 2. The trade-off is risk versus return: the share price could just as easily have fallen 15%, and the dividend could be cut. Ananya's real choice is between a guaranteed flat return and a variable but potentially growing one. That is exactly the judgment dividend yield is meant to inform — it is a starting question, not the final answer.

Limitations to keep in mind

A high yield can be a value trap: the price may have crashed because the market expects the dividend to be slashed. Dividend yield also ignores capital gains — a fast-growing company with a tiny yield can hugely outperform a high-yielder over a decade. And it says nothing about business quality; always cross-check profit growth, debt, and cash flow. For diversified income, many investors prefer a mutual fund or a dividend-focused ETF rather than betting on a single high-yield stock.

Check yourself
A stock trades at ₹250 and pays a total dividend of ₹10 per share this year. What is its dividend yield, and what happens to the yield if the price falls to ₹200 with the dividend unchanged?
At ₹250: yield = 10 ÷ 250 × 100 = 4.0%. If the price falls to ₹200 with the same ₹10 dividend, yield = 10 ÷ 200 × 100 = 5.0%. Yield rises as price falls — but check why the price fell before treating the higher yield as a bargain.

Key takeaways

  • Dividend Yield = annual dividend per share ÷ market price × 100.
  • Yield moves inversely to price — a very high yield may be a warning, not a gift.
  • Dividend rate on face value is not the same as yield on market price.
  • Indian dividends are taxed at slab rate with TDS above ₹5,000 — the headline yield is pre-tax.
  • Yield on cost rewards long-term holders as dividends grow.
  • Use yield alongside P/E, payout ratio, and business quality — never alone.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good dividend yield in India?

There is no single "good" number, but yields of 2–6% are common among stable large-caps and PSUs. A yield sharply above a stock's own history or its sector deserves scrutiny — it may reflect a depressed price or an expected dividend cut rather than a genuine opportunity.

Is dividend yield better than a fixed deposit?

They serve different needs. An FD gives a guaranteed, DICGC-insured return, while dividends are variable and can be cut. However, a growing dividend plus potential capital appreciation can beat an FD over time. The FD wins on safety; the stock offers growth with higher risk.

Are dividends taxed in India?

Yes. Since FY 2020–21, dividends are taxed in the investor's hands at their income-tax slab rate. Companies deduct TDS at 10% once your total dividend from that company crosses ₹5,000 in a financial year.

Why do some good companies have a very low dividend yield?

Fast-growing companies often reinvest profits into expansion instead of paying them out, so their yield is low or zero. Their reward comes as capital gains. A low yield is not bad — it can signal a company choosing growth over immediate income.

Does a high dividend yield mean the stock is cheap?

Not always. A high yield can appear because the price has crashed on bad news — a classic value trap. Always check whether the dividend is sustainable by looking at the payout ratio, earnings, and cash flow before buying purely for yield.

How is dividend yield different from dividend rate?

Dividend rate is the dividend declared as a percentage of the share's face value (e.g. 150% of ₹10 = ₹15). Dividend yield divides that rupee amount by the current market price. Because market price is usually far above face value, the yield is much smaller than the declared rate.
