Inflation

Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices for goods and services rises over time, reducing the purchasing power of money. In India it is officially measured by the year-on-year change in the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which the Reserve Bank of India targets at 4%, within a tolerance band of 2% to 6%.

What is inflation?

Inflation is the steady rise in the overall price of the things a household buys, from atta and onions to petrol, rent and school fees. When inflation runs at 6% a year, a basket of goods that costs ₹100 today costs ₹106 a year later. The money itself has not changed, but each rupee now buys less. This silent loss of value is why inflation is often called a "hidden tax" on savers.

A relatable example: a plate of pav bhaji that cost your parents ₹20 in the early 2000s can cost ₹120 or more today. Nothing about the dish changed, only the value of the rupee. That is inflation working quietly across two decades.

In India, the Ministry of Statistics (MoSPI) publishes CPI data monthly, and the RBI uses it to set the repo rate. A little inflation is normal and even healthy, it signals a growing economy. The danger is when inflation runs too hot or becomes unpredictable.

How inflation is measured and how it works

Inflation is calculated from a price index that tracks a fixed "basket" of goods and services weighted by how much households actually spend on each. The headline number is the percentage change in that index from one year to the next.

What actually pushes prices up? Broadly two forces: demand-pull inflation, when too much money chases too few goods, and cost-push inflation, when input costs like crude oil, wages or a weak rupee rise and get passed on to buyers.

Step 2 — Check whether the FD keeps up. The ₹1,00,000 FD at 7% grows to 1,00,000 × (1.07)10 = ₹1,96,715 in nominal terms. Impressive on paper.

Step 3 — Convert to today's rupees. Divide by the inflation factor (1.06)10 = 1.7908: ₹1,96,715 ÷ 1.7908 = ₹1,09,842 in 2026 purchasing power.

Understanding the risk vs return trade-off, and considering growth assets like equity shares or an index fund through a disciplined SIP, is how long-term savers aim to beat inflation.

Why inflation matters for your money

Inflation is the benchmark every investment must clear. A return that merely matches inflation leaves you no richer; only the real return (return minus inflation) builds wealth. This is why long-term investors spread money across asset classes, using diversification to seek returns that outpace prices over time. Equity, via a mutual fund or direct stocks, has historically delivered the strongest inflation-beating returns in India, though with higher volatility along the way.

Inflation also drives RBI policy. When inflation breaches the 6% upper band, the RBI typically raises the repo rate to cool demand, which in turn lifts FD and loan rates. So the number on your grocery bill is tightly linked to the interest you earn and the interest you pay.

Check yourself
If your bank FD pays 7% and inflation is 6%, roughly what is your real return, and why does it matter?
The real return is about 0.94% a year, calculated as (1.07 ÷ 1.06) − 1. It matters because only the return above inflation actually grows your purchasing power. A headline 7% can feel safe while barely keeping you ahead of rising prices, and taxes can wipe out even that.

Key takeaways

  • Inflation is the rate at which prices rise and the purchasing power of the rupee falls over time.
  • India measures it via CPI; the RBI targets 4% within a 2% to 6% band.
  • At 6% inflation, prices roughly double every 12 years (Rule of 72).
  • What matters is the real return = nominal return − inflation, not the headline rate.
  • FDs may not beat inflation after tax; growth assets like equity historically do, with more risk.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good inflation rate for India?

The RBI aims for CPI inflation of 4%, with a tolerance band of 2% to 6%. Mild inflation in this range is considered healthy because it supports growth without badly hurting savers. Trouble begins when inflation stays above 6% for long or turns unpredictable.

What causes inflation?

Two broad forces: demand-pull, when strong spending outpaces the supply of goods, and cost-push, when input costs like crude oil, food, wages or a weaker rupee rise and get passed to consumers. Excess money supply and supply-chain shocks can amplify both.

How does the RBI control inflation?

Mainly through the repo rate. When inflation runs hot, the RBI raises rates to make borrowing costlier, cooling demand. When inflation is low and growth is weak, it cuts rates to encourage spending. This is called monetary policy, decided by the Monetary Policy Committee.

What is the difference between CPI and WPI?

CPI tracks retail prices that households actually pay and is the RBI's official inflation target. WPI tracks wholesale prices at the producer level. CPI matters more to you as a consumer because it reflects your real cost of living.

How can I beat inflation on my savings?

You need investments whose returns exceed inflation after tax. Historically, equity via mutual funds, index funds or SIPs has outpaced inflation over the long run, while FDs often only match or trail it. Diversifying across assets and staying invested for years is the common approach.

Is inflation the same as interest rate?

No. Inflation is how fast prices rise; the interest rate is what you earn on savings or pay on loans. They are linked, though, the RBI raises interest rates to fight high inflation, so your FD and loan rates tend to move with it.
