Demat Account

Demat Account is the standard name for the electronic account in which your shares, bonds, fund units and other securities are held in dematerialised form instead of as paper certificates. In India the record sits with one of two depositories, NSDL or CDSL, and the account is opened for you by a SEBI-registered depository participant such as a bank or a broker. Securities are credited when you buy and debited when you sell, much as a bank account handles money. Anyone buying listed shares needs one, under the Depositories Act, 1996.

The depository, the participant and the number on your statement

NSDL and CDSL each keep a master record of who owns what, and neither deals with you directly. A depository participant stands in between, running your know-your-customer checks, opening the account, issuing statements and executing instructions. Your relationship is with the participant, your securities sit with the depository.

That explains the account number: the first eight characters are the DP ID identifying the participant, the next eight the client ID identifying you. NSDL accounts begin with the letters IN, CDSL accounts are sixteen digits. Inside, nothing is named. Each security carries an ISIN, a twelve-character code unique to that instrument, so an equity share, a bond and a fund unit are told apart by code.

Three accounts, three different jobs

New investors often assume the demat account does the buying. It does not. Placing the order is the trading account's work, holding the result the demat account's, and paying the bank's.

AccountWhat it holds or doesWho runs it
Bank accountRupees, moved on settlementYour bank
Trading accountPlaces orders on the NSE or BSEYour stockbroker
Demat accountHolds the securities themselvesA participant, recorded at NSDL or CDSL

The three are usually opened together, which is why the distinction blurs. It becomes visible when you hold securities without trading them, such as shares from an IPO allotment or mutual fund units in demat form, where the demat account alone suffices.

What the account costs to keep

Charges arrive in categories, and only some repeat.

C with the subscript annual is the yearly cost of the account and AMC is the annual maintenance charge for keeping it open. The letter n with the subscript debit counts debit instructions in the year, in practice the days on which you sold something, and c with the subscript debit is the flat charge per instruction. Other service charges cover one-off requests such as converting physical certificates or creating a pledge.

The shape matters more than any figure. A credit is free, so buying costs nothing at the depository level; selling triggers the debit charge whatever the value; and maintenance continues in a year with no orders. Participants publish a tariff sheet that must be disclosed to you, so read that rather than a general estimate.

Basic Services Demat Account, and naming a nominee

Small investors have a cheaper option. Under the SEBI framework revised in June 2024, an individual who is sole or first holder, holds only one demat account across both depositories and keeps holdings within ten lakh rupees qualifies for a Basic Services Demat Account. No maintenance charge applies while holdings stay within four lakh rupees, and a capped charge applies above that. Cross the upper limit and ordinary terms return.

Nomination deserves similar attention and gets less. SEBI's 2025 framework allows multiple nominees, lets a sole holder formally opt out instead, and strips away much of the paperwork heirs once faced. With neither a nominee nor an opt-out on record, your family must prove entitlement through succession documents.

Worked example
Rohan Kulkarni, a 26-year-old in Nagpur, buys 40 shares of a listed company at ₹1,250 on a Tuesday, then sells all 40 at ₹1,310 three weeks later. Where does the money go, where do the shares go, and what does the demat account contribute to each leg?

Step 1, the buy order. On Tuesday morning the order goes to the exchange from his trading account and executes at ₹1,250, a trade value of ₹50,000. His bank balance is blocked rather than debited, and his demat account shows nothing. Ownership does not pass at the moment of matching.

Step 2, settlement on the next working day. Indian equity settlement runs on a T+1 cycle, so on Wednesday the funds move to the clearing corporation and the 40 shares move the other way. Since SEBI made direct payout mandatory, the clearing corporation credits the shares straight into Rohan's demat account rather than routing them through the broker's pool account. Only now is the ISIN visible in his holdings.

Step 3, what he actually paid. Statutory and exchange costs sit on top of the trade value, and no depository debit charge applies to a purchase.

Step 4, the sale leg. Selling at ₹1,310 gives a trade value of ₹52,400. A depository debit charge now applies, levied once for the instruction whether he sold 40 shares or 400. Proceeds reach his bank the next working day and his holdings show zero.

Step 5, the year-end bill. Rohan sold on six days that year, so he paid six debit charges plus his participant's maintenance charge. His holdings never exceeded four lakh rupees and he keeps no second account, so he qualifies for a Basic Services Demat Account, where maintenance at that level is nil.

So what? The demat account is a custody record, not a trading tool: nothing on the way in, a charge per selling instruction rather than per share, and a maintenance fee that does not care whether you traded. Consolidating stray accounts and checking BSDA eligibility removes a recurring cost unrelated to how your investments perform.

Check yourself
Q
You buy shares worth ₹2,00,000 and sell shares worth ₹5,000 on the same day. Which transaction attracts a depository debit charge?
A: Only the sale, because the charge attaches to the debit instruction moving securities out of your account. It is a flat amount per instruction, so the small sale can cost the same as a large one.

Key takeaways

  • A demat account holds securities electronically, with NSDL or CDSL keeping the record and a participant serving you.
  • The sixteen-character account number combines a DP ID and a client ID, and every security is identified by ISIN.
  • The trading account places orders, the demat account holds the result, and the bank account pays.
  • Costs arrive as a maintenance charge, a debit charge per selling instruction, and one-off service charges.
  • A single account with holdings within ten lakh rupees qualifies for a Basic Services Demat Account.

Frequently asked questions

Can I hold more than one demat account?

Yes, with the same or different participants, provided the PAN matches. Each carries its own maintenance charge, and holding more than one disqualifies you from the Basic Services category, so a single account suits most individual investors.

Do I need a demat account to invest in mutual funds?

No. Units can be held in statement form with the registrar, which is how most direct investors hold them. A demat account is optional for funds but necessary for exchange traded funds, which are bought and sold on the exchange like shares.

What happens to my shares if my broker shuts down?

They remain yours, being recorded at the depository against your name and not on the broker's books. You can move the account to another participant or approach the depository directly. That separation is why direct payout of securities to client accounts was made mandatory.

Can I still hold physical share certificates?

You can hold them, but not transfer them in paper form, since transfers of listed securities must be dematerialised. Old certificates are converted by lodging a dematerialisation request form with them at your participant, which takes a few weeks.

Is a demat account needed to apply for an IPO?

Yes, because allotted shares can only be credited electronically. The application also needs a bank account supporting the blocked-amount facility; a trading account matters only when you sell after listing.

How do I keep the account safe?

Read the consolidated account statement the depository sends, register for transaction alerts, and never hand over instruction slips signed in blank. If you are away, the account can be frozen for debits and unfrozen later.
