Coupon Rate

Coupon Rate is a bond's annual interest expressed as a percentage of its face value, rather than its market price. On a plain fixed-rate bond, the rate is set at issue and stays unchanged; floating-rate bonds reset it according to their terms. Dated government securities generally pay coupons half-yearly, while corporate payment schedules vary by issue. Because market prices move, coupon rate, current yield and yield to maturity can differ.

Fixed at issue, and fixed for the life of the bond

A fixed-rate security with half-yearly payments described as a 7.10 percent bond of 2034 pays ₹7.10 a year on every ₹100 of face value, in halves of ₹3.55 on two fixed dates. The issuer set that rate at the primary issue, judging what investors would demand then, and from that point it is contractual. A borrower cannot trim it because rates have since fallen, and a holder cannot demand more because rates have risen.

That makes a bond unlike a bank deposit. A fixed deposit also locks a rate at the start, but nobody quotes your deposit at 94 paise in the rupee this afternoon. A bond carries both a rigid promise and a fluid price, and most confusion about coupon rates comes from mixing up the two.

Three numbers that sound alike and mean different things

The rupee coupon follows directly from the stated rate.

C is the rupee coupon paid over a full year, F is the face value printed on the bond, and c is the coupon rate as a decimal. Seven percent of ₹1,000 gives ₹70 a year, whatever the owner paid.

Current yield asks a narrower question: what income is that coupon on today's purchase price?

C is again the annual rupee coupon and P is the market price paid, so ₹70 bought for ₹900 is a current yield of 7.78 percent. Its weakness is that it stops there, ignoring the face value waiting at maturity. Yield to maturity folds in every coupon and that redemption, and is what a serious buyer works with.

When the market disagrees with the coupon, the price adjusts

Suppose comparable borrowers can now raise money at 9 percent. Nobody will pay ₹1,000 for an old bond handing out ₹70 a year, and the coupon cannot rise to meet the market, so the price falls until the return on offer matches what fresh issues pay. Reverse the logic and a bond paying more than the going rate is bid above par. For unchanged cash flows, price and yield move in opposite directions. Changes in the repo rate can influence market yields, alongside expectations, credit risk and liquidity.

Reading a coupon rate off an Indian security

One wrinkle catches buyers in the secondary market. Interest accrues day by day to the seller between coupon dates, so a purchase settles at the quoted clean price plus that accrued interest, a total known as the dirty price. It is not an extra charge; it stops the coupon from handing a windfall to whoever owns the bond on payment day. For a corporate issue, the term sheet spells out the rate, the frequency and any reset clause.

Worked example
Sneha Kulkarni, a radiologist in Pune, is shown two corporate bonds. Both have a face value of ₹1,000, both carry a coupon rate of 7 percent, and both mature in exactly five years. Bond A is quoted at ₹900 and Bond B at ₹1,100. Assume both pay half-yearly, have no call option, and are purchased on a coupon date with no accrued interest; ignore tax and fees. If the coupon rate on the two is identical, why are they priced so far apart, and which one actually pays her more?

Step 1, the rupee coupon is identical on both. Seven percent of the ₹1,000 face value is ₹70 a year, baked into both instruments. Buying Bond A cheaply does not shrink her coupon to ₹63, nor does paying up for Bond B lift it to ₹77. Each hands her ₹70 a year, in two instalments of ₹35.

Step 2, current yield already separates them. Dividing ₹70 by the ₹900 that Bond A costs gives 7.78 percent. The same ₹70 over the ₹1,100 that Bond B costs gives 6.36 percent. One coupon, two income yields, because the denominators differ.

Step 3, redemption pulls them further apart. Both repay ₹1,000 at maturity whatever Sneha paid. Bond A therefore returns ₹100 more than her outlay on top of the coupons, while Bond B returns ₹100 less. Discounting ten half-yearly receipts of ₹35 plus the ₹1,000 redemption back to each price gives a nominal annual yield to maturity of 9.56 percent on Bond A and 4.73 percent on Bond B, calculated as twice the half-yearly yield.

Step 4, the total cash says the same thing without algebra. Over five years each bond pays ₹350 of coupons plus ₹1,000 at redemption, or ₹1,350 in all. Sneha spends ₹900 to collect that on Bond A and ₹1,100 to collect it on Bond B, so her net gain is ₹450 in the first case and ₹250 in the second.

So what? The coupon rate tells Sneha what the bond pays, never what she earns. Two identical coupons produced a gap of almost five percentage points in yield to maturity purely because the entry price differed. Equal coupon and maturity do not make the bonds equally risky: different issuer credit, liquidity or other terms can explain the price gap. All cash totals assume payments arrive as promised. One caution belongs alongside that: a bond at a deep discount is often cheap for a reason, usually doubt about the issuer, so a fat yield to maturity should send her to the credit rating first.

Check yourself
Q
A bond with a 6 percent coupon on ₹100 face value is trading at ₹80. What is its coupon rate now?
A: Still 6 percent. The coupon rate is fixed against face value at issue and does not move with the traded price. What changed is the current yield: ₹6 divided by ₹80, or 7.5 percent.

Key takeaways

  • On a plain fixed-rate bond, the coupon rate is set against face value at issue and remains unchanged.
  • Dated government securities generally pay half-yearly; corporate bond schedules depend on the issue terms.
  • Current yield divides the annual coupon by the market price and ignores the gain or loss at redemption.
  • Yield to maturity adds the pull back to face value, and is the fairer comparison between bonds.
  • When market rates move, an existing bond's price moves the opposite way, because the coupon cannot.

Frequently asked questions

Is the coupon rate the same as the interest rate on a bond?

It is the contractual rate, not the rate of return. The coupon rate fixes the rupee payment against face value, while your return depends on what you paid, while quoted yield to maturity describes the contractual cash flows rather than a guaranteed realised return.

Can a coupon rate ever change?

On a plain fixed-rate bond, no. Floating rate bonds reset their coupon periodically against a reference rate, so the payment moves even though the spread is fixed. The government issues these alongside its fixed-coupon securities.

What is a zero coupon bond?

A bond with a coupon rate of zero. It pays nothing along the way and is sold below face value, the whole return being the gap between purchase price and redemption. Treasury bills work this way.

How often are coupons paid in India?

Half-yearly is standard for conventional dated government securities. Corporate bond payment schedules vary by issue. Some issues pay annually, quarterly or monthly, and a few accumulate everything until redemption.

Does a higher coupon rate mean a better bond?

Not by itself. A high coupon reflects either a high rate environment at issue or a weaker credit that had to pay up. Compare yield to maturity for return and the rating for risk.

How is coupon income taxed for an Indian investor?

For a resident individual, taxable bond interest is generally included in income and taxed at applicable slab rates, with tax deducted at source where required. Exempt securities and investor-specific rules can differ. A sale before maturity falls under the capital gains rules; confirm your position with the Income Tax Department.
