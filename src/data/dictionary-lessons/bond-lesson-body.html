What a bond is

When you buy a bond, you lend money to an issuer, such as the Government of India, a state government or a company. The face value (or par value) is the principal stated on the security. The coupon is the contractual interest payment, usually expressed as a percentage of face value, and maturity is the date on which the issuer is scheduled to redeem the bond.

For example, a plain ₹1,000 bond with a 7% annual coupon pays ₹70 each year and returns ₹1,000 at maturity, assuming the issuer meets its obligations. That predictable cash-flow pattern is why bonds are called fixed income, although not every bond has a fixed coupon and neither market price nor realised return is necessarily fixed. Unlike a typical bank fixed deposit, many bonds can be traded before maturity.

The inverse price-yield relationship

A fixed-rate bond’s coupon does not change when market rates change, but its market price can. All else equal, when required market yields rise, the present value of the bond’s fixed cash flows falls; when required yields fall, its price rises. RBI policy, including the repo rate, can influence market yields, but inflation expectations, liquidity, government borrowing, maturity and issuer credit also matter.

Current yield is a quick measure that compares the annual coupon with the bond’s market price:

If a ₹1,000 face-value bond pays ₹70 annually and trades at ₹950, its current yield is about 7.37%. Current yield ignores the redemption payment, time remaining and reinvestment of coupons. The more complete hold-to-maturity measure is yield to maturity.

The risks

Interest-rate risk is the risk that changing yields move a bond’s price; longer-duration bonds are generally more sensitive. Credit risk is the possibility that an issuer does not pay as promised. Credit ratings can help assess relative creditworthiness, but they are opinions rather than guarantees and can be revised. Liquidity risk matters when a bond is difficult to sell near a fair price. Inflation can also erode the purchasing power of fixed payments, while call and reinvestment risk affect some securities. These trade-offs belong in any risk-versus-return comparison.

Worked example
Priya buys a newly issued 10-year Government of India bond with a face value of ₹1,000 and a 7% annual coupon. Immediately after she receives the first coupon, similar nine-year bonds yield 8%. Assuming annual payments, what has she received and what is her bond worth at that point?

Step 1 — Calculate the coupon. Priya receives 7% of ₹1,000, or ₹70. The coupon remains fixed by the bond’s terms.

Step 2 — Discount the nine remaining coupons. With nine years left and a required yield of 8%, their present value is ₹70 × [1 − (1.08)−9] ÷ 0.08, or approximately ₹437.28.

Step 3 — Discount the redemption value. The present value of the ₹1,000 paid after nine years is ₹1,000 ÷ (1.08)9, or approximately ₹500.25.

Step 4 — Interpret the result. Priya has received ₹70, while the bond is worth about ₹937.53 immediately after that payment under the simplified assumptions. If she sells, the market-price decline is realised. If she holds to maturity and the government pays as scheduled, she continues receiving ₹70 annually and receives ₹1,000 at redemption.

So what? A G-Sec removes domestic credit risk in RBI’s description, but not market risk. The earlier ₹875 shortcut would only force current yield to 8%; it ignores the ₹1,000 redemption value and materially overstates the price fall.

Check yourself
A ₹1,000 face-value bond pays a ₹60 annual coupon and trades at ₹800. What is its current yield, and what does the lower price suggest about required market yields if the issuer’s credit and other factors are unchanged?
A: Current yield is ₹60 ÷ ₹800 = 7.5%. The 6% coupon rate is below the 7.5% current yield because the bond trades at a discount. If credit and other factors are unchanged, the lower price is consistent with required market yields having risen. Current yield alone does not give the bond’s YTM.

Key takeaways

  • A bond is debt: the issuer promises contractual cash flows and redemption according to the issue terms.
  • For fixed-rate bonds, required yields and prices generally move in opposite directions.
  • Central-government securities carry no domestic credit risk in RBI’s framework, but their market prices can still fall.
  • Corporate bonds add issuer credit risk; ratings are useful opinions, not guarantees.
  • Current yield compares coupon with price, while YTM incorporates remaining coupons, redemption value and time.

Frequently asked questions

Is a bond safer than a share?

Often, but not automatically. Bondholders rank ahead of shareholders if an issuer is liquidated, and Indian government securities carry no domestic credit risk according to RBI. Corporate bonds can still default, and all traded fixed-rate bonds can lose market value when yields rise.

How is a bond different from a fixed deposit?

A bond is a marketable security whose price can change before maturity. A bank fixed deposit is not exchange-traded and usually permits early closure subject to its terms and a penalty.

Can I lose money on a government bond?

A G-Sec’s market price can fall, so selling before maturity can crystallise a loss. Inflation and reinvestment rates can also reduce real or realised returns.

What does a bond credit rating mean?

It is an agency opinion about relative creditworthiness, not a guarantee. It can change and does not measure every risk.

How can a retail investor in India buy government bonds?

Eligible individuals can use RBI Retail Direct to access T-Bills, dated G-Secs and State Development Loans in primary and secondary markets.

Why does a fixed bond’s coupon stay the same when its price changes?

The coupon is contractual. Market yield adjusts through the price investors pay for those fixed cash flows.
