Rule of 72

Rule of 72 is a quick mental-maths shortcut that estimates how many years an investment takes to double in value, by dividing the number 72 by the annual rate of return. It is a fast approximation of compound interest, not an exact formula.

What is the Rule of 72?

The Rule of 72 is a back-of-the-envelope trick that tells you roughly how long money takes to double at a given annual growth rate. You simply divide 72 by the yearly return (in per cent). If your mutual fund compounds at 12% a year, then 72 ÷ 12 = 6 years to double. Invest ₹1,00,000 today and, at 12%, you should have about ₹2,00,000 in six years without adding a single rupee.

It works because compounding is exponential, not linear. A bank fixed deposit paying 6.5% doubles your money in roughly 72 ÷ 6.5 = 11 years, while a broad index fund tracking the Nifty 50 at a long-run 12% doubles it in half that time. That single comparison is why so many Indian investors shift long-term goals from FDs toward equity. The rule is deliberately approximate, but it is close enough to reshape how you think about risk vs return.

Why 72 and not some other number? The mathematically exact doubling time uses natural logarithms, and for continuous compounding the "true" numerator is about 69.3. But 72 is chosen because it divides cleanly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12, making mental maths effortless, and it is most accurate in the 6%–10% range where most Indian debt and hybrid products sit. Unlike simple interest, which grows in a straight line, the Rule of 72 captures the snowballing effect of returns earning returns.

Rule of 72 versus related rules

The "72" family has cousins for different questions. Use the right numerator for what you want to estimate.

For a 12% equity return, that means doubling in 6 years, tripling in about 9.5 years (114 ÷ 12), and quadrupling in 12 years (144 ÷ 12). Each equity share or fund unit you hold quietly follows the same arithmetic.

Example

Worked example
Priya, a 30-year-old in Pune, has ₹2,00,000 to invest for 24 years toward retirement. She is torn between a bank fixed deposit paying 6.5% and an equity mutual fund she expects to compound at 12%. Using the Rule of 72, how differently do these two choices grow, and what is the "so what"?

Step 1 — Find each doubling time. For the FD: 72 ÷ 6.5 = 11.08 years to double. For the equity fund: 72 ÷ 12 = 6 years to double.

Step 2 — Count the doublings in 24 years. The equity fund doubles every 6 years, so over 24 years it doubles 4 times (24 ÷ 6). The FD doubles every 11.08 years, so it manages about 2.17 doublings (24 ÷ 11.08).

Step 3 — Apply the doublings to ₹2,00,000. Each doubling multiplies the corpus by 2.

YearsEquity fund @ 12%Fixed deposit @ 6.5%
0₹2,00,000₹2,00,000
6₹4,00,000₹2,88,000
12₹8,00,000₹4,15,000
18₹16,00,000₹5,98,000
24₹32,00,000₹9,00,000

Step 4 — Read the result. After 24 years the equity fund reaches four clean doublings: 2 → 4 → 8 → 16 → ₹32 lakh. The FD, with only 2.17 doublings, grows to roughly 22.17 × ₹2,00,000 ≈ ₹9 lakh.

So what? The same ₹2,00,000 ends up more than three-and-a-half times larger in the equity route, purely because a higher rate slashes the doubling period from 11 years to 6. And remember inflation: at the RBI's 6% upper tolerance band, prices also double in 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years, so the FD's ₹9 lakh buys far less in real terms while the equity corpus stays comfortably ahead. Priya cannot control markets, but the Rule of 72 shows her exactly why a couple of extra percentage points, held for decades, is the difference between a modest and a life-changing outcome. Naturally, equity carries volatility the FD does not, so this is a horizon-and-risk decision, not a free lunch.

Where it helps and where it breaks

The Rule of 72 shines for quick comparisons: sizing up an FD against a recurring deposit, judging a SIP pitch, or gut-checking whether a "double your money" scheme is realistic. If someone promises to double your money in 2 years, the rule implies a 36% annual return (72 ÷ 2) — a red flag that usually signals fraud.

Its limits matter too. The rule assumes a fixed, steady rate with returns reinvested; it ignores taxes, exit loads, and the real-world bumpiness of markets. It grows less accurate at extreme rates — below 4% or above 20%, switch to the Rule of 70 or an exact calculator. It also does not account for SIP top-ups or lump-sum additions; it models a single amount left to compound.

Check yourself
You buy an index fund expecting an 8% annual return. Using the Rule of 72, roughly how long until your money doubles?
A: 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years. Your investment should roughly double in nine years, assuming the 8% return holds steady and gains are reinvested.

Key takeaways

  • Divide 72 by the annual return to estimate years to double: 72 ÷ 12% = 6 years.
  • Flip it — 72 ÷ years — to find the rate you need for a goal.
  • It is most accurate for returns between 6% and 10%; use 70 for low rates and inflation.
  • Sister rules: 114 to triple, 144 to quadruple.
  • It is an approximation — it ignores taxes, fees, and market volatility.
  • A promised "double in 2 years" implies a 36% return, a common scam signal.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Rule of 72 accurate?

It is a close approximation, not exact. In the 6%–10% range it is usually within a few months of the true doubling time calculated with logarithms. Accuracy drops at very high or very low rates, where the Rule of 70 or a proper compounding calculator works better.

Does the Rule of 72 work for a fixed deposit?

Yes. At a 6.5% FD rate, 72 ÷ 6.5 ≈ 11 years to double. Just remember FD interest is taxable at your slab, so your post-tax doubling time is a bit longer than the headline number suggests. DICGC insures bank deposits up to ₹5 lakh per bank.

Can I use the Rule of 72 for inflation?

Absolutely, and it is one of the best uses. At 6% inflation — the RBI's upper tolerance band around its 4% target — prices double in 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years, meaning your purchasing power halves. This is why parking long-term money in low-yield accounts quietly erodes wealth.

How is the Rule of 72 different from compound interest?

Compound interest is the actual exponential formula that grows your money. The Rule of 72 is a shortcut that estimates one specific output of that formula — the doubling time — without a calculator. Think of it as a quick mental proxy for the full compounding maths.

What return do I need to double money in 5 years?

Rearrange the rule: 72 ÷ 5 = about 14.4% a year. That is achievable historically with diversified equity over long horizons, but not guaranteed, and it comes with real volatility. Be sceptical of any product promising it as a "safe" or "fixed" return.

Why the number 72 specifically?

The mathematically precise numerator is roughly 69.3 for continuous compounding. But 72 is chosen because it divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12, making the mental arithmetic clean, while staying accurate for the mid-single-digit rates most people actually earn.
