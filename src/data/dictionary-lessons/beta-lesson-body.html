Beta

Beta is a measure of how much a stock or portfolio moves relative to the overall market, capturing its systematic (non-diversifiable) risk. Beta of 1 means the asset tends to move in line with the market, above 1 means it swings more sharply, and below 1 means it is steadier than the market.

What is Beta?

Beta tells you how sensitive an investment is to broad market moves. If the Nifty rises or falls, beta estimates how far your stock is likely to travel alongside it. A beta of 1.0 means the stock historically moves roughly one-for-one with the market. A beta of 1.5 suggests a move about 50% larger than the market, and a beta of 0.6 suggests a move about 40% smaller.

Think of a monsoon. The market is the rainfall over all of India. A high-beta stock like a small auto or metal company is a low-lying field that floods fast and dries fast. A low-beta stock like an FMCG or utility name is higher ground that barely notices the same rain. Both feel the monsoon, but not to the same degree.

Crucially, beta measures only systematic risk — the risk you cannot remove through diversification. It says nothing about company-specific risk like a fraud or a plant fire. This is why beta sits at the heart of the risk vs return trade-off.

Types of Beta

Not every beta means the same thing. The table below groups the common ranges and what they imply for an Indian equity investor.

Beta valueBehaviour vs marketTypical Indian examplesInvestor read
β > 1 (aggressive)Amplifies market movesMetals, real estate, autos, small capsHigher upside and higher drawdowns
β = 1 (market)Moves with the indexAn index fund or Nifty ETFPure market exposure

Note that an individual equity share and a diversified mutual fund can have very different betas even in the same sector, and beta is estimated from history — it shifts over time as a company's business and leverage change.

Worked example
Rohan, an investor in Pune, holds a ₹10,00,000 equity portfolio across three stocks. He wants to know his portfolio beta, how much he could lose if the Nifty drops 10%, and what return CAPM says he should demand. Assume a risk-free rate of 7% (10-year G-sec) and an expected market return of 13%.

Step 1 — List holdings, weights and betas. Portfolio beta is simply the value-weighted average of the individual betas.

StockAmount (₹)WeightBetaWeight × Beta
HDFC Bank4,00,0000.400.950.380
Infosys3,00,0000.300.800.240
Tata Motors3,00,0000.301.600.480
Total10,00,0001.00—1.100

Step 2 — Add the weighted betas. 0.380 + 0.240 + 0.480 = 1.10. Rohan's portfolio beta is 1.10, so it is about 10% more sensitive than the Nifty. The high beta of Tata Motors (1.60) pulls the whole portfolio above 1, even though HDFC Bank and Infosys are defensive.

Step 3 — Estimate the loss in a 10% market fall. Expected portfolio move = beta × market move = 1.10 × (−10%) = −11%. On ₹10,00,000 that is a fall of 0.11 × 10,00,000 = ₹1,10,000. If instead the Nifty rose 10%, the portfolio would be expected to gain the same ₹1,10,000 (a symmetric +11%).

Step 4 — Apply CAPM for the required return. E(R) = Rf + β(Rm − Rf) = 7% + 1.10 × (13% − 7%) = 7% + 1.10 × 6% = 7% + 6.6% = 13.6%. So Rohan should demand at least a 13.6% annual return to be compensated for the market risk he is carrying.

So what? Rohan is running a mildly aggressive book. In a sharp correction he should mentally prepare for an 11% hit — a bit worse than the index — but in rallies he captures more upside. If that ₹1,10,000 swing feels too large for his comfort, he can lower portfolio beta by trimming Tata Motors and adding a defensive FMCG name or a Nifty index fund, without needing to time the market. Beta turns a vague fear ("markets are risky") into a concrete rupee figure he can plan around.

Check yourself
If a stock has a beta of 0.7 and the Nifty falls 20%, roughly how much is the stock expected to fall, and is it aggressive or defensive?
Expected fall ≈ 0.7 × 20% = 14%. Because beta is below 1, the stock is defensive — it drops less than the market. Remember this is only an estimate of systematic risk; company-specific news could push the actual move higher or lower.

Key takeaways

  • Beta measures an asset's systematic risk — its sensitivity to overall market moves — not its total risk.
  • Beta = 1 moves with the market, above 1 is aggressive, below 1 is defensive, and negative beta moves opposite to the market.
  • It is calculated as covariance with the market divided by market variance, and it feeds directly into CAPM to set required return.
  • Portfolio beta is the value-weighted average of the individual betas.
  • Beta ignores diversifiable, company-specific risk and is estimated from past data, so it can drift over time.

FAQs

What is a good beta value for a stock?

There is no universally "good" beta — it depends on your goal. A cautious investor near retirement may prefer defensive stocks with beta below 1 to reduce swings, while a young investor chasing growth may accept beta above 1 for higher potential returns. The right beta is the one that matches your risk tolerance and time horizon.

Can beta be negative?

Yes, though it is uncommon for equities. A negative beta means the asset tends to move opposite to the market — rising when the market falls. Gold and certain hedging instruments sometimes behave this way, which is why they can cushion a portfolio during equity downturns.

Is a high beta always riskier?

High beta means larger swings relative to the market, so more systematic risk — but "risk" cuts both ways. A high-beta stock falls harder in corrections yet rises more in rallies. It is riskier in the sense of higher volatility, not automatically a bad investment. Beta also ignores company-specific risks entirely.

What is the difference between beta and volatility?

Volatility (standard deviation) measures how much a stock bounces around on its own, regardless of the market. Beta measures only the portion of that movement explained by the market. A stock can be highly volatile but have a low beta if its swings are unrelated to the index.

Where can I find the beta of an Indian stock?

Beta is published on stock exchange and broker platforms, financial data sites, and in the fact sheets of many mutual funds and ETFs. Different sources may show slightly different betas because they use different time periods and index benchmarks. Always check the lookback window (for example 1-year vs 3-year) before comparing.

Does an index fund have a beta of 1?

A fund tracking the same index used to define the market — such as a Nifty 50 index fund — will have a beta very close to 1 by construction, minus small tracking error and expense ratio drag. A fund tracking a different index, like a small-cap benchmark, can have a beta well above or below 1 versus the Nifty.
