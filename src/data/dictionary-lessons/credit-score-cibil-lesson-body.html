Credit Score (CIBIL)

Credit Score (CIBIL) is a three-digit number between 300 and 900 that summarises how reliably you have repaid past loans and credit cards, and lenders use it to decide whether to approve you and at what interest rate. A higher CIBIL score signals lower risk, which typically means faster approvals and cheaper borrowing.

What is a Credit Score (CIBIL)?

A Credit Score (CIBIL) is a single number, ranging from 300 to 900, that captures your repayment track record. It is calculated by credit bureaus from the data banks and NBFCs report about you. In India the most quoted score comes from TransUnion CIBIL, which is why people say "CIBIL score" the way they say "Xerox" for a photocopy. Three other RBI-licensed bureaus — Experian, Equifax and CRIF High Mark — produce their own scores too.

Think of Ravi, a Pune software engineer. He pays every credit-card bill on time and never misses an EMI. His CIBIL score is 805, so a bank sanctions his car loan in a day at a low rate. His colleague Anil, who has skipped a few card payments, scores 640 and is either rejected or offered a much higher rate. Same salary, very different borrowing cost — that is the power of the number.

A score of 750 or above is generally treated as "good" by most Indian lenders. Below 650, approvals get harder and pricing gets punishing.

  • H = Payment history — whether you paid every EMI and card bill on time. A single 30-day delay can drag the score down sharply.
  • U = Credit utilisation — how much of your available limit you actually use.
  • M = Credit mix & length — a healthy blend of secured loans (home, car) and unsecured credit (cards, personal loans), and how long you have held them.
  • N = New credit / enquiries — too many loan applications in a short window looks desperate and lowers the score.
  • E = Exposure / outstanding debt — the total balance you carry.

Worked example

Two friends, Priya (CIBIL 805) and Manoj (CIBIL 650), each take a ₹50,00,000 home loan for 20 years (240 months). Because of her score, Priya is offered 8.5% per annum; Manoj, seen as riskier, is offered 9.75%. How much does the 155-point gap actually cost?

We use the standard EMI formula, where P is the principal, r is the monthly rate (annual rate ÷ 12), and n is the number of months:

Priya at 8.5%: monthly rate r = 0.085 ÷ 12 = 0.0070833. With n = 240, (1+r)240 ≈ 5.4412. The EMI works out to about ₹43,391.

Manoj at 9.75%: monthly rate r = 0.0975 ÷ 12 = 0.008125. With n = 240, (1+r)240 ≈ 6.9735. The EMI works out to about ₹47,426.

So what? A poorer score does not just risk rejection; it silently taxes you for two decades. That ₹9.7 lakh, if instead invested through a SIP in an equity mutual fund, could have compounded into a serious corpus. Guarding your CIBIL score is one of the highest-return, lowest-effort money moves an Indian borrower can make.

How to build and protect your score

The score rewards boring consistency. Pay every EMI and card bill on or before the due date, keep CUR under 30%, avoid applying for multiple loans at once, and never close your oldest card — length of history helps. Check your CIBIL report free once a year and dispute any error, because a wrongly reported default can cost you dearly. Unlike returns from equities, which swing with market volatility, your credit score moves slowly and predictably, so small good habits pay off with certainty.

Check yourself
Your credit cards have a combined limit of ₹2,00,000 and your total outstanding is ₹80,000. What is your credit utilisation ratio, and is it in the healthy zone?
CUR = (80,000 ÷ 2,00,000) × 100 = 40%. That is above the recommended 30% threshold, so it is mildly hurting your score. Paying the balance down to ₹60,000 or below would bring you into the healthy zone.

Key takeaways

  • A CIBIL score runs from 300 to 900; 750+ is treated as good by most Indian lenders.
  • Payment history and credit utilisation together drive over half the score.
  • Keep utilisation below 30% and never miss an EMI or card due date.
  • A lower score means a higher interest rate — potentially lakhs of rupees extra over a home loan.
  • Four RBI-licensed bureaus exist; CIBIL is the most cited, but check any errors on all reports.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good CIBIL score in India?

A score of 750 or above is considered good and unlocks the best rates. Anything above 800 is excellent, while below 650 makes approvals difficult. Each lender sets its own cut-off, so the exact threshold can vary a little.

Does checking my own score reduce it?

No. Checking your own report is a soft enquiry and has zero impact on your score. Only hard enquiries — when a lender pulls your report after a loan or card application — can temporarily lower it. You are entitled to one free report per bureau every year.

How long does it take to improve a CIBIL score?

There is no overnight fix. With disciplined, on-time payments and low utilisation, most people see meaningful improvement over 6 to 12 months. A serious default can weigh on the score for years, though its impact fades as fresh good behaviour builds up.

Can I get a loan with a low CIBIL score?

Sometimes, but on worse terms. Options include adding a co-applicant, offering collateral such as a fixed deposit, or taking a secured credit card. Expect a higher interest rate to compensate the lender for the added risk.

Why is my score different across bureaus?

Because each of the four bureaus — CIBIL, Experian, Equifax and CRIF High Mark — uses its own model and may receive data on slightly different dates. A gap of 20 to 40 points between bureaus is normal and not a cause for worry.

Does having no loans mean a high score?

No. With no borrowing history, the bureau shows NA / NH / -1, meaning it cannot score you yet. Someone who only keeps a recurring deposit and never borrows is "new to credit". Responsibly using one card and paying it in full is how you build a score from scratch.
