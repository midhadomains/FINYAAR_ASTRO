ETF

ETF (Exchange Traded Fund) is a pooled investment fund that holds a basket of securities — usually tracking an index like the Nifty 50 — and trades on a stock exchange throughout the day like an ordinary share. In India, an ETF combines the low cost and diversification of an index fund with the real-time, intraday tradability of an equity share.

What is an ETF?

An ETF, or Exchange Traded Fund, is a basket of securities that you can buy and sell on the NSE or BSE exactly like a single stock. Instead of picking individual companies, one ETF unit gives you a slice of an entire index or theme — say all 50 Nifty companies at once. Think of it as a ready-made thali: order one plate and you get a balanced spread rather than cooking each dish yourself.

A practical Indian example: the Nippon India ETF Nifty 50 BeES mirrors the Nifty 50. If the Nifty rises 1% on a given day, the ETF's price also moves roughly 1%, and you can trade it live during market hours (9:15 am to 3:30 pm) through your Demat and trading account. Because it is passively managed, it charges a fraction of what an actively managed mutual fund does.

ETFs sit at the intersection of two worlds: they are structured as mutual fund schemes and regulated by SEBI, but they behave like listed shares — with a live market price, a bid-ask spread, and settlement into your Demat account.

How an ETF works

Every ETF tracks a benchmark. The fund house (AMC) buys and holds the underlying securities in the same weights as the index, so the ETF's value rises and falls with that index. Two prices matter here:

1. NAV / iNAV. The fund's Net Asset Value per unit is the true value of its holdings. During the trading day, exchanges publish a live estimate called the iNAV (indicative NAV).

Here Total Assets is the market value of all shares the ETF holds, Liabilities are fees and payables owed by the fund, and Units Outstanding is the number of ETF units held by all investors.

2. Market price. The price at which the ETF actually trades on the exchange, set by supply and demand. It can sit slightly above (premium) or below (discount) the iNAV.

Large investors called Authorised Participants keep the two prices tied together through a "creation and redemption" mechanism: if the ETF trades too expensive, they create new units and sell them, pushing the price back down toward NAV; if it is too cheap, they buy and redeem. This arbitrage is why a good ETF rarely strays far from its NAV.

ETF vs Index Fund vs Active Fund

An ETF and an index fund both track an index passively, but the ETF trades intraday on the exchange and needs a Demat account, while an index fund is bought at day-end NAV directly from the AMC and supports a SIP without a broker. Both have lower fees than a typical active fund, which relies on a manager trying to beat the market.

Worked example

Riya, a 30-year-old from Pune, wants to invest a ₹5,00,000 bonus in the Nifty 50 for 10 years. She compares three routes that carry different annual costs: a Nifty 50 ETF (expense ratio 0.05%), a direct index fund (0.20%), and an active large-cap mutual fund (1.50%). Assume each route delivers a gross 12% per year before costs for this illustration. What does each route grow to, and how much does cost alone decide her outcome?

Step 1 — Find the net return. Net return = gross return − expense ratio.

  • ETF: 12.00% − 0.05% = 11.95%
  • Index fund: 12.00% − 0.20% = 11.80%
  • Active fund: 12.00% − 1.50% = 10.50%

Step 2 — Compound the ₹5,00,000 for 10 years using future value = P × (1 + r)10. This is the power of compound interest working on the whole corpus.

So what? Riya never had to predict a single winning stock. A 1.45% difference in annual cost quietly compounded into nearly two lakh rupees. For a large lump sum held long term, the ETF's lower ongoing fee can help; for small monthly amounts, an index fund SIP is often simpler.

Check yourself
An ETF's live market price is ₹252 while its iNAV is ₹250. Is it trading at a premium or a discount, and by how much?
It is trading at a premium. Premium/Discount = (252 − 250) / 250 × 100 = +0.8%. The market price is 0.8% above the fund's underlying value, so you are paying a small margin over iNAV — normally arbitrage by Authorised Participants keeps this gap small.

Key takeaways

  • An ETF is a basket of securities that tracks an index and trades on the NSE/BSE like a share, needing a Demat account.
  • ETFs are passively managed, so their expense ratios can be far below active funds — and that cost gap compounds heavily over time.
  • Two prices matter: the fund's NAV/iNAV and its live market price; arbitrage keeps them close.
  • Tracking error measures how faithfully an ETF mirrors its index; lower is better.
  • India offers equity, sectoral, gold, debt, and international ETFs — all regulated by SEBI.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a Demat account to buy an ETF in India?

Yes. Because ETFs trade on the stock exchange like shares, you need both a Demat and a trading account with a broker. This is the key difference from an index fund, which can be bought directly from the AMC with just a bank account and KYC.

Are ETFs safer than stocks?

An ETF spreads your money across many companies, so a single company crashing hurts far less than if you held only that stock — that is the benefit of diversification. However, an equity ETF still falls when the whole market falls, so it is not risk-free. It reduces company-specific risk, not market risk.

Can I do a SIP in an ETF?

Some brokers offer ETF SIP features, but ETFs trade at live market prices in whole units, so fixed-rupee monthly buys can be less straightforward than a mutual-fund SIP. For disciplined monthly investing, an index fund SIP may be simpler; ETFs can suit lump sums or investors who prefer exchange trading.

How are ETFs taxed in India?

Tax treatment depends on the ETF's underlying assets and the applicable holding period. Equity-oriented, gold, debt, and international ETFs may be taxed differently, and tax rules can change. Verify the current rules with the Income Tax Department or a qualified tax adviser.

What is the difference between an ETF and a mutual fund?

A mutual fund is bought and sold once a day at its closing NAV directly from the fund house. An ETF trades continuously on the exchange at a live price and needs a Demat account. ETFs are also usually cheaper because most are passive index trackers.

Do ETFs pay dividends?

When underlying companies pay dividends, the ETF receives them. Depending on the scheme's option and distribution policy, that income may be retained in the fund or distributed to investors. Check the scheme documents before investing.
