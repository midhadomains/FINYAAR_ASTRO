Cash Flow Statement

Cash Flow Statement is a financial statement that tracks the actual cash moving into and out of a business over a period, sorted into operating, investing and financing activities, so that a reader can see how a company generated and used real money rather than accounting profit. It answers a simple question that a profit figure alone cannot: did the business actually collect and hold more cash, or less?

What a cash flow statement really shows

A company can report a healthy net profit and still run out of cash. That gap is exactly what the cash flow statement exposes. While the profit and loss account is built on the accrual principle — recognising revenue when the applicable recognition criteria are met, which need not coincide with invoicing or collection — the cash flow statement strips all of that away and follows the bank balance. For an Indian investor reading an annual report, it is a useful check on profit, but cash-flow timing and classification also require scrutiny.

Indian companies preparing a cash flow statement use Ind AS 7 or AS 3 according to their applicable reporting framework; scope and exemptions depend on the entity, not simply its size. It covers a reporting period and reconciles opening cash and cash equivalents to the closing balance. Cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments meeting the applicable accounting criteria. If a company held ₹10 crore at the start of the year and ₹14 crore at the end, the statement explains, activity by activity, where that extra ₹4 crore came from.

The three sections

Every cash flow statement splits movements into three buckets, and the split tells a story on its own.

Operating activities capture cash from the core business — money received from customers, minus cash paid to suppliers, employees, and for taxes. This is the engine. Strong operating cash helps fund a business, but may not cover all investment needs and financing obligations. Investing activities cover cash spent on or received from long-term assets: buying machinery, a factory, or shares, and selling them. Heavy negative investing cash often signals a company building capacity. Financing activities track cash between the firm and its funders: money raised from issuing shares or taking loans, and money paid out as loan repayments or dividends. Interest paid is financing for a non-financial entity under Indian standards, while financial institutions classify it as operating.

Here CFO, CFI and CFF are the net cash flows from operating, investing and financing activities. Their sum is the net cash flow from the three activities. The simplified formula assumes no exchange-rate effects on cash and cash equivalents; those effects are shown separately when reconciling opening and closing balances. The related view sits on the balance sheet, where the closing cash figure finally lands.

Why profit differs from cash

Profit and cash flow measure different things; both require careful reading. Several items create the gap. Depreciation reduces reported profit without a cash payment for the depreciation charge itself, so it is added back under the indirect method. The asset purchase may still involve a cash payment in that same year. A sale made on credit lifts profit immediately, yet the cash arrives only when the customer pays — and until then it sits in receivables. Inventory purchased for cash ties up cash before its cost is recognised in profit on sale, unless a write-down or other expense is required. This is why a fast-growing firm can be profitable on paper and still short of cash: it is funding rising receivables and stock.

The table below shows a simplified reconciliation, assuming no other non-cash items, tax timing differences or investing and financing adjustments.

Net profit5,00,000
Add: Depreciation1,20,000
Less: Increase in receivables(2,00,000)
Add: Increase in payables80,000
Cash from operations5,00,000

The profit of ₹5,00,000 happens to equal operating cash here, but the mix is different — and in a bad year those adjustments can turn a profit into negative cash.

Direct versus indirect method, and spotting trouble

There are two ways to build the operating section. The direct method lists actual cash receipts and payments — cash from customers, cash to suppliers — like a bank statement. The indirect method starts with net profit and adjusts for non-cash items and working capital changes, as in the table above. The indirect method makes the reconciliation with profit visible alongside the income statement.

For spotting trouble, watch the relationship between profit and operating cash across several years. Profit rising while operating cash stagnates or falls is a classic warning — it can mean sales are being booked but not collected, or inventory is piling up. A firm that repeatedly funds dividends and interest by raising fresh loans, visible in the financing section, is another red flag. Investigate the causes and accounting policies rather than treating any one pattern as proof of poor reporting.

Worked example
In this hypothetical example, Ananya Iyer manages Sahyadri Textiles Private Limited, a garment manufacturer in Pune. Assume no adjustments beyond those listed, no exchange-rate effects and no difference between tax expense and tax paid. For the year ended 31 March 2026 the firm reported a net profit of ₹8,00,000, yet cash increased by less than reported profit. She wants to build a cash flow statement to understand why. During the year: depreciation was ₹2,50,000; receivables rose by ₹6,00,000 as she extended credit to a large buyer; inventory rose by ₹3,00,000; payables rose by ₹1,50,000. She bought a new stitching machine for ₹4,00,000, raised a bank loan of ₹5,00,000, and paid a dividend of ₹2,00,000.

Step 1 — Operating activities. Start with net profit and adjust. Add back depreciation (₹2,50,000, non-cash). Subtract the rise in receivables (₹6,00,000) and inventory (₹3,00,000) because cash is tied up there. Add the rise in payables (₹1,50,000) because suppliers are effectively funding the firm.

Step 2 — Investing activities. The machine purchase is a cash outflow of ₹4,00,000.

Step 3 — Financing activities. The loan brings in ₹5,00,000; the dividend takes out ₹2,00,000, leaving a net ₹3,00,000 inflow.

ActivityAmount (₹)
Net profit8,00,000
Add: Depreciation2,50,000
Less: Increase in receivables(6,00,000)
Less: Increase in inventory(3,00,000)
Add: Increase in payables1,50,000
Cash from operations (CFO)3,00,000
Investing: buy machine (CFI)(4,00,000)
Financing: loan minus dividend (CFF)3,00,000
Net change in cash2,00,000

So operating cash of ₹3,00,000 is far below the ₹8,00,000 profit, because ₹9,00,000 was swallowed by rising receivables and inventory. Net cash rose ₹2,00,000: ₹3,00,000 minus ₹4,00,000 plus ₹3,00,000.

So what? The example shows that receivables and inventory absorbed cash, partly offset by supplier credit. Operating cash did not cover the machine purchase and dividend, so the loan helped fund the shortfall. Ananya should review collection timing, inventory needs and funding plans; these figures alone do not prove the quality of reported profit.

Check yourself
Q
A company reports rising net profit every year, but its cash from operations is negative and falling. What is the most likely explanation, and why should an investor care?
A: Sales are being booked on credit (or inventory is building) faster than cash is collected, so profit is not turning into cash. It matters because a firm can be profitable on paper yet unable to pay suppliers, staff or loans — a common path to a cash crunch.

Key takeaways

  • The cash flow statement follows real cash, not accrual profit, and reconciles the opening cash balance to the closing balance over a period.
  • It has three sections — operating (the core engine), investing (long-term assets), and financing (funders) — with any exchange-rate effect shown separately to reconcile cash and cash equivalents.
  • Profit differs from cash because of non-cash items like depreciation and because of working capital changes in receivables, inventory and payables.
  • Operating cash flow can be presented using the direct or indirect method; the indirect method reconciles profit with cash flow.
  • Watching profit against operating cash over several years is one of the sharpest ways to spot a business heading for trouble.

Frequently asked questions

Is the cash flow statement the same as a bank passbook?

No. A passbook lists every individual transaction in date order. The cash flow statement groups a period of movements into three meaningful categories — operating, investing and financing — so a reader can judge how the business earned and used cash, not just when each rupee moved.

Why is depreciation added back to profit here?

Depreciation included in profit is a non-cash charge, so it is added back in the indirect operating cash-flow reconciliation. Cash paid to acquire the asset is recorded separately, usually as investing cash flow, and may occur in the same year.

Which section should a long-term investor look at first?

Cash from operations. Strong operating cash shows cash generation from operations, but compare it with investment needs, debt repayments and other commitments before judging funding sufficiency. Weak or negative operating cash, despite good profit, deserves close scrutiny before investing.

Can a profitable Indian company still go bankrupt?

Yes. If profit is locked in unpaid receivables and unsold inventory, the firm may lack the cash to pay suppliers, salaries or loan instalments on time. Several Indian firms with reported profits have faced defaults because their cash flow, not their profit, ran dry.

What does negative investing cash flow indicate?

It usually means the company is spending cash on long-term assets — new plant, machinery or acquisitions. For a growing firm this is often healthy, showing investment in future capacity. Assess investment quality, expected returns, liquidity and financing as well; negative investing cash flow is neither automatically healthy nor automatically a warning.

Where does dividend paid appear in the statement?

Dividend paid to shareholders is a cash outflow shown under financing activities, because it is money returned to the firm's funders. Under Indian accounting standards, interest and dividends received or paid are classified consistently, and dividend paid typically sits in the financing section.
