FINYAAR DICTIONARY · RATIOS AND VALUATION

P/B Ratio

P/B Ratio (Price to Book) compares the market price of a share with the net assets the accounts say stand behind it. Divide the price of one share by its book value per share, or market capitalisation by shareholders' funds, and you learn how many rupees investors pay for each rupee of audited net worth. It carries real information for banks, finance companies and asset-heavy manufacturers, where the balance sheet holds most of what the business is, and much less for asset-light firms, whose brands, code and trained people never reach the books at all.

Price on one side, net assets on the other

Take what the market charges for one share, set it against the net assets recorded for that share, and the result is the price-to-book ratio. Consistency is the only discipline required: the same share count on both sides, and preference capital excluded, since preference holders are not the owners being measured.

Scaling up changes nothing. Market capitalisation divided by shareholders' funds gives an identical answer. Either way the price side moves continuously while the book side is restated only at each result, so the ratio drifts daily on price alone.

The ratio written out

Market price per share is the traded price on the NSE or BSE at that moment. Book value per share is total assets minus total liabilities minus preference capital, divided by the equity shares outstanding. Market capitalisation is that price multiplied by the share count. Shareholders' funds, reported as share capital plus reserves and surplus, or as equity share capital plus other equity under Ind AS, is the same net figure before it is sliced per share. Since reserves are mostly accumulated profit, a company paying out nearly everything it earns grows its book slowly.

Balance sheets that hold the business

Lending is the clearest case. A bank's assets are loans and investments, its liabilities deposits and borrowings, and the difference is a measured quantity carried net of provisions under rules the RBI enforces. Net worth there is no accounting leftover; it is the regulated cushion that absorbs loan losses and limits how much may be lent.

Provisioning is the catch. A book propped up by loans that should have been written down makes the ratio look cheaper than it is.

Balance sheets that miss the business

Accounting rules expense most of what makes an asset-light company valuable. Brand building goes through the profit and loss account as advertising, research is written off unless it clears a strict capitalisation test, and an engineering team appears nowhere, employees being nobody's asset. What remains is cash, receivables and a little equipment, so the book is small by construction and the ratio dividing by it correspondingly large.

Buybacks do the same deliberately, since retiring shares for cash reduces net worth while earning power is untouched. Goodwill pushes the other way, recording a price once paid, which is why many analysts also use tangible book.

It speaks only with return on equity beside it

A rupee of book value is worth paying more for when it earns more, and the relationship is exact: price to book equals the price-to-earnings ratio multiplied by return on equity. Two companies at the same ratio are not priced alike if one earns three times the return on its book, so read the pair together with the earnings per share behind them.

Worked example
Farida Qureshi, a physiotherapist in Hyderabad, has two unnamed stocks on her watchlist, a private bank and a mid-sized software services firm. Both trade at a price-to-book ratio of exactly 3.0. The bank's book value is ₹200 a share and its price is ₹600; it earns 15 per cent on equity. The software firm's book value is ₹50 a share and its price is ₹150; it earns 40 per cent on equity. Is she looking at two similarly priced companies?

Step 1, turn book value into earnings. Return on equity applied to book value gives earnings per share. The bank earns 15 per cent of ₹200, that is ₹30 a share. The software firm earns 40 per cent of ₹50, or ₹20 a share.

Step 2, price those earnings. The bank at ₹600 on ₹30 trades at 20 times earnings. The software firm at ₹150 on ₹20 trades at 7.5 times. Identical price-to-book, and one is priced at well under half the other on profits.

Step 3, see why. The bank's ₹200 of book is a real, regulated, provisioned quantity the business needs in order to lend. The software firm's ₹50 is mostly cash and receivables; the code, the client list and the delivery teams that produce the ₹20 were expensed as they were built and never entered the balance sheet. Dividing by that ₹50 measures almost nothing.

Step 4, the correction that matters. For the software firm to trade at the bank's 20 times earnings, its price would have to be ₹400, putting its price-to-book at 8.0. Nothing about the business would have changed, only the smallness of the base being divided into.

So what? The same 3.0 says the bank is priced at three times a meaningful asset cushion and says almost nothing about the software firm beyond how little of its worth the accounts capture. Farida should judge the bank on this ratio against its return on equity and its provisioning, and the software firm on earnings and cash.

Check yourself
Q
A lender trades at a price-to-book ratio of 0.6. Why might that not be the bargain it appears to be?
A: Because the denominator may be overstated. If loans that have stopped performing are still carried near full value, true net worth is lower and the real ratio higher. A ratio under one often says the market doubts the book, the returns on it, or both.

Key takeaways

  • Price per share over book value per share, or market capitalisation over shareholders' funds.
  • It means most where the balance sheet holds the business, as in banks and asset-heavy manufacturers.
  • It means least for asset-light firms, whose brands, research and people are expensed.
  • Price to book equals price to earnings multiplied by return on equity, so read both together.
  • A ratio below one is a question about asset quality or returns, not a verdict on value.

Frequently asked questions

What does a price-to-book ratio below one mean?

The market values the company at less than the net assets its accounts report, which points to doubts about those carrying values, or to returns below what investors require.

Why are banks valued on price to book rather than price to earnings?

A lender's earnings swing with provisioning decisions that can be deferred or accelerated, while its net worth is a regulated quantity supporting the loan book. Book value is the steadier anchor.

Is price to tangible book better?

It is stricter. Removing goodwill and intangibles leaves only assets that could be sold, which matters after large acquisitions. The resulting ratio is higher, so compare it only with itself.

How does a buyback change the ratio?

Buying shares back uses cash, so assets and net worth both fall. Done above book value, it lowers book value per share too, lifting the ratio with no change in the business.

Can the ratio be negative?

Arithmetically yes, once accumulated losses have wiped out reserves, but the result is useless as a valuation measure. Treat negative net worth as a solvency signal.

Should I use standalone or consolidated book value?

Use consolidated figures where subsidiaries matter, because the share price reflects the whole group. Mixing consolidated market capitalisation with standalone net worth describes nothing.
