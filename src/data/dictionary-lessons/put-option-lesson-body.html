Put Option

Put Option is a contract that gives its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to sell a fixed quantity of an underlying asset at a pre-agreed price, called the strike, on or before a set expiry date, in return for an upfront premium. The buyer profits when the price of the underlying falls well below the strike, because the right to sell high while the market trades low becomes valuable. The most a buyer can lose is the premium paid.

What a put option is

A put option is one of the two basic building blocks of the options market, the other being the call option. Buying a put can express a bearish view or protect an existing holding against a fall in the price of the underlying — an equity share, an index, or a commodity — will fall. The buyer pays a premium today and receives the right to sell the underlying at the fixed strike price (K) no matter how far the market drops. If the underlying is at or above the strike at expiry, the put expires worthless and the option buyer loses the premium, before costs. NSE stock options are European-style and physically settled: an in-the-money put held to expiry requires delivery of shares. Check delivery requirements before expiry. This right-without-obligation is what separates an option from a firm commitment such as a futures contract.

Buyer payoff and break-even

At expiry the value of a put to its buyer depends only on where the underlying price (S) sits relative to the strike (K). If S is below K, the put pays K minus S per unit; if S is at or above K, it pays nothing. Subtracting the premium gives the buyer profit per unit before taxes and transaction costs. Multiply by the number of units to obtain total profit. The buyer only turns an overall profit once the price falls below the break-even, which equals the strike minus the premium.

Using a put to hedge: the protective put

The most common defensive use is the protective put. An investor who already owns shares and fears a near-term fall buys a put on the same shares. If the price crashes, the loss on the shares is offset by the gain on the put, so the put behaves like an insurance policy with the premium as its cost. This is a core hedging tool, and it links directly to broader ideas of risk control covered in the derivatives and risk cluster. The trade-off is real: the premium is a sunk cost that drags on returns in every period when the feared fall does not happen.

Buyer versus seller risk

The buyer and the seller (the writer) sit on opposite sides. The standalone option payoff caps the buyer loss at the premium before costs; physical delivery obligations must also be met. For a share whose price cannot fall below zero, the maximum buyer profit per share is the strike minus the premium. The seller collects the premium upfront but takes on the mirror-image risk: a large loss if the price falls far, and only the premium as reward. Because that loss can be heavy, the exchange forces the seller to post margin, which rises as risk grows. The premium itself is heavily influenced by volatility: the more the market expects the price to swing, the dearer the put. SEBI studies show that most individual futures and options traders lose money, so both sides deserve caution.

Worked example
In this hypothetical example, ignoring taxes and transaction costs and assuming available option lots cover exactly 500 shares, Anaya holds 500 shares of an Indian company now trading at ₹1,000, a holding worth ₹5,00,000. She is nervous about results season and buys a protective put with a strike of ₹980, paying a premium of ₹30 per share. What happens to her position at several possible expiry prices?

Step 1 — Set out the cost of protection. Anaya buys puts on all 500 shares. The premium is ₹30 per share, so she pays ₹30 × 500 = ₹15,000 upfront. This is the fixed cost of her insurance, and it is gone whether or not she ever uses the put.

Step 2 — Work out the put payoff formula. Per share, the put pays max(K − S, 0), where K = ₹980. Her break-even on the option alone is strike minus premium = ₹980 − ₹30 = ₹950. Below ₹950 the put more than repays its cost.

Step 3 — Value the combined position at each expiry price. The combined value equals the value of the 500 shares plus the put payoff, minus the ₹15,000 premium. This is an economic valuation: physical settlement of an in-the-money stock put delivers the shares at the strike, rather than retaining the shares and receiving a separate cash payoff.

Once the price falls below ₹980, the put steps in: at ₹900 and at ₹800 her net position holds firm near ₹4,75,000, even though the raw shares have fallen much further. The put has put a floor under her holding.

So what? At expiry, the protective put caps Anaya's loss relative to the current ₹5,00,000 holding value at ₹25,000 (the ₹10,000 fall to the strike plus the ₹15,000 premium) no matter how deep the crash goes. She trades a small, certain cost for protection against a large, uncertain loss — exactly the insurance logic of buying a put.

Check yourself
Q
A put has a strike of ₹500 and cost a premium of ₹20. At what expiry price does the buyer break even, and what is the buyer maximum loss?
A: Break-even is strike minus premium = ₹500 − ₹20 = ₹480. The buyer maximum loss is the premium of ₹20 per share, suffered whenever the price stays at or above the ₹500 strike.

Key takeaways

  • A put option gives the buyer the right, not the obligation, to sell the underlying at the strike price for an upfront premium.
  • Buyer profit equals max(K − S, 0) minus the premium; break-even is strike minus premium.
  • The buyer maximum loss is capped at the premium, while the gain grows as the price falls.
  • A protective put insures an existing holding, trading a known premium cost for a floor under losses.
  • The seller collects the premium but faces large losses and must post margin; SEBI data show most retail F&O traders lose money.

Frequently asked questions

Is buying a put the same as short selling?

Both can profit from a falling price. The standalone put payoff limits loss to the premium before costs, while a short sale can lose without limit as the price rises. A put expires, and physically settled stock puts held in the money at expiry require delivery of shares.

What happens if the price never falls below the strike?

If the underlying is at or above the strike at expiry, the put expires worthless and the buyer loses the premium, plus transaction costs. It is the expiry price that determines the final payoff, even if the price crossed the strike earlier.

Who should consider a protective put?

An investor who owns an asset, wants to keep it, but fears a short-term fall. The put acts as insurance, and like insurance it costs a premium in every period.

Why is the seller required to post margin?

Because the seller carries a large potential loss if the price falls sharply. Margin is collateral held by the exchange to ensure the seller can meet that obligation.

What makes a put more expensive?

Other things equal, higher implied volatility, a higher strike and a lower underlying price increase a put premium. More time often adds value, but this is not an unconditional rule for European puts; interest rates and dividends also affect pricing.

Can I sell a put before expiry?

Yes. A bought exchange-traded put can generally be sold during market hours before expiry, provided there is a willing buyer. Liquidity and bid-ask spreads affect the price and whether an order executes. Closing an existing long put is different from writing a new short put.
