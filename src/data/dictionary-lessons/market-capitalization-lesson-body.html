Market Capitalization

Market Capitalization is the total rupee value of a listed company's outstanding shares, calculated by multiplying the current share price by the number of shares in issue. It is the market's live estimate of what the whole company is worth, and in India it decides whether a stock is classified as large-cap, mid-cap, or small-cap.

What is Market Capitalization?

Market Capitalization (often shortened to "market cap") is simply the price of one share multiplied by the total number of shares a company has issued. If a company's shares trade at ₹500 and it has 10 crore shares, its market cap is ₹5,000 crore. That single number tells you the size of the business as the market values it right now, not its sales, profit, or assets.

Think of a well-known name like Reliance or TCS. Their share prices look ordinary on screen, but because they have hundreds of crores of shares outstanding, their market caps run into lakhs of crores of rupees. A smaller company can have a higher share price yet a far smaller market cap simply because it has fewer shares. So price alone never tells you company size, market cap does.

Every equity share you buy is a slice of this total value. Market cap is the yardstick behind the indices you hear about daily, Sensex and Nifty, and it shapes how fund managers, index providers, and SEBI itself categorise stocks.

Large-cap, mid-cap and small-cap

SEBI classifies listed companies by their rank in full market capitalization, not by fixed rupee cut-offs. The top 100 companies are large-cap, the next 150 (ranks 101 to 250) are mid-cap, and everything from rank 251 onward is small-cap. AMFI publishes this list every six months, and mutual fund schemes must follow it.

These thresholds drift up over time as the whole market rises. A rising tide can push a company from small-cap into mid-cap without the business changing much, purely because its share price and market cap climbed.

Why market cap matters

Market cap is not just a label. It drives index weights: in a cap-weighted index fund or ETF, the biggest companies get the biggest share of your money. It is also the base for valuation ratios. Market cap divided by annual profit gives a whole-company version of the P/E ratio, and market cap sits behind per-share metrics like EPS.

It signals risk too. Larger caps tend to have lower beta and steadier dividends, while smaller caps swing harder. Building a portfolio across cap sizes is a core form of diversification.

Example

Worked example

Bharat Cements Ltd is listed on the NSE. It has 12 crore shares outstanding and its share price is ₹850. Promoters hold 55% of the company; the remaining 45% is public free-float. Calculate its full market cap and free-float market cap, and work out what happens to both if the share price rises to ₹1,000 after a strong results season.

Step 1 — Full market cap at ₹850. Using Market Cap = P × N:

12 crore × ₹850 = ₹10,200 crore. That places Bharat Cements comfortably in mid-cap territory (below the ~₹85,000 crore large-cap zone, above the small-cap line).

Step 2 — Free-float factor. Promoters hold 55%, so public free-float is 100% − 55% = 45%, giving a free-float factor of 0.45.

Step 3 — Free-float market cap. Free-Float Market Cap = P × N × f = ₹10,200 crore × 0.45 = ₹4,590 crore. This smaller number, not the full ₹10,200 crore, is what index providers use to decide Bharat Cements' weight in an index.

Step 4 — After the price rises to ₹1,000. Shares outstanding do not change (no new shares were issued). New full market cap = 12 crore × ₹1,000 = ₹12,000 crore. New free-float market cap = ₹12,000 crore × 0.45 = ₹5,400 crore.

MetricAt ₹850At ₹1,000Change
Share price (P)₹850₹1,000+17.6%
Shares outstanding (N)12 crore12 crore0%
Full market cap₹10,200 crore₹12,000 crore+₹1,800 crore
Free-float market cap₹4,590 crore₹5,400 crore+₹810 crore

So what? A 17.6% jump in the share price lifts market cap by exactly 17.6% (₹10,200 to ₹12,000 crore), because the share count is fixed. Market cap moves one-for-one with price when no new shares are issued. Notice the free-float value stays far below the full value throughout, so Bharat Cements' pull on an index is much smaller than its headline size suggests. And if this rally pushes the company past rank 100, it could be reclassified as large-cap at the next AMFI review, changing which funds are allowed to hold it. That is why traders watch market cap, not just the price ticker.

Check yourself
Company A trades at ₹2,000 with 5 crore shares. Company B trades at ₹100 with 200 crore shares. Which is bigger by market cap?

Company A's market cap is ₹2,000 × 5 crore = ₹10,000 crore. Company B's is ₹100 × 200 crore = ₹20,000 crore. Despite its far lower share price, Company B is twice as big. This is exactly why share price alone never tells you company size, only market cap does.

Key takeaways

  • Market cap = current share price × total shares outstanding. It is a live figure that moves with price.
  • A high share price does not mean a big company; only market cap reveals true size.
  • SEBI ranks companies: top 100 are large-cap, 101 to 250 mid-cap, 251+ small-cap, reviewed by AMFI every six months.
  • Indian indices like Nifty and Sensex use free-float market cap, which excludes promoter and locked-in shares.
  • Market cap drives index weights and underpins valuation measures like the whole-company P/E ratio.
  • Spreading investments across large, mid and small caps is a basic form of diversification.

Frequently asked questions

Is a higher market cap always better?

No. Large-cap companies are generally more stable and liquid, but they often grow slowly. Mid-cap and small-cap companies carry more risk and volatility yet can deliver faster growth. The "better" choice depends entirely on your goals, time horizon and risk appetite, not on size alone.

What is the difference between market cap and enterprise value?

Market cap values only the equity, the shares. Enterprise value adds the company's debt and subtracts its cash, so it reflects the total cost of buying the whole business. Enterprise value is often compared against EBITDA for valuation, while market cap is compared against net profit.

Does market cap change when a company pays a dividend?

Indirectly, yes. On the ex-dividend date the share price usually drops by roughly the dividend amount, which nudges market cap down. The share count is unchanged, so the fall comes purely through price. Over time, the effect is small compared with normal price swings.

How does an IPO affect market cap?

When a company completes an IPO, its market cap is set for the first time by the issue price multiplied by total shares. On listing day, the price can open above or below the issue price, immediately re-rating the company's market cap higher or lower than the IPO valuation.

Why do indices use free-float market cap instead of full market cap?

Free-float counts only shares that can actually be bought and sold by the public. Promoter and government holdings rarely trade, so including them would overstate a company's real influence on the market. Free-float weighting makes indices like the Nifty 50 reflect tradable value more fairly.

Can market cap go up without the company earning more profit?

Absolutely. Market cap tracks the share price, which reflects investor expectations, not just current profit. Optimism, sector rerating, or falling inflation and interest rates can all lift prices, and therefore market cap, even before earnings actually improve.
