EBITDA

EBITDA is a company's Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization, a measure of the core operating profit a business generates before financing choices, tax rules, and non-cash accounting charges are stripped in. It is widely used to compare the underlying earning power of companies across different capital structures and industries.

What is EBITDA?

EBITDA tells you how much profit a company makes purely from running its business, before you subtract interest on loans, taxes to the government, and two non-cash charges called depreciation and amortization. In plain words, it isolates the profit engine of the operations from how the company is financed and how it accounts for its assets.

Think of a Kirana-turned-FMCG maker in Pune. Its factory earns money by selling products, but the promoter also took a big bank loan for machinery, and the tax officer takes a cut of the final profit. EBITDA answers a simpler question: "Ignoring the loan and the tax, how much did the actual business earn?" That is why analysts, banks, and buyers of businesses lean on it heavily.

Because it removes financing and tax effects, EBITDA lets you compare a debt-heavy infrastructure firm with a debt-free IT services firm on a like-for-like operating basis. It is not a substitute for net profit or the metrics behind EPS (Earnings Per Share), but it is a cleaner lens on operations.

How EBITDA is calculated

There are two standard routes to the same number. The top-down method starts from operating profit; the bottom-up method starts from net profit (PAT) and adds back the items EBITDA ignores.

Reading each piece:

  • Net Profit (PAT) is the bottom line after everything has been deducted.
  • Interest is the cost of borrowed money; adding it back removes the effect of how much debt the firm carries.
  • Taxes are corporate income tax; adding it back neutralises differing tax positions.
  • Depreciation spreads the cost of tangible assets (plant, machinery) over their life; it is a non-cash charge.
  • Amortization does the same for intangibles (patents, software, goodwill).

EBITDA vs other profit measures

EBITDA sits at the top of a profit "waterfall". Each measure below it subtracts one more layer, moving from pure operations toward the shareholder's actual return, which is what ultimately funds a dividend or gets reinvested into an equity share base.

MeasureWhat it excludesBest used for
EBITDAInterest, tax, depreciation, amortizationComparing core operations across firms
EBIT (Operating profit)Interest, tax onlyOperating profit after asset wear-and-tear
EBT (Profit before tax)Tax onlyPre-tax return to all capital providers
PAT (Net profit)Nothing furtherFinal profit to shareholders; feeds EPS

A key caution: EBITDA ignores capital expenditure and working capital. A firm can post glowing EBITDA yet bleed cash if it constantly buys new machinery. Warren Buffett famously distrusts EBITDA for exactly this reason, since depreciation reflects a real, recurring cost of staying in business.

Worked example
A mid-cap manufacturer, "Deccan Industries Ltd", reports the following for FY26. Revenue is ₹1,000 crore, operating expenses excluding depreciation and amortization are ₹800 crore, depreciation and amortization is ₹50 crore, interest on its term loan is ₹30 crore, and it pays corporate tax at 25%. Compute EBITDA, the EBITDA margin, and PAT, then explain what the numbers say.

Step 1 - Build the P&L top-down. Start from revenue and subtract operating costs to reach EBITDA.

Line itemAmount (₹ crore)
Operating Revenue1,000
Less: Operating expenses (ex D&A)(800)
EBITDA200
Less: Depreciation & Amortization(50)
EBIT (Operating profit)150
Less: Interest(30)
EBT (Profit before tax)120
Less: Tax @ 25% of 120(30)
PAT (Net profit)90

Step 2 - Confirm EBITDA bottom-up. Using the add-back formula: PAT ₹90 cr + Interest ₹30 cr + Tax ₹30 cr + D&A ₹50 cr = ₹200 crore. Both methods agree, which is a good self-check.

Step 3 - EBITDA margin. ₹200 cr ÷ ₹1,000 cr × 100 = 20%. For an Indian manufacturer, a 20% EBITDA margin is healthy and broadly comparable to established consumer and industrial names on the Nifty.

Step 4 - So what does this mean? The business itself earns ₹200 crore of operating profit for every ₹1,000 crore of sales. But notice the gap between EBITDA (₹200 cr) and PAT (₹90 cr): depreciation, interest, and tax together consume ₹110 crore, more than half the operating profit. The ₹30 crore interest bill shows this company is meaningfully leveraged, and the ₹50 crore depreciation warns that it runs an asset-heavy factory that will need reinvestment. If you only looked at EBITDA, Deccan would look twice as profitable as it truly is for shareholders. This is exactly why lenders quote a Net Debt/EBITDA ratio (a firm with ₹600 cr net debt here would be at 3.0x, on the higher side) while equity investors still care about PAT and cash flow. EBITDA is the starting point of the analysis, not the finish line.

Check yourself
A company has PAT of ₹40 crore, interest of ₹10 crore, tax of ₹15 crore, and depreciation of ₹35 crore. What is its EBITDA?
EBITDA = 40 + 10 + 15 + 35 = ₹100 crore. Just add the four excluded items back to net profit.

Why investors and analysts use EBITDA

EBITDA is popular for three practical reasons. First, it enables apples-to-apples comparison between firms with different debt levels and tax jurisdictions, aiding diversification decisions across sectors. Second, it acts as a rough proxy for operating cash flow, useful when screening acquisition targets or a fresh IPO. Third, valuation multiples such as EV/EBITDA are a mainstay alongside the P/E Ratio for judging whether a stock is cheap or expensive.

That said, remember EBITDA is a non-GAAP measure: SEBI and Ind-AS do not mandate a single definition, so management can flatter it by labelling costs as "one-off". Always read the reconciliation. A firm with high but volatile EBITDA can still expose you to real earnings volatility, and understanding that trade-off is part of thinking about risk vs return before you invest.

Key takeaways

  • EBITDA = Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization, a measure of core operating profit.
  • Compute it bottom-up as PAT + interest + taxes + depreciation + amortization, or top-down as revenue minus operating expenses excluding D&A.
  • EBITDA margin = EBITDA ÷ revenue; 20% is healthy for an Indian manufacturer.
  • It ignores capex, working capital, interest, and tax, so it overstates cash available to shareholders.
  • Use EV/EBITDA and Net Debt/EBITDA for valuation and leverage, but always cross-check with PAT and cash flow.

Frequently asked questions

Is a higher EBITDA always better?

Generally a higher EBITDA and EBITDA margin signal stronger operations, but not always. A company can inflate EBITDA by taking on cheap debt to expand or by excluding "exceptional" items. Compare it against capex, interest cost, and actual cash flow before concluding the business is genuinely more profitable.

What is a good EBITDA margin in India?

It varies sharply by sector. IT services and FMCG firms often post 20-30% EBITDA margins, while low-margin trading or construction businesses may sit at 5-10%. Always compare a company only against peers in the same industry, not across sectors.

How is EBITDA different from cash flow?

EBITDA adds back non-cash charges but ignores changes in working capital, actual tax paid, and capital expenditure. A firm can show strong EBITDA yet generate weak or negative operating cash flow if inventories, receivables, or capex are rising fast. Treat EBITDA as a proxy, never a replacement, for cash flow.

What does EV/EBITDA tell me?

EV/EBITDA compares Enterprise Value (market cap plus net debt) to EBITDA. It is a valuation multiple like the P/E ratio but unaffected by capital structure, so it is useful for comparing debt-heavy companies. A lower multiple can suggest a cheaper stock, all else equal.

Is EBITDA reported in Indian financial statements?

EBITDA is a non-GAAP figure not mandated by Ind-AS, so it may not appear as a labelled line in audited statements. Most Indian listed companies disclose it in investor presentations and press releases. You can always compute it yourself from the profit and loss statement using the add-back formula.

Why do lenders use Net Debt/EBITDA?

Banks use Net Debt/EBITDA to gauge how many years of operating profit it would take to repay borrowings. A ratio below 2x is comfortable, while above 3-4x signals higher leverage risk. Rising inflation and interest rates make this ratio matter even more for debt-heavy firms.
