Options

Options are contracts that give the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a fixed strike price on or before a set expiry date, in return for an upfront payment called the premium. A call option is the right to buy; a put option is the right to sell. The buyer risks only the premium paid, while the seller collects that premium but accepts far larger risk. In India, index and stock options trade on the NSE, and options trading is often described as the most misunderstood corner of the market.

What an option is

An option is a type of derivative, which means its value is derived from something else — an underlying asset such as an equity share, an index, or a commodity. Buying an option is not the same as buying the share itself. Instead, you are buying a right attached to that share for a limited time.

Every option carries three defining details. The strike price is the fixed price at which the contract can be exercised. The expiry is the last date on which the right is valid. The premium is the price the buyer pays the seller to obtain the contract. NSE stock options are European-style and physically settled at expiry; settlement rules depend on the contract. Options follow the risk versus return logic of all derivatives: a small outlay controls a large exposure, which magnifies both gains and losses. This leverage is why options are powerful tools and also why they are dangerous in untrained hands. Options also lose value as expiry approaches, a decay that works against the buyer every single day.

Calls versus puts

There are only two basic option types, and every strategy is built from them. A call gives the right to buy the underlying at the strike price; a call buyer profits when the price rises well above the strike. A put gives the right to sell at the strike; a put buyer profits when the price falls well below the strike. The premium a buyer pays depends heavily on volatility and on how much time is left to expiry.

Buyer versus seller payoff and risk

The payoff to an option buyer at expiry is easy to state. For a call, the buyer receives the amount by which the price exceeds the strike, and nothing if the price is below the strike. The net profit subtracts the premium paid.

Here ST is the underlying price at expiry and K is the strike. The buyer's option payoff loss is capped at the premium before costs; physical delivery can create funding obligations and subsequent stock exposure. The seller, or writer, is on the opposite side. The seller collects the premium up front but must honour the contract if it is exercised. A call writer faces theoretically unlimited loss if the price keeps rising, and a put writer faces a large loss if the price collapses. This asymmetry — limited risk for buyers, large risk for sellers — is the single most important idea in options trading. The seller's position also demands margin, which the exchange adjusts as the price moves against them.

The reality of options in India

Options are marketed heavily to retail investors in India, often as a fast route to wealth. The evidence says otherwise. SEBI studies of the equity derivatives (F&O) segment have repeatedly found that the large majority of individual traders make net losses, and that these losses grow once brokerage and taxes are counted. The few who profit tend to be well capitalised and disciplined, not casual speculators chasing tips.

A sensible view is that options are best understood as tools for hedging and for learning how markets price risk, not as a shortcut to riches. A long-term investor building wealth through a mutual fund or an index fund rarely needs to trade options at all. If you do explore them, size positions so that a total loss of premium would not hurt, and never write naked options without deeply understanding the risk. Worked example
In this hypothetical example, ignoring taxes and transaction costs, Reliance is trading at ₹2,900. A trader is bullish and buys one call option with a strike of ₹3,000 expiring at month end, paying a premium of ₹40 per share. Assume a lot size of 250 shares for this illustration; check the current exchange contract specifications before trading. The table shows the economic payoff, not the delivery funding required for a physically settled stock option. What is the total premium, the break-even price, and the profit or loss if Reliance ends expiry at ₹2,900, ₹3,000, ₹3,040, and ₹3,120?

Step 1 — Total premium paid. The premium is quoted per share, so the trader pays ₹40 × 250 = ₹10,000. This is the maximum loss on the option payoff before costs; physical settlement can require additional funding.

Step 2 — Break-even price. A call buyer only starts to profit once the price clears the strike by enough to recover the premium. Break-even = strike + premium = ₹3,000 + ₹40 = ₹3,040. Below this, the trade is a net loss even if the option has some value.

Step 3 — Payoff at each price. Using profit per share = max(ST − 3,000, 0) − 40, then multiplying by the 250-share lot:

So what? Notice the shape. At ₹2,900 and even at ₹3,000, the whole ₹10,000 premium is gone — the option expires worthless because the price never crossed the strike. The trader needed the price to reach ₹3,040 just to escape a loss, and a solid ₹20,000 gain required a move all the way to ₹3,120, more than 7 percent in a few weeks. This is the leverage trap: the payoff at ₹3,120 looks glamorous, but two of the four outcomes here lose the entire premium, one breaks even, and one makes a profit; these illustrative scenarios do not indicate probabilities. The stock did not even have to fall for the trade to fail; it merely had to not rise enough, fast enough. The example illustrates why a correct directional view alone does not guarantee a profit.

Check yourself
Q
A trader buys a put option with a strike of ₹500 for a premium of ₹15. At what expiry price does the trader break even, and in which direction must the stock move to profit?
A: Break-even = strike − premium = ₹500 − ₹15 = ₹485. The stock must fall below ₹485 for the put buyer to make a net profit, because a put gains value as the price falls.

Key takeaways

  • An option gives the buyer a right, not an obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) at a fixed strike before expiry, in exchange for a premium.
  • The buyer's maximum loss is the premium, while the seller collects the premium but faces large, potentially unlimited risk.
  • A call buyer needs the price above strike plus premium to profit; a put buyer needs it below strike minus premium.
  • Options carry heavy leverage and lose time value daily, so the price must move enough and fast enough to win.
  • SEBI data shows most individual F&O traders lose money, so treat options as tools to understand and hedge, not to get rich.

Frequently asked questions

Is options trading gambling?

Not by definition, but it can become gambling when used without a plan. Options are legitimate risk-transfer tools. Used to hedge a holding or to express a measured view, they serve a purpose. Bought blindly on tips, they behave like lottery tickets, which is why so many retail traders lose.

Can I lose more than I invest as an option buyer?

The option payoff limits a standalone buyer's loss to the premium, before costs. However, physically settled stock options can require funds or shares at expiry. Delivery obligations, charges and any resulting stock exposure must also be considered.

What is the difference between an option and a future?

A future is a binding obligation for both parties to transact at a set price. An option gives one side a right and the other side an obligation. The option buyer can simply walk away and lose only the premium, whereas a futures position must be honoured or squared off.

Why do options expire worthless so often?

At expiry, a call is worthless when the underlying is at or below its strike; a put is worthless when it is at or above its strike. An in-the-money option has intrinsic value but can still produce a net loss if that value is less than the premium paid. Time decay reduces time value, all else equal.

Do I need a large amount to trade options in India?

Buying options can be done with modest amounts because you pay only the premium. Selling or writing options, however, requires substantial margin that the exchange revises as the market moves. The low entry cost of buying is part of what makes options tempting and risky.

Are options a good idea for a beginner?

For most beginners, no. Building wealth steadily through diversified funds and a SIP is far more reliable. If you want to learn options, start by studying them on paper and risk only money you can fully afford to lose.
