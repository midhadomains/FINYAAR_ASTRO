Capital Gains Tax

Capital Gains Tax is the tax you pay in India on the profit you make when you sell a capital asset — such as equity shares, mutual fund units, or property — for more than you paid for it. The tax applies only to the gain, not the full sale value, and the rate depends on the type of asset and how long you held it before selling. It is a central government tax charged under the head "Capital Gains" of the Income Tax Act.

What a capital gain actually is

A capital gain arises when you sell a capital asset for more than its cost of acquisition. If you buy equity shares for ₹1,00,000 and sell them later for ₹1,40,000, your capital gain is ₹40,000, and that gain — not the ₹1,40,000 — is what tax is charged on. A loss on sale is a capital loss, which you can set off against other capital gains under the rules. The key point is that the gain is only realised, and only taxed, when you actually sell. Paper profits on units you continue to hold are not taxed. Because the profit is measured in rupees today against a purchase made years ago, inflation can quietly eat into the real value of what you keep after tax.

Short-term versus long-term

The single most important question is how long you held the asset, because that decides whether the gain is short-term or long-term, and the two are taxed very differently. For listed equity shares and equity mutual fund units, the dividing line is 12 months. Hold for 12 months or less and the gain is a Short-Term Capital Gain (STCG); hold for more than 12 months and it becomes a Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG), which enjoys a friendlier rate and an annual exemption. Property and other assets carry their own, longer holding periods and separate rules, so the equity thresholds below do not automatically apply to them.

The rates: equity versus other assets

For listed equity and equity mutual funds the rates are fixed and do not depend on your income slab. For debt mutual fund units bought on or after 1 April 2023, there is no special long-term rate at all — the whole gain is added to your income and taxed at your personal slab rate, with no indexation benefit. This is a major change from the older debt-fund treatment, so check the purchase date.

How to reduce and plan

The most common legal lever for equity is tax harvesting: booking long-term gains up to the ₹1.25 lakh exemption each financial year so that portion escapes tax, then reinvesting. Spreading large sales across two financial years can let you use the exemption twice. Holding equity past the 12-month mark converts a 20% STCG into a 12.5% LTCG. Capital losses can be set off against gains, and unused long-term losses can be carried forward for eight years. Note that Capital Gains Tax is separate from deductions like Section 80C, which reduce your salary or business income, not your capital gains. For long-horizon money, low-cost index funds keep churn — and therefore taxable events — low.

Worked example
Ananya, a Bengaluru software engineer, invested ₹5,00,000 in an equity mutual fund. Sixteen months later she redeems all units for ₹7,20,000. Separately, she also sold shares she had held for only 8 months, booking a ₹60,000 profit. What is her capital gains tax for the financial year?

Step 1 — Classify each sale. The equity mutual fund was held for 16 months, which is more than 12 months, so that gain is long-term (LTCG) and taxed at 12.5%. The shares were held for 8 months, which is 12 months or less, so that gain is short-term (STCG) and taxed at 20%.

Step 2 — Compute each gain. On the fund: ₹7,20,000 − ₹5,00,000 = ₹2,20,000 long-term gain. On the shares: ₹60,000 short-term gain, already given.

Step 3 — Apply the ₹1.25 lakh LTCG exemption. The exemption applies only to long-term equity gains. Her taxable LTCG is ₹2,20,000 − ₹1,25,000 = ₹95,000. The short-term gain gets no such exemption.

Step 4 — Tax each slice at its own rate.

So what? Ananya owes roughly ₹23,875 in capital gains tax before any surcharge or cess. Two lessons stand out. First, the ₹1.25 lakh exemption saved her 12.5% of ₹1,25,000, or about ₹15,625 — a real reason to book long-term gains deliberately each year. Second, her short-term share sale cost 20% while the long-term fund gain cost only 12.5%; had she simply waited past 12 months on those shares, that slice would have been taxed far more gently, and part of it could even have fallen under the same annual exemption. Patience and timing, not clever tricks, do most of the work here.

Check yourself
Q
You sell equity mutual fund units after holding them for 10 months, making a gain of ₹90,000. What rate applies, and does the ₹1.25 lakh exemption help you?
A: Ten months is 12 months or less, so this is a short-term capital gain taxed at 20% — here, ₹18,000. The ₹1.25 lakh exemption applies only to long-term equity gains, so it does not help this short-term sale at all.

Key takeaways

  • Capital gains tax is charged only on the profit when you sell a capital asset, and only when the sale actually happens.
  • For listed equity and equity mutual funds, more than 12 months is long-term (12.5% above a ₹1.25 lakh yearly exemption); 12 months or less is short-term (20%).
  • Debt mutual fund units bought on or after 1 April 2023 are taxed at your slab rate with no special long-term rate and no indexation.
  • The ₹1.25 lakh LTCG exemption is per financial year across all equity sales, so harvesting gains yearly is a simple, legal saving.
  • Property, gold, and other assets follow their own holding periods and rules, and every rate can change with the Union Budget.

Frequently asked questions

Is capital gains tax the same as income tax?

It is a part of income tax, but capital gains are taxed under their own head with their own special rates, separate from salary or business income.

Do I pay tax if I have not sold my shares?

No. Capital gains tax applies only on sale. Unrealised paper gains on units you still hold are not taxed.

What is the ₹1.25 lakh exemption exactly?

It is an annual allowance on long-term equity and equity fund gains. Only the long-term gain above ₹1.25 lakh in a financial year is taxed at 12.5%.

How are debt mutual funds taxed now?

For units bought on or after 1 April 2023, the whole gain is added to your income and taxed at your slab rate, with no long-term rate and no indexation.

Can I set off a capital loss?

Yes. Capital losses can be set off against capital gains under the rules, and unused long-term losses can generally be carried forward for eight years.

Will these rates stay the same?

Not necessarily. Rates, holding periods, and exemptions are reviewed in each Union Budget, so always verify the current figures before you sell.
