Yield to Maturity (YTM)

Yield to Maturity (YTM) is the annualised discount rate that equates a bond’s current price with the present value of its remaining coupons and redemption amount. It is a conditional return measure: the calculation assumes the bond is held to maturity, all promised payments arrive, and coupons are reinvested at the same yield. Bond price and YTM move inversely when the promised cash flows are unchanged.

What yield to maturity means

A conventional bond promises periodic coupon payments and repayment of its face value or redemption amount at maturity. In the secondary market, the bond may trade above or below face value because of market yields, credit expectations, liquidity, supply and demand, and embedded features. YTM answers: what single discount rate makes the present value of the bond’s remaining contractual cash flows equal its market price?

YTM is an internal rate of return, not a guaranteed realised return. It incorporates coupon income and the gain or loss between purchase price and redemption value. But it assumes no default, holding to maturity and reinvestment of interim coupons at the YTM. Callable, puttable, floating-rate, amortising or default-risky bonds may require additional measures such as yield to call, yield to worst or scenario analysis.

YTM versus coupon rate and current yield

The coupon rate measures coupon payments against face value. Current yield measures the annual coupon against the current clean price. YTM discounts all remaining cash flows and includes the pull between price and redemption value.

For a standard fixed-rate bond redeemed at par, a discount price normally means YTM exceeds the coupon rate, while a premium price normally means YTM is below it. At par, YTM equals the coupon rate when timing and compounding conventions align.

How investors use YTM

YTM provides a common starting point for comparing conventional bonds with different coupons, prices and maturities. It should be paired with credit quality, duration, liquidity, tax, seniority and call features. Comparing YTM with a bank fixed-deposit rate also requires adjustments for compounding, tax, early-exit terms, credit risk and applicable deposit insurance.

All else equal, rising market yields reduce the price of existing fixed-rate bonds, while falling yields raise it. The repo rate can influence the yield curve, but different maturities also react to inflation expectations, government borrowing, liquidity and credit spreads. A high YTM can therefore indicate higher risk, not a free return advantage.

Worked example
A non-callable company bond has a face and redemption value of ₹1,000, pays an annual coupon of ₹80 and has exactly five years remaining. Assume the quoted ₹925 price is the relevant price for this simplified annual-cash-flow calculation. Estimate its YTM and compare it with the exact annual yield.

Step 1 — List the inputs. F = ₹1,000, P = ₹925, C = ₹80 and n = 5. Because price is below redemption value, the YTM should exceed the 8% coupon rate if promised payments are made.

Step 2 — Annualise the price pull. The ₹75 difference spread evenly over five years is ₹15 per year for the approximation.

Step 3 — Find the average base. (₹1,000 + ₹925) ÷ 2 = ₹962.50.

Step 4 — Apply the approximation. (₹80 + ₹15) ÷ ₹962.50 = 0.09870, or approximately 9.87%.

So what? The 8% coupon alone misses the return contribution from buying below the ₹1,000 redemption value. But even 9.977% is conditional: default, sale before maturity, call provisions or reinvesting coupons at another rate will change the realised result.

Check yourself
A standard fixed-rate bond with a 7% coupon trades above its face value and will be redeemed at par. Is its YTM normally higher or lower than 7%, and why?
A: Normally lower than 7%. The investor pays more than the par amount received at maturity, so the capital loss offsets part of the coupon income. The conclusion assumes unchanged promised cash flows and aligned yield conventions.

Key takeaways

  • YTM is the discount rate equating price with remaining contractual bond cash flows.
  • It includes coupons and the pull between purchase price and redemption value.
  • Quoted YTM is conditional on holding to maturity, receiving promised payments and reinvesting coupons at that yield.
  • For a standard par-redemption bond, discount price usually means YTM above coupon; premium price means YTM below coupon.
  • The approximation is useful for intuition, but proper comparison uses exact yield conventions and risk analysis.

Frequently asked questions

Is a higher YTM always better?

No. It can compensate for higher credit, liquidity, call or duration risk. Compare like with like.

Does YTM assume I reinvest the coupons?

Yes. Different reinvestment rates produce a different realised return.

How is YTM different from current yield?

Current yield uses only annual coupon divided by price. YTM discounts every remaining coupon and redemption payment.

Why does bond price fall when yields rise?

Fixed cash flows become less attractive relative to newer yields, so price falls until their yield becomes competitive, all else equal.

Can I compare YTM with a fixed-deposit rate?

Only as a starting point. Adjust for compounding, tax, credit risk, liquidity, early-sale risk, calls and deposit insurance.

Where can I find a bond’s YTM?

Recognised exchanges and trading platforms may display indicative yields. RBI Retail Direct provides information for government securities. Verify the calculation conventions.
