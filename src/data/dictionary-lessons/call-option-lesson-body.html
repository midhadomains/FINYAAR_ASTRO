Call Option

Call Option is a contract that gives its buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy an underlying asset at a fixed strike price on or before a set expiry date, in exchange for an upfront premium paid to the seller. The buyer profits when the underlying price rises well above the strike; the most the buyer can lose is the premium paid. The seller, or writer, collects that premium but takes on the risk of large losses if the price climbs.

What a call option is

A call option is one of the two basic building blocks of the options market, the other being the put option. When you buy a call, you are buying the right to purchase something, most commonly an equity share or an index, at a price you lock in today. You can sell the option before expiry. Exercise follows the contract rules: NSE equity options are European-style, so exercise occurs only at expiry. An out-of-the-money call expires worthless; an in-the-money stock call can create a delivery obligation. This one-sided payoff, limited loss but large potential gain for the buyer, is what makes calls attractive to speculators and useful to hedgers. Like a futures contract, a call derives its value from an underlying, but unlike futures it does not force the buyer to transact.

Strike, premium and expiry

Three numbers define every call. The strike price is the fixed price at which the buyer may purchase the underlying. The premium is the price of the option itself, paid upfront by the buyer to the seller and quoted per share, though options trade in fixed lots. The expiry is the last date the right is alive; Indian equity options are European style, meaning they can be exercised only at expiry, although they can be traded before then. Other things equal, a call premium generally increases with volatility and often with time remaining, because more time and bigger swings mean more chance the price ends above the strike.

Buyer payoff and break-even

At expiry, a call buyer earns the intrinsic value, which is how far the underlying price sits above the strike, floored at zero. Subtract the premium already paid and you get profit per share before costs. Here S is the underlying price at expiry and K is the strike; multiply the per-share result by the lot size for the total. The break-even price is simply the strike plus the premium: below it the buyer is still in the red even after exercising. Above it, every rupee of price rise is a rupee of profit.

Buyer versus seller (writer) risk

The buyer and the seller face mirror-image risks. The standalone option payoff limits the buyer's loss to the premium before costs. Physical settlement can require additional funding and creates subsequent stock exposure. An uncovered writer collects the premium as maximum profit but faces large, potentially unlimited loss if the price runs up, and must post margin with the exchange as security. Writing calls is therefore a professional, capital-heavy activity, closer to insurance underwriting than to buying a lottery ticket. Writers may speculate or use calls within a broader strategy. A covered call earns premium against shares already held, but caps the upside and leaves most of the shares' downside risk; it is not a complete hedge. SEBI studies show that most individual traders in the futures and options segment lose money, so treat both sides with respect. For the wider context, see our derivatives and risk hub.

Worked example
In this hypothetical example, ignoring taxes and transaction costs, Priya is bullish on Reliance Industries, trading at ₹2,900. She buys one lot of the ₹3,000 strike call option expiring at month end. Assume a lot size of 250 shares for illustration; verify current contract specifications before trading. The assumed lot is 250 shares and the quoted premium is ₹40 per share. What does she pay, where is her break-even, and what is her profit or loss if the stock closes at ₹2,850, ₹3,000, ₹3,040 and ₹3,150 on expiry day?

Step 1 — Cost of the position. Priya pays the premium upfront: ₹40 per share multiplied by the lot of 250 shares equals ₹10,000. This ₹10,000 is the maximum loss on the option payoff before costs. Holding an in-the-money stock call to expiry can require delivery margins and funds to buy the shares. At this assumed strike and lot size, the share purchase requires ₹7,50,000 in addition to the premium. The table shows economic profit or loss, not a cash settlement payment.

Step 2 — Break-even. Using break-even equals strike plus premium, she needs the stock at ₹3,000 plus ₹40, which is ₹3,040, just to recover her cost. Only above ₹3,040 does she make a net profit.

Step 3 — Payoff at each expiry price. Profit per share equals max(S minus 3,000, 0) minus 40, and total profit is that figure times 250 shares.

At ₹2,850 the option is worthless because nobody would buy at ₹3,000 when the market offers ₹2,850, so she loses the full ₹10,000. At exactly ₹3,000 the option is still worthless, and she again loses the premium. At ₹3,040 she recovers her premium exactly and breaks even. At ₹3,150 the option is worth ₹150 per share; after subtracting the ₹40 premium she nets ₹110 per share, or ₹27,500 on the lot.

So what? Priya risked a known ₹10,000 for a shot at an open-ended gain, and her position only rewards her once Reliance clears ₹3,040, well above where she bought. A modest rise to ₹3,000 still wipes out her stake. That is the hard truth of buying calls: you can be right about direction yet still lose if the move is too small or too slow, even when the stock has risen.

Check yourself
Q
A trader buys a ₹500 strike call for a ₹15 premium. At what stock price does the buyer break even at expiry, and what is the maximum loss?
A: Break-even is strike plus premium, so ₹500 plus ₹15 equals ₹515. The maximum loss is the premium of ₹15 per share, suffered whenever the stock closes at or below the ₹500 strike.

Key takeaways

  • A call option gives the buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy the underlying at the strike price for a premium.
  • The buyer maximum loss is the premium paid; the upside is large as the price rises above the strike.
  • Break-even equals strike price plus premium; the buyer profits only above that level.
  • The seller or writer collects the premium but faces large, potentially unlimited losses and must post margin.
  • SEBI data shows most individual futures and options traders lose money, so size positions with care.

Frequently asked questions

Is buying a call safer than short selling?

The loss on a bought call is capped at the premium, whereas a short sale can lose far more than the initial stake. In that narrow sense a call limits downside, but the option can still expire worthless, so it is not risk-free.

What happens if I do not sell my call before expiry?

If the underlying finishes at or below the strike, the call expires worthless. An in-the-money NSE stock call is physically settled, requiring the buyer to pay for and receive shares at the strike. Check delivery funding requirements before expiry; the option payoff alone does not describe the cash required.

Why does a call lose value even when the stock does not move?

Premium contains time value that erodes as expiry approaches, a process called time decay. A call can lose money simply because time passes and the expected move fails to arrive quickly enough.

How is a call option different from a futures contract?

A futures buyer is obliged to transact and faces two-sided profit and loss. A call buyer only has a right, pays a premium for it, and can walk away, giving a limited-loss, one-sided payoff.

Do I need a large account to buy calls?

The initial purchase costs the premium times the lot size, plus charges. Physically settled stock calls can require delivery margins and funds to purchase shares near expiry. Uncovered call writing requires margin and carries potentially unlimited loss.

Are call options suitable for beginners?

They are complex, decay with time and often expire worthless, and regulators note that most retail traders lose money. Beginners should learn the mechanics thoroughly and risk only money they can afford to lose.
