Book Value

Book Value is what a company's accounts say is left for its shareholders once every liability has been settled: total assets minus total liabilities. On an Indian balance sheet the same figure appears directly as shareholders' funds, being share capital plus reserves and surplus, or equity share capital plus other equity under Ind AS. Divide it by the equity shares outstanding and you have book value per share. It is neither the face value printed at issue nor the price the NSE and BSE quote today, and the distance between the three is the informative part.

What is left after every claim is met

Every rupee of assets a company holds was funded by somebody, either a lender or an owner, and the balance sheet exists to show that arithmetic closing. Remove the lenders' claims, together with payables, provisions and every other liability, and the remainder belongs to the equity holders. That remainder is book value, described as net worth when the subject is a whole company rather than one share.

Reserves do most of the work. Share capital captures only the face value of shares issued, so money raised at a premium went into securities premium, and every year of profit kept back has been piling into retained earnings since. A business two decades old can carry share capital of a few crore and reserves many times larger.

Reducing it to one share

Total assets is everything the company owns at the reporting date, carried at the values the books allow. Total liabilities is everything it owes, long-term borrowings and current dues alike. Preference capital comes off because those holders rank ahead of ordinary owners and their stake is not what the equity shareholders own. The denominator is the count of equity shares in issue, and a diluted count answers more honestly where convertibles exist.

Run it again with goodwill and intangibles removed and you get tangible book value per share, which matters most for companies that grew by acquisition.

The events that move it

Book value changes only when something passes through the accounts, which makes it steadier than a market price and slower to respond to news.

Why the market price refuses to match it

Accounts look backwards at cost while prices look forward at earnings, so the two were never going to agree. A factory bought years ago sits at original cost less accumulated depreciation, worth a fraction of its replacement cost on paper while producing perfectly well, and land bought in the 1980s may be carried at a figure that resembles nothing. The things that make a modern business valuable, a brand built through decades of advertising, a research programme, a trained workforce, were expensed as they were created.

Market price therefore usually exceeds book value, and the multiple between them is the price-to-book ratio. Keep face value separate from both: a nominal figure fixed at issue, commonly ₹10, ₹5, ₹2 or ₹1, changed only by a split, it matters here only in deciding how much of net worth is labelled share capital rather than reserves.

Worked example
Sunil Bhattacharya, a railway engineer in Kharagpur, opens the annual report of an unnamed listed company he owns. Assets are property, plant and equipment of ₹420 crore, capital work in progress of ₹30 crore, goodwill of ₹50 crore, inventories of ₹120 crore, receivables of ₹150 crore and cash of ₹130 crore. It owes long-term borrowings of ₹180 crore, trade payables of ₹110 crore and other current liabilities of ₹60 crore. There are 5 crore equity shares of ₹10 face value, and the share trades at ₹385. What is a share worth on the books?

Step 1, total up what is owned. Adding the six asset lines gives ₹900 crore, of which ₹30 crore is a plant still being built and ₹50 crore is goodwill from an old acquisition.

Step 2, take away what is owed. Liabilities come to ₹350 crore. Net assets are therefore ₹550 crore, and the equity side of the same balance sheet confirms it: share capital of ₹50 crore, being 5 crore shares at ₹10, plus reserves and surplus of ₹500 crore.

Step 3, cut it into shares. Divide ₹550 crore by 5 crore shares and book value per share is ₹110. Strip out goodwill and tangible book value per share is ₹100. Against a market price of ₹385, the shares trade at 3.5 times book, or 3.85 times tangible book.

Step 4, roll it forward a year. Suppose the company earns ₹90 crore and pays out ₹30 crore as dividend. The retained ₹60 crore lands in reserves, net worth becomes ₹610 crore and book value per share rises to ₹122 without a single new share being issued.

So what? Three numbers attach to Sunil's share and each answers a different question. Face value of ₹10 is the accounting unit. Book value of ₹110 is the audited net assets behind it. The ₹385 price is what buyers will pay for the earnings those assets are expected to produce, and the gap of ₹275 is the market saying this company is worth considerably more than the sum of its recorded parts.

Check yourself
Q
A company declares a one-for-one bonus issue. What happens to its total book value and to its book value per share?
A: Total book value does not change, because a bonus issue only moves money from reserves into share capital inside the same net worth. The share count doubles, so book value per share halves. Nothing has been created or destroyed.

Key takeaways

  • Book value is total assets minus total liabilities, and equals shareholders' funds.
  • Book value per share divides that figure, after deducting preference capital, by the equity shares outstanding.
  • Retained profits build it, while dividends, losses, depreciation and impairments reduce it.
  • Market price normally exceeds it because internally built brands, research and skills are expensed rather than capitalised.
  • Face value, book value and market price are three separate numbers and should never be substituted for one another.

Frequently asked questions

Is book value the same as net worth?

For a company the two describe the same quantity, assets minus liabilities, with net worth the commoner term in lending language. Book value is preferred when the figure goes per share.

Can a share trade below its book value?

Yes, when investors expect poor returns on those assets, doubt the values they are carried at, or fear losses ahead. Treat it as a reason to examine asset quality, not as a bargain.

What is the difference between book value and market value?

Book value is a historical measure fixed at each reporting date; market value is what buyers and sellers agree on continuously. One records what was spent and retained, the other prices what is expected.

How does depreciation affect it?

Each year's charge reduces the carrying value of the asset and the profit flowing into reserves, so book value falls though no cash left. Over a long asset life it can sink well below replacement cost.

Does book value show what a shutdown would pay me?

Only very roughly. A forced sale rarely realises carrying values, closure costs arise and secured lenders are paid first, so liquidation value usually lands below book value.

Why do banks report book value so prominently?

Because a lender's net worth is the regulated cushion that absorbs loan losses and caps how much it can lend, and its assets are financial instruments carried at measured values.
