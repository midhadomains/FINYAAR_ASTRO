Capital Gains Tax

Capital Gains Tax is the tax you pay in India on the profit you make when you sell a capital asset — such as equity shares, mutual fund units, or property — for more than you paid for it. The tax applies to the taxable gain, not the full sale value, and the rate depends on the type of asset, transaction conditions and how long you held it before selling. It is charged under the head "Capital Gains" of the Income Tax Act.

What a capital gain actually is

A capital gain arises when you transfer a capital asset for more than its eligible cost of acquisition after applying the relevant computation rules. If you buy equity shares for ₹1,00,000 and sell them later for ₹1,40,000, the simple difference is ₹40,000, and that gain — not the ₹1,40,000 sale value — is the starting point for tax. A loss on sale is a capital loss, which may be set off against other capital gains under the rules. The key point is that a gain is generally taxed when a transfer happens; paper profits on units you continue to hold are normally not taxed merely because their market value increased. Because the profit is measured in rupees today against a purchase made earlier, inflation can quietly eat into the real value of what you keep after tax.

Short-term versus long-term

The single most important question is how long you held the asset, because that helps decide whether the gain is short-term or long-term. For qualifying listed equity shares and equity-oriented mutual fund units, the dividing line is 12 months. Hold for 12 months or less and the gain is a Short-Term Capital Gain (STCG); hold for more than 12 months and it becomes a Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG). Property and other assets carry their own holding periods and separate rules, so the equity thresholds below do not automatically apply to them.

The rates: equity versus other assets

For qualifying listed equity and equity-oriented mutual funds, special rates can apply when conditions such as Securities Transaction Tax are met. For specified debt-oriented mutual fund units acquired on or after 1 April 2023, the former long-term indexation treatment generally does not apply; gains are commonly treated as short-term and taxed at the investor’s applicable slab rate. Check that the particular fund meets the statutory definition and confirm its acquisition date.

How to reduce and plan

A common planning technique for qualifying equity is tax-gain harvesting: realising long-term gains within the annual ₹1.25 lakh threshold and then deciding whether to reinvest, while accounting for transaction costs, market movement and the reset holding period. Spreading a large sale across financial years may allow the threshold to be used in each year. Holding qualifying equity beyond 12 months can change a 20% STCG into a 12.5% LTCG subject to the annual threshold and other conditions. Capital losses can be set off under specific rules: short-term losses can generally offset short- or long-term gains, while long-term losses can generally offset only long-term gains. Eligible unabsorbed losses may be carried forward for eight assessment years when filing conditions are met. Capital-gains treatment is also separate from deductions such as Section 80C. For long-horizon money, low-cost index funds can help keep portfolio churn — and therefore taxable sale events — low.

Worked example
Ananya, a Bengaluru software engineer, invested ₹5,00,000 in a qualifying equity-oriented mutual fund. Sixteen months later she redeems all units for ₹7,20,000. Separately, she sells qualifying listed shares held for eight months, booking a ₹60,000 profit. Assuming the special equity rates apply and she has no other Section 112A gains, what is her indicative capital gains tax for the financial year?

Step 1 — Classify each sale. The equity-oriented mutual fund was held for 16 months, more than 12 months, so the gain is long-term (LTCG) and the amount above the annual threshold is taxed at 12.5%. The shares were held for eight months, so the gain is short-term (STCG) and taxed at 20% under the assumed conditions.

Step 2 — Compute each gain. On the fund: ₹7,20,000 − ₹5,00,000 = ₹2,20,000 long-term gain. On the shares: ₹60,000 short-term gain, already given.

Step 3 — Apply the ₹1.25 lakh LTCG threshold. The threshold applies to the aggregate qualifying long-term equity gains. Her taxable LTCG is ₹2,20,000 − ₹1,25,000 = ₹95,000. The short-term gain does not use this threshold.

Step 4 — Tax each slice at its own rate.

Check yourself
You sell qualifying equity mutual fund units after holding them for 10 months, making a gain of ₹90,000. What rate applies, and does the ₹1.25 lakh long-term threshold help you?
A: Ten months is 12 months or less, so this is a short-term capital gain. Assuming the special equity conditions are met, it is taxed at 20%, producing ₹18,000 before cess and any surcharge. The ₹1.25 lakh threshold applies to qualifying long-term equity gains, so it does not reduce this short-term gain.

Key takeaways

  • Capital gains tax is generally charged on the taxable gain when a capital asset is transferred, not merely because its quoted value rises.
  • For qualifying listed equity and equity-oriented mutual funds, more than 12 months is long-term, while 12 months or less is short-term; special rates and conditions apply.
  • Specified mutual-fund units acquired on or after 1 April 2023 generally do not receive the former long-term indexation treatment and may be taxed at the applicable slab rate.
  • The ₹1.25 lakh Section 112A threshold is aggregate per financial year across qualifying long-term gains.
  • Property, gold and other assets follow their own holding periods and rules, and tax provisions can change through the Union Budget and Finance Act.

Frequently asked questions

Is capital gains tax the same as income tax?

Capital gains tax forms part of income tax, but capital gains are computed under their own head and may use special rates rather than the slab rates that apply to salary or business income.

Do I pay capital gains tax if I have not sold my shares?

Generally, capital gains tax arises on a transfer or sale. An increase in the market value of shares or units you continue to hold is normally an unrealised gain and is not taxed merely because the quoted price increased.

What is the ₹1.25 lakh exemption exactly?

It is an aggregate annual threshold for qualifying long-term gains covered by Section 112A, including listed equity shares and equity-oriented fund units when the applicable conditions are met. The qualifying gain above ₹1.25 lakh in a financial year is generally taxed at 12.5%.

How are debt mutual funds taxed now?

Specified mutual-fund units acquired on or after 1 April 2023 generally do not receive the former long-term indexation treatment. Their gains are commonly treated as short-term and taxed at the investor’s applicable slab rate, subject to the fund and transaction meeting the statutory definition.

Can I set off a capital loss?

Yes, subject to the rules. A short-term capital loss can generally be set off against short- or long-term capital gains, while a long-term capital loss can generally be set off only against long-term capital gains. Eligible unabsorbed losses may be carried forward for eight assessment years when return-filing conditions are met.

Will these rates stay the same?

Not necessarily. Rates, holding periods, thresholds and exemptions can change through the Union Budget and Finance Act, so verify the rules for the relevant financial year before selling.
