NAV (Net Asset Value)

NAV (Net Asset Value) is the per-unit market value of a mutual fund scheme, calculated as the fund's total assets minus its total liabilities, divided by the number of units outstanding. It is the single price at which you buy or redeem units of a fund on any given business day.

What is NAV (Net Asset Value)?

NAV (Net Asset Value) is the price of one unit of a Mutual Fund scheme. When a fund pools money from thousands of investors and buys shares, bonds and cash, the total worth of that portfolio, after subtracting what the fund owes, divided across all units, gives you the NAV. If a scheme owns securities worth ₹510 crore across 30 crore units, its NAV is ₹17.00 per unit.

Think of a family that jointly owns a shop worth ₹51 lakh split into 3 lakh "shares" of ownership. Each share is worth ₹17. As the shop's stock and cash rise or fall, so does the value of each share. A mutual fund works the same way, only its "shop" is a basket of stocks and bonds priced by the market every day.

Indian mutual funds are regulated by SEBI, which mandates that fund houses compute and publish NAV for every scheme at the end of each business day. This end-of-day pricing is why mutual funds differ from an ETF or a listed Equity Share, which trade continuously at live market prices.

Breaking down each term in plain words:

Total Assets is the market value of everything the fund owns, the current price of all shares and bonds held, plus cash, plus accrued income such as an unpaid Dividend or interest.

Total Liabilities is everything the fund owes, mainly management fees, custodian charges and other operating costs captured in the Expense Ratio, along with any pending payables.

Total Units Outstanding is the number of units held by all investors combined. This number changes daily as investors buy in through a lump sum or a SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) and redeem out.

A common myth is that a fund with a lower NAV is "cheaper" or better value. It is not. NAV only reflects per-unit accounting value; a ₹12 NAV fund and a ₹250 NAV fund can deliver identical returns. What matters is the percentage change in NAV, not its absolute level.

Types of NAV: Regular vs Direct, Growth vs IDCW

The same scheme quotes different NAVs depending on the plan and option you choose. A Direct plan carries a lower Expense Ratio because no distributor commission is paid, so its NAV compounds faster over time. The Growth option reinvests all gains, while the IDCW (Income Distribution cum Capital Withdrawal) option pays some out, lowering NAV on each payout date.

Because the Direct Growth NAV grows fastest, most long-horizon investors seeking Compound Interest prefer it. The IDCW option is not a bonus; its payout is simply your own capital returned, and the NAV falls by exactly that amount.

Example

Worked example

Riya invests in the fictional Yaar Bluechip Fund. On 1 August 2026 the fund reports the balance sheet below. She wants to know the day's NAV, how many units her ₹10,000 SIP instalment buys, and what her holding is worth one year later if the NAV grows 10%.

Step 1: Total the assets. The fund's holdings on 1 August 2026:

ItemValue (₹ crore)
Equity holdings (market value)495.00
Cash and bank balance12.00
Dividend and interest receivable5.00
Total assets512.00
Less: management fees payable1.50
Less: other payables0.50
Total liabilities2.00
Net assets510.00

Step 2: Divide by units outstanding. The scheme has 30 crore units outstanding.

NAV = (512 − 2) ÷ 30 = 510 ÷ 30 = ₹17.00 per unit.

Step 3: Convert Riya's SIP into units. Her instalment is ₹10,000. Ignoring any stamp duty for clarity:

Units allotted = 10,000 ÷ 17.00 = 588.235 units.

Notice the units are fractional. Unlike buying a whole Equity Share, mutual funds allot units to three decimals, so every rupee is invested. This is also how SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) rupee-cost averaging works: when NAV is low, each ₹10,000 buys more units; when NAV is high, it buys fewer.

Step 4: Value one year later. Suppose strong markets lift the NAV by 10%, so it rises from ₹17.00 to ₹18.70. Riya's 588.235 units are now worth:

588.235 × 18.70 = ₹11,000 (rounded).

The "so what". Her ₹10,000 grew to ₹11,000, a 10% gain, exactly matching the NAV's percentage rise. This is the key insight: your return equals the change in NAV, not its starting level. Had she instead picked a fund with a starting NAV of ₹170 that also rose 10%, her outcome would be identical. Chasing a low NAV is meaningless; what compounds your wealth is the growth rate, the fund's quality, and a low Expense Ratio quietly eating fewer of your returns each year.

Why NAV matters for Indian investors

NAV is your entry and exit price. SEBI's cut-off time rules decide which day's NAV you get: for most equity and debt schemes, invest before 3:00 PM with funds realised, and you receive the same day's NAV; miss it, and you get the next business day's. For liquid and overnight funds, the previous day's NAV can apply.

Because NAV moves with the market, it embodies Volatility and Risk vs Return. An equity fund tracking the Sensex and Nifty will swing more than a debt fund. Spreading money across a low-cost Index Fund and other categories gives you Diversification. Unlike a bank Fixed Deposit, which carries DICGC insurance of up to ₹5 lakh, mutual fund NAV is not guaranteed and can fall.

Check yourself
A fund has total assets of ₹808 crore, liabilities of ₹8 crore and 40 crore units outstanding. What is its NAV?
(808 − 8) ÷ 40 = 800 ÷ 40 = ₹20.00 per unit.

Key takeaways

  • NAV = (Total Assets − Total Liabilities) ÷ Units Outstanding, published daily by SEBI-regulated fund houses.
  • A low NAV does not mean a cheap or better fund; your return equals the percentage change in NAV.
  • Direct plans have a lower expense ratio, so their NAV grows faster than Regular plans over time.
  • SEBI cut-off times (usually 3:00 PM) decide which day's NAV applies to your purchase or redemption.
  • Mutual fund NAV is not guaranteed, unlike a bank deposit covered by DICGC insurance up to ₹5 lakh.

Frequently asked questions

Is a lower NAV fund better to buy?

No. NAV is only a per-unit accounting figure. A ₹15 NAV fund and a ₹300 NAV fund that both rise 12% give you the same 12% return. Focus on the fund's track record, category and expense ratio, not the NAV level.

At what time is NAV declared?

Fund houses compute NAV after markets close and typically publish it by 9:00 to 11:00 PM on the AMC website and AMFI's portal. SEBI cut-off times, usually 3:00 PM for equity and debt funds, determine which day's NAV your transaction receives.

Why did my fund's NAV suddenly drop sharply?

If you hold an IDCW option, the NAV falls by the exact amount of any payout, because that cash left the fund. Otherwise a sharp drop reflects a market fall in the underlying holdings. Check whether a distribution or a market correction caused it.

How does NAV differ from an ETF's market price?

A mutual fund transacts once a day at NAV. An ETF trades live on the exchange like a stock, so its market price can differ slightly from its underlying NAV due to demand and supply. Both are eventually anchored to the value of their holdings.

Does a higher NAV mean I own less of the fund?

Not at all. If NAV is high you simply get fewer units for the same rupee, but each unit is worth more. Your total invested amount and your proportional ownership of the fund are unchanged.

Is mutual fund NAV guaranteed like an FD return?

No. NAV rises and falls with market prices and can go down. A bank Fixed Deposit offers a fixed rate with DICGC cover up to ₹5 lakh, whereas mutual funds carry market risk and no capital guarantee.
