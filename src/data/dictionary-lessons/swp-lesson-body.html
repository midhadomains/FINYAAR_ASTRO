FINYAAR DICTIONARY · MUTUAL FUNDS AND SIP

SWP

SWP (Systematic Withdrawal Plan) is a standing instruction to a mutual fund to sell a fixed rupee amount of your units at a fixed interval and credit the proceeds to your bank. Retired Indians use it to manufacture a monthly income from a lump sum, naming the date and amount themselves rather than waiting for the fund to declare a payout. Nothing is guaranteed: the fund redeems whatever units the cheque requires at that day's NAV, so a falling market consumes more of them. Used carelessly it drains capital quietly; used carefully it is a clean income arrangement.

Selling, dressed up as income

A fixed deposit pays interest and leaves the principal untouched. An SWP differs in kind: on the chosen date the fund house redeems units from your folio, so the money is partly your own capital returning and partly whatever the market added. Calling it income is a household convenience.

The plumbing is ordinary because it is a redemption. Units go out at the applicable NAV, the registrar sells first-in-first-out, and the balance keeps moving with the market like any other mutual fund holding.

The rupee amount is fixed, the unit count is not

Each instalment redeems W divided by the NAV on that date, so a lower NAV means more units cancelled for the same cheque. Tracking the corpus from one withdrawal to the next takes one line.

C with subscript t is the folio value just after the withdrawal in period t, C with subscript t minus one its value after the previous one, the fraction the growth factor of the NAV between those dates, and W the fixed rupee withdrawal. The market acts first and the withdrawal is subtracted second, which is why the order of good and bad years matters.

A withdrawal of ₹30,000 at a NAV of ₹120 costs 250 units; the same cheque at ₹80 costs 375. Rupee-cost averaging helps while buying and hurts while selling.

The rate that decides whether the corpus lasts

Divide the annual withdrawal by the corpus and you have the number that governs everything. The return columns use a simplified annual model: one withdrawal at year-end, expressed as a percentage of the opening corpus. Preserving the opening corpus after 6 per cent inflation requires the withdrawal rate plus 6 per cent. Monthly withdrawals have different timing, so these are reference figures, not exact monthly-plan targets.

Set beside the IDCW option

The alternative is the income distribution cum capital withdrawal option, the modern name for what people still call the dividend plan. Control settles half the choice: the fund house alone decides whether to declare a payout, how much and when, while an SWP pays what you asked for on your chosen date.

Tax structure settles the rest. An IDCW payout is added to total income and taxed at your slab, with tax deducted at source above a threshold, so the whole distribution is exposed. An SWP withdrawal is a redemption, so only the gain inside the units sold is capital gains, with the treatment depending on the fund type and the units sold. Equity-oriented funds follow equity holding-period rules with an annual exemption for eligible long-term gains; other funds can follow different rules. Rates, thresholds and the TDS trigger have all moved in recent Finance Acts, so check the current position with the Income Tax Department.

Worked example
Kamala Iyer retires in Coimbatore with ₹50,00,000 in an equity-oriented fund priced at ₹100 a unit, giving her 50,000 units. She starts an SWP of ₹30,000 a month. How does her folio behave over six months if the market rises gently, and how does it behave if the market falls?

Step 1, the rate she has chosen. Twelve cheques of ₹30,000 come to ₹3,60,000 a year, or 7.2 per cent of her opening corpus. In the simplified year-end model, holding the capital at ₹50,00,000 in rupee terms needs 7.2 per cent after expenses. Monthly withdrawals require a timing adjustment, and holding her purchasing power needs more.

Step 2, the rising market. The NAV climbs from ₹100 towards ₹108 across six months, so each redemption cancels slightly fewer units than the last.

Step 3, the falling market. The NAV slides from ₹100 to ₹82 over the same months while the cheque stays at ₹30,000, so the units cancelled grow month by month and the folio thins from both ends at once.

Step 4, reading the two columns together. Kamala received exactly ₹1,80,000 in both worlds, six cheques of ₹30,000, and her bank statement would look identical either way. In the rising market she gave up 1,725.86 units and the corpus ended ₹2,13,607 higher than it began. In the falling market she gave up 2,018.79 units, about 17 per cent more, and the corpus stands at ₹39,34,459, down ₹10,65,541 against a total of ₹1,80,000 withdrawn.

Step 5, why the extra units matter later. The units cancelled at ₹82 are gone permanently. Should the NAV climb back to ₹100, those 293 additional units would have been worth roughly ₹29,300 that she can never recover, because a cheque of fixed size forced her to sell them cheap. This comparison shows the cost of selling more units at lower prices. It does not isolate sequence-of-returns risk, because the two paths also have different total returns.

So what? An SWP does not shield a corpus from a bad market; it magnifies one, because a fixed cheque forces heavier selling exactly when prices are low. A conservative withdrawal rate, two or three years of cheques parked in something stable, and an annual review of the amount are what make the arrangement survivable.

Check yourself
Q
Two retirees each withdraw ₹25,000 a month for a year. One fund's NAV rises through the year, the other's falls. Both received ₹3,00,000. What is different?
A: The number of units consumed. The falling fund cancelled far more units for the same money, so less of the folio remains for any recovery. Equal cash out is not equal damage.

Key takeaways

  • An SWP sells units on a schedule, so each payout mixes your own capital with market gains.
  • The rupee amount is fixed and the unit count is not, so a falling NAV consumes the folio faster.
  • The withdrawal rate against the corpus decides whether the money lasts.
  • Only the gain inside the units sold is taxed, unlike an IDCW payout taxed at slab in full.
  • A buffer of stable assets stops equity units being sold into a slump.

Frequently asked questions

Is an SWP better than a monthly income from a fixed deposit?

They trade different risks. A deposit gives a contracted payout and no market risk, while an SWP offers growth alongside the chance the corpus shrinks. Many retired households run both.

What withdrawal rate is safe?

None is guaranteed safe, since outcomes depend on the sequence of returns you happen to get. Lower rates survive far more scenarios, and an annual review against the actual corpus matters more than any rule fixed at the outset.

How is each SWP withdrawal taxed?

Every withdrawal is a redemption, and only the capital gain within the units sold is taxable. For an equity fund the holding period of those specific units, picked first-in-first-out, decides the treatment. Verify current rates and the exemption limit with the Income Tax Department.

Can I run an SWP from an ELSS?

Only on units that have completed the statutory three-year lock-in. Units still locked cannot be redeemed, so an SWP started too early fails for want of free units.

Does an exit load apply to SWP withdrawals?

It can, if the units redeemed are younger than the scheme's exit-load period. Many investors start one only after the earliest units cross that window.

What happens if the corpus runs out?

Withdrawals cannot continue once no units remain. The scheme terms determine how an insufficient balance and any notifications are handled. That is why the withdrawal rate deserves an annual review.
