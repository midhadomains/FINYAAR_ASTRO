FINYAAR DICTIONARY · MUTUAL FUNDS AND SIP

Expense Ratio

Expense Ratio is the annual fee, shown as a percentage of a scheme's average assets, that a mutual fund charges every investor to manage and run the fund. It is quietly deducted from the fund's assets each day, so you never pay it as a separate bill, but it directly reduces the returns you finally take home.

What is Expense Ratio?

Expense Ratio is the total yearly cost of owning a mutual fund, expressed as a percentage of the money the fund manages. In India it is officially called the Total Expense Ratio (TER) and it bundles the fund manager's fee, administration, marketing and distribution costs, registrar charges and audit fees into a single number. If a fund has a TER of 1.5%, it means ₹1.50 is charged every year for every ₹100 you have invested.

Here is the part most first-time investors miss: you never see this fee leave your bank account. It is deducted daily from the fund's assets before the NAV (Net Asset Value) is declared. So the NAV you see each evening is already net of the expense ratio. Think of it like the service charge already baked into a restaurant's menu price rather than added at the bottom of the bill.

Relatable Indian example: suppose you invest ₹5,00,000 in an equity fund with a TER of 1.8%. Over the year the fund silently uses about ₹9,000 of your money to run itself. If the fund earns 12% before costs, you actually pocket only about 10.2%. Small number, big long-term impact.

How the Expense Ratio is calculated

The formula SEBI uses is simply the fund's yearly running costs divided by the average money it manages.

Where Total Annual Fund Expenses is the sum of management fees, distribution/marketing costs, administrative and legal costs; and Average AUM is the average value of all investors' money in the scheme over the year. The result is multiplied by 100 to state it as a percentage.

Because it is a percentage, larger funds can spread fixed costs over a bigger base and usually offer a lower ratio. SEBI does not let funds charge whatever they like — it sets tiered upper limits that fall as a scheme's AUM grows, protecting retail investors from runaway fees.

Types of plans and typical ranges

Every scheme in India comes in two versions: a Regular plan (bought through a distributor or agent, whose commission is built into the TER) and a Direct plan (bought straight from the AMC, with no commission). The direct plan always has a lower expense ratio — often 0.5% to 1% lower — for the exact same portfolio.

Example

Worked example
Riya, a 30-year-old from Pune, wants to invest a lump sum of ₹10,00,000 for 10 years. Her advisor offers a Regular plan with a TER of 1.5%. Her friend suggests the Direct plan of the same fund with a TER of 0.5%. Assume the fund earns a gross 12% a year before costs. How much does that 1% difference in expense ratio actually cost Riya?

Step 1 — find the net return of each plan. The expense ratio is subtracted from the gross return.

Regular plan net return = 12% − 1.5% = 10.5%.
Direct plan net return = 12% − 0.5% = 11.5%.

Step 2 — grow the money for 10 years using the power of compound interest, with Future Value = Principal × (1 + r)10.

Regular: ₹10,00,000 × (1.105)10 = ₹10,00,000 × 2.7143 = ₹27,14,270.
Direct: ₹10,00,000 × (1.115)10 = ₹10,00,000 × 2.9700 = ₹29,70,004.

Extra wealth—₹2,55,734

Step 3 — the "so what". By simply choosing the Direct plan, Riya keeps an extra ₹2,55,734 — more than a quarter of her original investment — without taking on one rupee of extra risk. The 1% "small" fee cost her over 25% of her starting capital because it compounds every single year. Over 20 or 30 years, or through a monthly SIP (Systematic Investment Plan), this gap grows even wider. This is why the expense ratio is often called the most important number after returns themselves.

Why it matters for your returns

A high expense ratio can quietly wipe out the benefit of diversification and clever stock picking. When you compare two similar funds, the one with the lower TER has a permanent head start every year. That said, expense ratio is not the only thing to judge — a slightly costlier active fund that consistently beats its benchmark can still be worth it, so weigh cost against the fund's long-term risk vs return record, not in isolation. Unlike a fixed deposit where the rate is fixed and clean, mutual fund returns are always shown after expenses, so the TER is already working on you silently.

Check yourself
You invest ₹3,00,000 in a fund with an expense ratio of 2%. Roughly how much do you pay in fees in one year, and does it come out of your bank account separately?
You pay about ₹6,000 a year (2% of ₹3,00,000). No — it is never billed separately. It is deducted daily from the fund's assets before the NAV is declared, so it is already reflected in the value you see.

Key takeaways

  • Expense Ratio (TER) is the annual cost of a mutual fund, shown as a percentage of assets.
  • It is deducted daily from the fund and is already built into the NAV — you never pay it separately.
  • Direct plans always cost less than Regular plans for the identical portfolio.
  • SEBI caps TER on tiered slabs; index funds and ETFs are cheapest, capped at 1%.
  • A 1% higher expense ratio can cost you lakhs over a decade because it compounds.
  • Always compare TER, but judge it alongside a fund's long-term performance, not alone.

Frequently asked questions

Is a lower expense ratio always better?

For passive funds like index funds and ETFs, yes — since they all track the same index, the cheapest one usually wins. For active funds, a low TER is desirable but not the only test; a fund that reliably beats its benchmark after costs can justify a slightly higher fee. Always compare cost against consistent net returns.

How do I find a fund's expense ratio?

Every AMC must disclose the daily TER of each scheme on its website, and it is also listed on the scheme information document, fact sheet and platforms like the SEBI-regulated aggregators. Look specifically at the Direct plan's TER if you buy directly. Funds must inform investors of any change in TER before it takes effect.

Does the expense ratio include exit load or brokerage?

No. The TER covers ongoing running costs only. Exit load (a charge for redeeming early), STT, stamp duty and the fund's own trading brokerage are separate and not part of the TER. Exit load, if any, is charged only when you sell within a defined period.

Why is the Direct plan cheaper than the Regular plan?

The Regular plan builds in a commission paid to the distributor or agent who sold you the fund. The Direct plan has no such commission because you buy straight from the AMC. Same portfolio, same fund manager — only the TER differs, which is why the Direct plan's NAV grows faster over time.

Does the expense ratio affect my SIP too?

Yes. Whether you invest a lump sum or through a monthly SIP, the same daily deduction applies to your holdings. Because SIP investors typically stay invested for many years, a lower expense ratio compounds into a meaningfully larger corpus over the full journey.

What is a good expense ratio for an equity fund in India?

As a rough guide, a Direct equity fund under about 1% is competitive, an index fund under 0.30% is attractive, and an ETF under 0.10% is excellent. Anything near the SEBI cap of 2.25% deserves a second look, especially if the fund is not outperforming.
