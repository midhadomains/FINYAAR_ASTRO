BSE

BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange) is India's oldest stock exchange and the oldest in Asia, established in Mumbai in 1875. It carries several thousand listings, publishes the Sensex, and runs a main board plus dedicated platforms for smaller firms. Trading has been fully electronic since the outcry ring closed in the mid-1990s, clearing runs through its own clearing corporation, and BSE Limited is itself a listed company today, under SEBI regulation like every Indian exchange.

From a group of brokers in 1875 to a listed company

Brokers meeting informally under a banyan tree near Mumbai's Town Hall formalised themselves on 9 July 1875 as The Native Share and Stock Brokers' Association, Premchand Roychand among the moving figures. That body became BSE, and in 1957 it was the first exchange in the country recognised by the Government of India under the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956.

The changes since have been structural rather than cosmetic. Screen-based trading arrived in 1995 and ended the shouted bargains of the ring. Corporatisation and demutualisation followed in 2007 under a scheme framed in 2005, separating ownership from trading rights and turning member-brokers into shareholders. In 2017 BSE Limited was itself listed, on the NSE.

What the Sensex measures, and how

Thirty companies make up the Sensex, published since 1986 against a base period of 1978 to 1979 taken as 100. The number quoted is no average of thirty share prices. It is a ratio.

P with the subscript i is the current price of the i-th constituent, Q with the subscript i is the number of shares it has issued, and F with the subscript i is its free-float factor, the share of those actually available to the public rather than locked with promoters. Their product is that company's free-float market capitalisation, and the thirty are summed. The index divisor scales the sum back to the base period and is adjusted whenever a corporate action would otherwise create a jump reflecting no real price change.

Free-float weighting has a direct consequence: influence follows tradable value, so a large company held mostly by its promoters counts for less than its headline valuation suggests.

Three doors to a listing

A company does not face one set of listing conditions at BSE. The route depends on size and record of profits.

PlatformWho it is forDistinguishing features
Main boardCompanies meeting SEBI eligibility normsFull disclosure, quarterly reporting
SME platformSmall and medium enterprises, from 2012Lighter norms, minimum lot size, market making
Startups platformEarly-stage companies in newer sectorsAccess restricted to informed investors

An IPO on the SME platform is not a permanent status. A company that grows, meets the main board conditions and completes the prescribed period can migrate upward under a published framework.

Clearing, settlement and the other counters

Trades on BSE are cleared and settled by Indian Clearing Corporation Limited, a wholly owned subsidiary acting as central counterparty. It stands between buyer and seller, collects margins and guarantees settlement even when one side defaults, which is what lets you buy from an anonymous stranger without checking that the stranger can deliver.

Equity settlement follows the T plus 1 rolling cycle used across Indian exchanges, with securities credited directly to the buyer's demat account. Alongside the equity counter, BSE runs derivatives and currency segments and a mutual fund order collection platform, so it handles more than share trading.

Worked example
Manish Agarwal in Jaipur holds ₹8,00,000 in a Sensex index fund. The index opens at 82,400. During the day one heavyweight with a 9 per cent index weight falls 4 per cent, a bank with 6.5 per cent weight rises 1.2 per cent, an IT company with 4 per cent weight slips 0.5 per cent, and the remaining constituents together add 0.1 per cent. What does the index do, and what does it cost him?

Step 1, where a weight comes from. Each company's weight is its free-float market capitalisation divided by that of all thirty together. Weight therefore tracks tradable value, which is why a 9 per cent weight can belong to a company that is not the largest by headline size at all.

Step 2, converting a share move into an index move. A constituent contributes its own percentage move multiplied by its weight. The heavyweight falling 4 per cent on a 9 per cent weight pulls the index down by 0.09 × 4, which is 0.36 per cent, whatever its share price happens to be in rupees. The rupee price of the share never enters the calculation.

Step 3, turning percentages into points. Multiply each contribution by the opening level of 82,400.

ConstituentIndex weightMove todayContribution in points
Heavyweight9.0%−4.0%−297
Bank6.5%+1.2%+64
IT company4.0%−0.5%−17
Other 27 together80.5%+0.1%+66
Net effect100%−184

Step 4, what the screen reports. The index closes near 82,216, down about 184 points, a fall of roughly 0.22 per cent. One stock did nearly all the damage: without it the other twenty-nine would have carried the index up by more than a hundred points.

Step 5, what it costs Manish. His fund tracks the index, so a point is worth ₹8,00,000 divided by 82,400, about ₹9.71. A fall of 184 points costs him roughly ₹1,787, the same answer as applying 0.22 per cent to ₹8,00,000 directly. On a portfolio of ₹80,00,000 the identical day would cost ₹17,870.

So what? Index points mean nothing until they are converted into your own rupees, and the conversion is simply your portfolio value multiplied by the percentage move. It also shows how concentrated a free-float weighted index can be: a single 9 per cent constituent having a bad day can outweigh twenty-nine others having a mildly good one, so a fall in the Sensex is not evidence that the market broadly declined.

Check yourself
Q
Two Sensex companies both rise 5 per cent. One has a 7 per cent index weight, the other 1 per cent. How much does each add to the index?
A: The first adds 0.35 per cent and the second 0.05 per cent, because a contribution is the weight multiplied by the move. The identical share price gain is worth seven times as much to the index in the first case.

Key takeaways

  • BSE was established in 1875, is the oldest exchange in Asia, and was recognised by the government in 1957.
  • It moved from the outcry ring to electronic trading in 1995, demutualised in 2007 and listed its own shares in 2017.
  • The Sensex is thirty companies weighted by free-float market capitalisation and scaled by an index divisor against a 1978 to 1979 base.
  • Companies list on the main board, the SME platform or the startups platform, and can migrate upward over time.
  • Indian Clearing Corporation Limited is the central counterparty for BSE trades and guarantees settlement.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the Sensex hold only thirty companies?

A small, liquid set was easier to compute and follow when the index began in 1986, and thirty free-float weighted companies still capture much of total market value. Broader BSE indices exist for wider coverage.

Does it matter to me whether I trade on BSE or NSE?

For a large company listed on both, very little, since prices stay within paise of each other. For a smaller company it can matter, because one exchange may carry the deeper order book and the better fill on any real size.

Can I buy shares of BSE itself?

Yes. BSE Limited is a listed company and its shares trade on the NSE. That makes you a shareholder in the exchange business, which earns from transaction charges, listing fees and data services, separately from the companies listed on it.

Why do the Sensex and the Nifty move by different point amounts on the same day?

They sit at different levels and hold different constituents in different weights, so a point on one is not comparable to a point on the other. Compare percentage changes, where the two usually land close together.

What is an SME listing and should a retail investor buy into one?

It is a listing on the platform for smaller companies, with lighter entry norms and a lot size that keeps the ticket large. Volumes are thin, disclosures less extensive than on the main board and price swings violent, so the risk profile differs materially.

Does the Sensex account for dividends?

The headline index is a price index, capturing price movement alone. Separate total return versions add dividends back, and those are the fairer benchmark for a fund's performance over long periods.
