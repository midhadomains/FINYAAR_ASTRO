ROE (Return on Equity)

ROE (Return on Equity) is a profitability ratio that measures how much net profit a company generates for every rupee of shareholders' equity it holds. Expressed as a percentage, it answers a simple owner question: how hard is the money that belongs to the shareholders actually working? A company with net profit of ₹15 crore on shareholders' equity of ₹100 crore has an ROE of 15 percent. Higher is generally better, but ROE must always be read together with how much debt the company carries, because borrowing can push the number up without the underlying business improving at all.

What ROE measures

ROE links the profit shown at the bottom of the profit and loss statement to the owners' capital shown on the balance sheet. Shareholders' equity is what remains after every liability is subtracted from total assets, so it is genuinely the owners' stake in the business. ROE therefore tells you the annual return the company earned on that stake, in the same way a fixed deposit rate tells you the return on money parked in a bank.

Because it isolates the owners' capital, ROE is one of the cleanest single measures of business quality. A firm that consistently earns a high return on the equity entrusted to it is compounding shareholder wealth from within, without needing to raise fresh capital. This is why long-term investors treat sustained high ROE as a hallmark of a strong franchise.

The formula

The core calculation is straightforward.

Net profit is the profit after tax and after all interest costs, the same figure used to compute EPS. Shareholders' equity is share capital plus reserves and surplus. Many analysts use average equity, the mean of the opening and closing balances for the year, because profit is earned across the whole period while the closing equity figure is a single snapshot. For a first pass, closing equity is acceptable.

ROE is usually stated as a percentage, so the raw ratio is multiplied by 100. A value of 0.18 becomes an ROE of 18 percent.

What a good ROE looks like and the debt caveat

As a broad guide for Indian companies, a sustained ROE of 15 percent or more is considered healthy, and a business that holds ROE above 20 percent for many years is usually a high-quality compounder. Below 10 percent, the company may be earning less than its own cost of capital, meaning it is not creating much value for owners.

The critical caveat is debt. ROE can be inflated simply by replacing equity with borrowing. If a company buys back shares or funds itself with loans, equity shrinks, and the same profit is divided by a smaller number, lifting ROE mechanically. That higher ROE carries higher financial risk, not higher business quality. For this reason ROE should always be read alongside the debt-to-equity ratio from the balance sheet. A high ROE built on heavy debt is fragile; a high ROE with little or no debt is the genuine article.

The DuPont breakdown

The DuPont method splits ROE into three drivers, so you can see exactly where the return comes from.

DriverWhat it captures
Net marginProfit earned per rupee of sales — pricing power and cost control
Asset turnoverSales generated per rupee of assets — operating efficiency
Financial leverageAssets funded per rupee of equity — how much debt is used

The breakdown is powerful because two companies can report the same ROE for very different reasons. One may earn it through fat margins and no debt; another may earn it through thin margins propped up by heavy leverage. DuPont exposes which is which, and it is why analysts pair ROE with margin measures such as EBITDA when judging quality.

Worked example
Two Indian firms each report net profit of ₹60 crore for the year. SteadyMakers Ltd funds itself almost entirely with equity, while LeverCo Ltd uses a large loan. Compute the ROE of each and decide which business is genuinely stronger.

Step 1 — Gather the equity figures. Both firms earn the same ₹60 crore net profit, so profit alone cannot separate them. From the balance sheet, SteadyMakers holds shareholders' equity of ₹400 crore with negligible debt. LeverCo holds shareholders' equity of just ₹150 crore, because a ₹350 crore loan funds much of its assets.

Step 2 — Apply the formula. ROE equals net profit divided by shareholders' equity, expressed as a percentage.

CompanyNet profitShareholders' equityDebtROE
SteadyMakers Ltd₹60 cr₹400 crNegligible15.0%
LeverCo Ltd₹60 cr₹150 cr₹350 cr40.0%

Step 3 — Read the numbers honestly. On the headline ratio, LeverCo looks spectacular: an ROE of 40 percent dwarfs SteadyMakers at 15 percent. A careless investor would conclude LeverCo is the far better business. But the two firms earn identical profit. LeverCo's superior ROE comes entirely from its small equity base, which exists only because a large loan sits underneath it.

Step 4 — Bring in the debt-to-equity ratio. SteadyMakers has a debt-to-equity ratio near zero. LeverCo has debt-to-equity of ₹350 crore divided by ₹150 crore, or 2.3 times. That leverage magnifies returns in good years, but the interest bill is fixed. If profit falls in a weak year, LeverCo must still service ₹350 crore of debt, and its ROE can collapse or turn negative far faster than SteadyMakers.

So what? ROE on its own would have rewarded the riskier company. Once you pair it with the debt-to-equity ratio, the picture reverses: SteadyMakers earns a solid 15 percent purely from operations, while LeverCo's 40 percent is a leveraged illusion. The quality judgement belongs to SteadyMakers. This is exactly why ROE is never read alone, and why the DuPont breakdown separates real operating return from the financial-leverage effect.

Check yourself
Q
A company reports ROE of 35 percent, but its debt-to-equity ratio is 3 times. Should you treat the high ROE as a clear sign of quality?
A: No. A debt-to-equity of 3 times means the ROE is heavily inflated by borrowing. The 35 percent figure reflects financial leverage more than business strength, and the company faces high risk if profits fall and interest still must be paid. Read the ROE through the DuPont breakdown and compare it with a low-debt peer before judging quality.

Key takeaways

  • ROE equals net profit divided by shareholders' equity, showing the return earned on the owners' money.
  • A sustained ROE of 15 percent or more with low debt usually signals a quality business.
  • Debt can inflate ROE by shrinking the equity base, so it must be read with the debt-to-equity ratio.
  • The DuPont breakdown splits ROE into net margin, asset turnover and financial leverage.
  • Use average equity for accuracy, and never judge a company on ROE in isolation.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good ROE for an Indian company?

A sustained ROE of 15 percent or more is generally considered healthy, and above 20 percent for many years marks a high-quality business. Below 10 percent, the company may be earning less than its cost of capital.

Why can a high ROE be misleading?

Because debt shrinks the equity base, dividing the same profit by a smaller number and lifting ROE mechanically. A high ROE built on heavy borrowing reflects risk, not business strength, so always check the debt-to-equity ratio.

What is the difference between ROE and ROCE?

ROE measures return on shareholders' equity alone, while return on capital employed measures return on equity plus debt together. ROCE is less distorted by leverage, so comparing the two helps reveal how much of the ROE comes from borrowing.

Should I use opening, closing or average equity?

Average equity, the mean of the opening and closing balances, is the most accurate because profit is earned across the whole year. Closing equity is acceptable for a quick calculation but can distort the ratio after a large capital change.

How does ROE relate to valuation?

Companies with consistently high ROE often trade at a higher P/E ratio, because the market pays more for businesses that compound owner capital efficiently. ROE helps explain why two firms with similar profits can command very different valuations.

Can ROE be negative?

Yes. If a company makes a net loss, ROE turns negative. It can also become distorted or meaningless when shareholders' equity is very small or negative due to accumulated losses, so treat such figures with caution.
