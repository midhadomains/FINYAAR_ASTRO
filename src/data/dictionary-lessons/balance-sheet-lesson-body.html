Balance Sheet

Balance Sheet is a financial statement that reports what a company owns and what it owes at a single point in time, together with the residual value belonging to its owners. It is built on one unbreakable rule: total assets must equal total liabilities plus shareholders' equity. Unlike the income statement, which covers a period, the balance sheet is a snapshot taken on one specific date, such as 31 March. It tells you how a business is financed, how liquid it is, and how much of it truly belongs to the shareholders.

The accounting equation

Every balance sheet is an expression of a single identity. The company reports economic resources it controls (assets), obligations it owes (liabilities), and the residual interest of its owners (shareholders' equity). Liabilities include trade payables and other obligations as well as borrowings. Because every rupee of resource must have a source, the two sides can never disagree.

Rearranged, equity is the accounting residual after recognised liabilities are deducted from recognised assets, rather than a guaranteed liquidation payout: Equity = Assets − Liabilities. This is why equity is often called net worth or book value. When a company earns a profit and retains it, equity rises; when it makes a loss or pays a large dividend, equity falls. The statement is said to balance because it is prepared by double-entry bookkeeping, where total debits equal total credits and the accounting equation remains balanced. A transaction can change two accounts on the same side, such as buying equipment with cash.

The main line items

Assets and liabilities are generally split into current and non-current. Current classification considers the normal operating cycle as well as the twelve-month horizon; operating-cycle inventory, receivables and payables can be current even when the cycle exceeds a year. Trading items and restrictions on cash or settlement also matter. This split is the heart of how the statement is read.

  • Current assets — cash, bank balances, receivables from customers, inventory, and short-term investments.
  • Non-current assets — property, plant and equipment, long-term investments, and intangibles such as goodwill.
  • Current liabilities — trade payables, short-term borrowings, and dues payable within a year.
  • Non-current liabilities — long-term loans, debentures, and deferred tax.
  • Shareholders' equity — paid-up share capital plus reserves and retained earnings.

What investors read from it

Investors treat the balance sheet as a health check. They look for three things. First, leverage: how much debt sits against equity, because heavy borrowing raises returns in good years but threatens survival in bad ones. Second, liquidity: whether current assets comfortably cover current liabilities, which the current ratio measures. Third, asset quality: whether reported assets are real and productive, or padded with stale inventory and doubtful receivables. A strong balance sheet gives a business room to invest, borrow, and endure a downturn. It also feeds ratios such as return on equity, which measures profit earned on the book value of owners' capital.

How it links to the other statements

The balance sheet does not stand alone. Profit from the income statement flows into retained earnings and lifts equity, so earnings per share and the price multiple in the P/E ratio both trace back to values that settle here. The cash-flow statement explains how the cash line moved between two balance sheet dates. Operating profit measures such as EBITDA are read alongside balance sheet debt to judge whether borrowings are affordable. In short, the balance sheet is the anchor that the other statements connect into. You can explore the wider cluster from the accounting and financial statements hub.

Worked example
In this hypothetical example, Sunrise Textiles Limited reports the summarised balance sheet below as on 31 March 2026. Read it, then compute the current ratio and the debt-to-equity ratio, and comment on what they say about the company.

Step 1 — Lay out the balance sheet. First confirm the statement balances. Total assets should equal total liabilities plus equity, otherwise a figure is missing.

ItemAmount (₹ lakh)
Cash and bank40
Trade receivables60
Inventory50
Current assets150
Plant and equipment250
Total assets400
Trade payables45
Short-term borrowings30
Current liabilities75
Long-term loan125
Shareholders' equity200
Total liabilities and equity400

Step 2 — Check the identity. Total assets are ₹400 lakh. Total liabilities are ₹75 lakh current plus ₹125 lakh long-term, which is ₹200 lakh, and equity is ₹200 lakh. Liabilities plus equity equal ₹400 lakh, matching assets. The statement balances, so the numbers are internally consistent.

Step 3 — Current ratio. The current ratio measures short-term liquidity: can the company meet bills due within a year using assets that convert to cash within a year?

A ratio of 2.0 means current assets are twice current liabilities. This suggests a buffer at book values, but payment capacity depends on when receivables are collected, whether inventory can be sold, and when liabilities fall due. Compare the ratio with industry peers and prior periods.

Step 4 — Debt-to-equity ratio. This compares borrowed money against owners' capital. Here debt is the interest-bearing borrowing: short-term borrowings of ₹30 lakh plus the long-term loan of ₹125 lakh, giving ₹155 lakh.

At 0.78 the company owes about 78 paise of debt for every rupee of equity. Whether that level is sustainable depends on cash flows, interest costs, repayment dates, industry conditions and other obligations. The ratio alone does not establish low distress risk.

So what? Sunrise has current assets twice its current liabilities and interest-bearing debt equal to about 78 percent of equity. These are useful starting points, not proof that it can pay every bill or safely borrow more. Review cash flows, receivable ageing, inventory quality, loan terms and the notes before assessing financial resilience.

Check yourself
Q
A company has current assets of ₹90 lakh and current liabilities of ₹60 lakh. What is its current ratio, and is it able to cover short-term dues?
A: Current ratio = 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5. Current assets exceed current liabilities at book values. This does not guarantee timely payment: asset quality, cash conversion and the timing of dues still matter.

Key takeaways

  • A balance sheet is a snapshot on one date, not a record of a period.
  • It always balances because Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity.
  • Current classification considers the normal operating cycle, the twelve-month horizon and other classification criteria.
  • Investors read it for leverage, liquidity, and asset quality.
  • It connects to the income statement through retained earnings and to the cash-flow statement through the cash line.

Frequently asked questions

Why does a balance sheet always balance?

Double-entry bookkeeping records equal total debits and credits, preserving Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Transactions need not change both sides: buying equipment with cash changes two asset accounts. A balanced statement can still contain errors or misleading valuations.

What is the difference between current and non-current items?

Current classification considers the normal operating cycle and the twelve-month horizon, with additional criteria for trading items, cash restrictions and settlement rights. Operating-cycle items can remain current even beyond twelve months. Items that do not meet current classification criteria are non-current.

What is shareholders' equity?

It is the residual value left for owners after all liabilities are deducted from assets. It equals paid-up share capital plus reserves and retained earnings, and is also called net worth or book value.

Is the balance sheet the same as book value?

No. The balance sheet is the whole statement; its equity is the book value attributable to owners. It reflects accounting figures, which can differ from market value, since the market prices future prospects that the book does not capture.

How is the balance sheet different from the income statement?

The balance sheet is a snapshot at one date showing what a company owns and owes. The income statement covers a period and shows revenues, expenses, and profit earned during it.

What ratios come from the balance sheet?

The current ratio measures liquidity, and the debt-to-equity ratio measures leverage. Combined with profit figures it also feeds return on equity and other performance measures.
