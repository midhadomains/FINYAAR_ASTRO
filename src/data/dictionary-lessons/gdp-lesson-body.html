What GDP measures

GDP measures production of final goods and services, or the value added at each stage, so intermediate inputs are not counted twice. If a mill supplies cloth to a shirt maker and the finished shirt is sold to a customer, adding both the cloth sale and the shirt’s full value would double-count the cloth. National accounts avoid that by measuring final expenditure or value added.

Domestic refers to location, not ownership. Output from a foreign-owned factory in Chennai contributes to India’s GDP, while production at an Indian-owned factory abroad contributes to the host country’s GDP. GDP also covers only production in the measurement period. A used car’s resale value is not new output, although a dealer’s newly provided service can count.

India’s National Statistical Office (NSO), under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI), publishes annual and quarterly national-accounts estimates. In February 2026, MoSPI introduced a new GDP series with 2022–23 as the base year, replacing the 2011–12 series. Estimates are revised as more complete information becomes available.

Imports are subtracted because they may already appear in C, I or G but were produced outside the country.

Official tables use finer national-accounting categories such as private and government final consumption expenditure, gross fixed capital formation, changes in stocks, valuables and discrepancies. The four-part formula is an educational summary, not the full publication layout. Buying shares or bonds is a financial transaction and is not itself counted as GDP investment.

Nominal GDP, real GDP and growth

Nominal GDP, also called GDP at current prices, reflects both changes in output quantities and changes in prices. Real GDP, or GDP at constant prices, removes price effects so that production volumes can be compared across periods. A GDP deflator can be expressed as the ratio of nominal GDP to real GDP:

Real GDP growth compares real GDP between two periods. A single year’s nominal GDP and deflator can produce a real GDP level, but cannot by themselves produce a growth rate; that requires a comparable earlier-period real GDP figure. Official constant-price estimates are constructed from detailed sector and product data rather than merely deflating one aggregate number.

Two consecutive quarterly declines are sometimes called a “technical recession,” but recession assessment is broader and can depend on whether figures are seasonally adjusted, which comparison is used and what other indicators show.

Why GDP matters—and what it misses

GDP helps policymakers, businesses and investors assess the economy’s scale and direction. Faster real growth can coincide with stronger employment, incomes and company revenue, but the relationship is neither immediate nor uniform across sectors. GDP data can influence expectations for earnings, bond yields and the repo rate, yet markets respond to surprises and future expectations rather than the GDP figure alone.

GDP is not a complete measure of wellbeing. It does not reveal how income is distributed, and it omits or poorly captures unpaid household work, leisure, environmental depletion and many quality-of-life changes. Population growth also matters, so real GDP per capita can be more informative than aggregate GDP when discussing living standards. Read it alongside inflation, labour-market data and broader social and environmental measures.

Worked example
An illustrative economy reports current-price expenditure of ₹80 lakh crore in consumption, ₹30 lakh crore in investment, ₹20 lakh crore in government spending, ₹22 lakh crore in exports and ₹27 lakh crore in imports. Its GDP deflator is 110 with the base-year index equal to 100. Calculate nominal and real GDP. Can these figures alone tell us the real GDP growth rate?

Step 1 — Calculate net exports. X − M = ₹22 − ₹27 = −₹5 lakh crore. The negative figure means imports exceed exports; it does not by itself mean imports make the country poorer.

Step 2 — Calculate nominal GDP. C + I + G + (X − M) = 80 + 30 + 20 − 5 = ₹125 lakh crore.

ComponentCurrent-price value (₹ lakh crore)
Consumption80
Investment30
Government20
Net exports−5
Nominal GDP125

Step 3 — Convert to a real level. Real GDP = 125 ÷ (110 ÷ 100) = 125 ÷ 1.10 = approximately ₹113.6 lakh crore at base-year prices.

Step 4 — Interpret carefully. The deflator of 110 means the aggregate current-price level is 10% above the base-year price level under this simplified setup. The ₹11.4 lakh crore numerical gap between nominal and real GDP reflects the valuation difference between current and base-year prices; it is not a separately spendable “inflation amount.”

Step 5 — Do not invent a growth rate. We need the previous period’s comparable real GDP to calculate real growth. For example, if prior real GDP were ₹110 lakh crore, growth would be (113.6 − 110) ÷ 110 × 100, or about 3.3%. Without that prior figure, no growth rate can be inferred.

So what? Always distinguish a GDP level from a GDP growth rate, and check whether figures are at current or constant prices. The original inputs establish today’s nominal and real levels only.

Check yourself
An economy has nominal GDP of ₹150 lakh crore and a GDP deflator of 120. What is its real GDP, and can you calculate its real GDP growth rate without another period’s figure?
A: Real GDP = 150 ÷ (120 ÷ 100) = ₹125 lakh crore at base-year prices. You cannot calculate real GDP growth without a comparable real GDP figure for an earlier period.

Key takeaways

  • GDP measures final production within a country during a specified period.
  • The simplified expenditure identity is C + I + G + (X − M); official accounts use more detailed categories.
  • Nominal GDP uses current prices, while real GDP removes price effects for comparison across time.
  • India’s current GDP series uses 2022–23 as its base year and includes annual and quarterly estimates.
  • GDP is useful for measuring economic activity but does not by itself measure distribution or wellbeing.

Frequently asked questions

What is GDP in simple terms?

It is the value of final goods and services produced inside a country during a specified period.

How do nominal and real GDP differ?

Nominal GDP uses current prices; real GDP removes price effects to compare production volumes across periods.

What are the four simplified expenditure components?

Consumption, investment, government spending and net exports.

Why are imports subtracted?

They may appear in domestic spending totals but were produced abroad, so subtraction leaves domestic production.

Does rising GDP mean everyone is better off?

No. GDP omits distribution and many dimensions of welfare, so it should be paired with other indicators.

Who calculates India’s GDP and what is the base year?

The NSO under MoSPI compiles it. The current series introduced in 2026 uses 2022–23 as its base year.
