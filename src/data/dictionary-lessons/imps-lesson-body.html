IMPS

IMPS (Immediate Payment Service) is the National Payments Corporation of India's interbank system for moving money between accounts within seconds, at any hour of any day. Launched publicly in November 2010, it was the first Indian rail to make round-the-clock retail transfers ordinary. It can address a beneficiary by account number and IFSC or by a mobile number paired with a seven-digit MMID, and it works through mobile banking, net banking, ATMs and branches. The RBI raised the per-transaction ceiling to ₹5,00,000 in 2021, and each bank sets its own channel limits within that.

Instant credit sitting on deferred settlement

Two things happen when an IMPS transfer goes through, and only one of them is instant. Your bank debits you and sends a message through the NPCI switch to the beneficiary bank, which credits the account at once on the strength of that message. The money itself moves between the two banks afterwards, netted through NPCI, so the beneficiary sees the funds long before the banks have squared up. That split is what lets IMPS work at three in the morning on a public holiday, when neither an NEFT batch nor an RTGS counter suits someone sending a few thousand rupees.

When a credit fails the rules are specific. The beneficiary bank must reverse the transaction automatically no later than the day after it was made, and if it does not, the customer is owed ₹100 for every day the reversal is late, paid without any complaint. Authentication varies by channel: the app relies on your MPIN, an ATM on the card and its PIN.

Two ways to address the money

The mobile and MMID route has no equivalent in the older systems. An MMID is a seven-digit code your bank issues against a mobile number registered with your account, the first four digits identifying the bank. Share the number and the MMID and someone can pay you without ever seeing your account number.

Limits, and the arithmetic of splitting a payment

The RBI ceiling of ₹5,00,000 per transaction is an outer boundary, not what your bank will let you do. Most banks set a lower cap on the app, another on net banking and a lower one again at an ATM, with a daily cumulative limit on top. Exceed the per-transaction cap and the payment simply becomes several payments.

n is the number of transfers you must make, A is the total to be sent, and L is the per-transaction limit on that channel. The brackets mean round up, because a part transfer is still a transfer. Sending ₹1,60,000 where the app allows ₹50,000 needs four attempts, each charged separately if your savings account attracts an IMPS fee.

Why IMPS survived the arrival of UPI

UPI took over everyday small payments so completely that IMPS can look redundant, yet the two are not interchangeable. UPI needs an app, a handle and a working smartphone; IMPS runs from net banking, an ATM or a branch, which matters when a phone is lost. Bank limits on a single IMPS transfer are often higher than the usual UPI person-to-person cap.

For a household keeping an emergency fund in a bank account, the practical point is to know both routes work and to check your own limits before the day you need them.

Worked example
At 23:40 on a Sunday, Meera Nair in Kochi gets a call from Ranchi: her sister has been admitted and the hospital wants a deposit of ₹85,000 before the night is out. Meera has the money in her savings account. Her bank's mobile app allows ₹50,000 per IMPS transaction with a cumulative daily limit of ₹2,00,000, and its published tariff levies ₹5 plus GST on transfers in that band. How does she get the money there, and what does it cost?

Step 1, working out how many transfers. The amount she needs to send is ₹85,000 and the per-transaction limit is ₹50,000. Dividing gives 1.7, and rounding up gives two transfers. She sends ₹50,000 first and ₹35,000 immediately after, both well inside the ₹2,00,000 she is allowed for the day.

Step 2, what she pays. Each transfer attracts ₹5 plus GST at 18 percent, which is ₹5.90, so the whole ₹85,000 costs her ₹11.80. Splitting the payment doubled the fee, which is a real cost but a trivial one here; the same split on twenty transfers a month would be worth avoiding by raising her limit or using net banking, where the cap is usually higher.

Step 3, why she does not wait for morning. A branch transfer is impossible at that hour and an NEFT would sit until the next batch settled before the receiving bank started its own two-hour clock. IMPS credits the hospital account within seconds of each instruction, which is the only property of the rail that matters to her tonight.

Step 4, what happens if a leg fails. Suppose the second transfer is debited but not credited because of a technical failure at the other bank. That bank must reverse it automatically by the following day. Should the reversal slip past that deadline, Meera is owed ₹100 for each day of delay, credited to her without any complaint from her side.

So what? No branch was open, no batch was due to settle, and the hospital had its money before midnight for under twelve rupees. The per-transaction limit shaped how the payment was made, not whether it could be made at all, and knowing that limit in advance is what turns a panic into two taps.

Check yourself
Q
Your IMPS transfer is debited but never credited to the payee. What are you entitled to?
A: The beneficiary bank must reverse it automatically no later than the day after the transaction. If the reversal is late you are owed ₹100 for every day of delay, paid without you filing a complaint.

Key takeaways

  • IMPS credits the beneficiary in seconds while the banks settle later, which is what makes it work at any hour.
  • It can route on a mobile number plus an MMID, not only on an account number and IFSC.
  • The RBI ceiling is ₹5,00,000 per transaction, but the binding limit is almost always your own bank's channel cap.
  • Charges are bank-specific rather than waived by regulation, so splitting a payment multiplies the fee.
  • A failed IMPS credit must be auto-reversed by the next day, with ₹100 a day payable for delay beyond that.

Frequently asked questions

What is an MMID and where do I find mine?

A seven-digit code your bank issues against the mobile number registered with your account, the first four digits identifying the bank. Most banks show it in the app or send it by SMS on request.

Is IMPS free?

That depends on your bank. No rule waives IMPS charges the way online NEFT charges were waived, so banks publish their own slabs and several charge nothing on small transfers.

How much can I send through IMPS in a day?

The RBI caps a single transaction at ₹5,00,000, and your bank sets the per-transaction and daily limits within that, usually differing between the app, net banking and an ATM.

What is the difference between IMPS and UPI?

UPI is addressed by a handle or mobile number through an app and is generally free person to person; IMPS is addressed by account and IFSC or by mobile and MMID, works without a smartphone app, and may carry a bank fee.

Can I use IMPS without internet banking?

Yes. IMPS is reachable from ATMs and branches, and several banks support it through mobile banking without a data connection. The channel determines the limit and the authentication method.

Does IMPS work on Sundays and bank holidays?

Yes, it runs continuously on all days of the year, which was its original point of difference. Only your bank's maintenance windows interrupt it.
