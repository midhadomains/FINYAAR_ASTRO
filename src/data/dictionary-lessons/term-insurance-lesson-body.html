Term Insurance

Term Insurance is life cover for a specified period. If the insured person dies while an in-force policy covers the event, the insurer pays the contractual death benefit to the eligible claimant or beneficiary. A standard plan normally has no survival benefit, which generally lets it provide more death cover per rupee of premium than policies that combine insurance with savings.

What term insurance actually is

Term insurance is a comparatively simple form of life cover. You select a sum assured, policy term, premium-payment structure and payout option. If the insured person dies during the covered term while the policy is in force, the claim is assessed under the policy wording and applicable law. If the insured survives, a standard policy pays no maturity value. Return-of-premium variants cost more and return only the premium components specified in the contract.

Prices vary widely with age, health, tobacco use, occupation, policy term, payment term, medical underwriting, riders and insurer. Illustrative online quotes should never be treated as guaranteed until underwriting is complete. Eligible premiums can qualify within the combined Section 80C limit under the old tax regime, subject to current conditions. Term insurance can complement health insurance, but the two protect against different risks.

How much cover you need

The popular 10-to-15-times-income rule is only a first-pass check. A needs-based calculation is better: estimate the present value of income your dependants require, add outstanding loans, education, caregiving and final expenses, allow for inflation, and subtract suitable liquid assets and existing cover. Do not subtract retirement assets your family still needs for another goal without considering the trade-off.

Someone earning ₹12 lakh with a ₹40 lakh home loan and young dependants may need substantially more than a simple income multiple suggests. Review the calculation after marriage, childbirth, a new loan, a material income change or a decline in dependants’ needs. A payout also needs an investment and withdrawal plan; no single mutual fund is automatically appropriate for every part of it.

Term versus endowment and ULIP

Endowment and ULIP products combine life cover with savings or investment features. They are different products rather than automatically better or worse: guarantees, market exposure, charges, liquidity, surrender values and tax treatment vary. Separating protection and investing can provide clearer control and higher cover for the same initial outlay, but the result depends on premiums, actual investment returns, taxes, behaviour and the compared contracts.

What to check before buying

Start with the policy wording, benefit illustration, exclusions, premium-payment term, whether premiums are guaranteed, medical underwriting and claim process. IRDAI-published death-claims-paid ratios provide useful context, but the headline percentage alone does not prove that a particular future claim will be paid. Check annual ratios by number and, where available, amount; repudiated and pending claims; complaint and servicing information; solvency; and several years of data.

Choose a policy term linked to the years your dependants need support rather than automatically extending cover to the maximum age. Riders can be useful but may overlap with standalone accident, disability or health cover. Answer every proposal-form question fully and accurately, retain a copy of the submitted answers and medical reports, keep premiums current, update contact and nomination details, and tell the nominee where the policy documents are stored. Life policies are also subject to Section 45 of the Insurance Act, so simplistic statements that any non-disclosure always permits rejection are inaccurate.

Worked example
Rahul is 32 and has a spouse and child. Assume a term plan is issued at ₹12,000 a year for ₹1 crore of cover, while an endowment illustration charges ₹60,000 a year for ₹15 lakh of cover and shows an ₹18 lakh value after 25 years. If Rahul chooses term cover and invests the ₹48,000 annual difference at the beginning of each year, what does a 10% return assumption produce?

Step 1 — Compare the assumed cover. Under the stated illustrations, the term plan provides ₹1 crore during its covered term and the endowment provides ₹15 lakh. Actual benefits depend on each issued contract remaining in force and the claim being covered.

Step 2 — Identify the difference. The annual outlay difference is ₹60,000 − ₹12,000 = ₹48,000.

Step 3 — Project, without promising, the investment. If ₹48,000 is invested at the beginning of every year for 25 years and earns exactly 10% annually, the annuity-due value is approximately ₹51.9 lakh before investment costs and taxes. Real market returns will vary and can be lower or negative over some periods.

So what? Under these assumptions, separating protection and investing supplies more death cover and a higher projected investment value. This is not a universal product verdict: Rahul must compare guaranteed and non-guaranteed endowment values, actual term quotes, investment risk, taxes, charges, discipline and policy terms on a like-for-like basis.

Check yourself
A colleague says standard term insurance is a waste because there is normally no payout if the insured survives the term. How would you explain its purpose?
A: Standard term insurance is purchased to transfer the financial risk of death during the covered years, much like other protection insurance. No survival payout helps keep the premium lower for a given death benefit. Whether to invest the cost difference separately depends on the person’s goals, discipline and risk tolerance.

Key takeaways

  • Standard term insurance primarily provides a contractual death benefit during a specified policy term.
  • No survival value generally allows more death cover per rupee than bundled savings products.
  • Use a needs-based cover calculation; income multiples are only rough starting points.
  • Compare term and bundled products using issued benefits, guarantees, charges, liquidity, risk and taxes.
  • Claim ratios are context, not proof; accurate disclosure, an in-force policy and covered circumstances remain essential.

Frequently asked questions

Do I get my money back at the end of a term plan?

A standard policy generally has no maturity payout. Return-of-premium variants cost more and refund only the components defined by the contract.

How much cover should I buy?

Estimate dependant income needs, debts, future goals, final expenses and inflation, then subtract suitable assets and existing cover. Revisit the calculation after major life changes.

What does the death-claims-paid ratio tell me?

It shows the proportion of reported death claims paid using the stated basis. Also review claims by amount, repudiated and pending claims, servicing, solvency and policy wording.

Does term insurance give a tax benefit?

Eligible premiums can fall within Section 80C under the old regime, subject to the combined limit and other conditions. Qualifying death benefits are generally exempt under Section 10(10D).

When should I buy?

Buy when someone would suffer financially from your death. Applying earlier can produce a lower quote, but underwriting and policy terms determine the issued premium.

Is term insurance enough on its own?

No. It does not replace health insurance, disability planning, an emergency fund, retirement savings or estate planning.
