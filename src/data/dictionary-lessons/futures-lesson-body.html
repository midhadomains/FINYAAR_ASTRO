Futures

Futures are exchange-traded contracts that bind both the buyer and the seller to trade a standardised quantity of an underlying asset — an index, a stock, a currency or a commodity — at a price fixed today, for settlement on a set future date. Unlike a spot trade, nothing changes hands in full at the start: you post a small deposit called margin, and the position is settled a little every day. A futures contract is a firm obligation, not a choice, and it is used either to hedge an existing exposure or to speculate on price with leverage.

What a futures contract is

A futures contract is a legally binding agreement to buy or sell a fixed quantity of an underlying at an agreed price on a future date. Because it trades on an exchange, the terms are standardised: the lot size, the expiry date and the tick are all set by the exchange, so any buyer can be matched with any seller. In India the National Stock Exchange lists futures on the Nifty and other indices as well as on individual equity shares.

A clearing house sits between the two parties and guarantees the trade, so you never worry about the person on the other side defaulting. The profit of one side is the exact loss of the other — futures are a zero-sum contract before costs.

How futures differ from options

The key difference is obligation versus right. A future must be honoured or squared off before expiry — both parties are committed. An option gives its buyer only a right, never a duty: the holder of a call option or a put option can simply let it lapse and lose only the premium paid.

Because a future carries a two-sided obligation, its payoff is symmetric — you can lose far more than you put in. An option buyer, by contrast, has losses capped at the premium. That single distinction drives almost everything else about how the two instruments behave.

Margin and mark-to-market

You do not pay the full contract value to trade a future. You post initial margin — often around 10 to 20 percent of the contract value — and the exchange revalues your position at the close every day. This daily settlement is called mark-to-market: gains are credited to your account and losses are debited, each trading day, until you exit.

If losses eat into your deposit, the broker issues a margin call and you must add funds or the position is closed out. This is where leverage cuts both ways: a small deposit controls a large exposure.

Hedging versus speculation

Hedging means using a future to offset a risk you already carry. A fund holding a basket of shares can sell index futures so that a market fall is cushioned by a gain on the short future — this is hedging in practice, and the size of the hedge depends on the portfolio beta.

Speculation means taking a futures position purely to profit from an expected move, with no underlying exposure to protect. Leverage magnifies both outcomes, and higher volatility raises the stakes. SEBI studies show most individual futures and options traders lose money, so speculators must respect risk versus return. For more, see our derivatives and risk hub.

Worked example
Priya, a trader in Pune, is bullish on Nifty. On 1 August the Nifty August future trades at 24,000. One lot is 25 units. She buys one lot. Initial margin is 12 percent. By 20 August the future rises to 24,400 and she squares off. What is her profit, and how does mark-to-market work along the way?

Step 1 — Find the contract value. One lot of the Nifty future is 25 units. At 24,000 the notional value is 24,000 × 25 = ₹6,00,000. This is the exposure Priya controls.

Step 2 — Find the margin she posts. Initial margin at 12 percent is 0.12 × ₹6,00,000 = ₹72,000. Priya deposits only ₹72,000 to control a ₹6,00,000 position — leverage of roughly 8.3 times.

Step 3 — Compute the profit on exit. She bought at 24,000 and sells at 24,400, a gain of 400 points. Profit = (24,400 − 24,000) × 25 = 400 × 25 = ₹10,000.

Step 4 — See the return on margin. A profit of ₹10,000 on margin of ₹72,000 is about 13.9 percent, even though the index itself rose only about 1.7 percent. That amplification is leverage.

Step 5 — Follow the mark-to-market. The gain does not arrive in one lump at exit. Each day the exchange settles the change in price to Priya's account.

DateCloseDaily change (points)Daily cash flow
1 Aug24,000——
8 Aug23,800−200−₹5,000
14 Aug24,100+300+₹7,500
20 Aug24,400+300+₹7,500

On 8 August the position moved against her and ₹5,000 was debited that day — had her balance fallen below the maintenance level, she would have faced a margin call. The later gains were credited daily, and the cash flows sum to the net ₹10,000.

So what? The same 400-point move against Priya would have debited ₹10,000 — nearly 14 percent of her deposit — and a larger drop could have wiped out the margin entirely. Futures magnify both directions, and the daily settlement means losses hit your cash immediately, not just at expiry.

Check yourself
Q
You buy one lot of a stock future at ₹500 with a lot size of 1,000 and sell it at ₹520. Ignoring costs, what is your profit?
A: Profit = (520 − 500) × 1,000 = ₹20,000.

Key takeaways

  • A futures contract binds both parties to trade a standardised quantity at a fixed price on a future date.
  • It is exchange-traded and guaranteed by a clearing house, so counterparty default is not your concern.
  • You post margin and the position is marked to market daily, with gains and losses settled in cash each day.
  • Unlike an option, a future is an obligation — it must be honoured or squared off, and losses can exceed your deposit.
  • Futures serve either to hedge an existing exposure or to speculate with leverage, and most individual traders lose money.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to take delivery of the underlying?

Usually no. Index futures are cash-settled, and most traders square off stock futures before expiry rather than taking physical delivery, though some stock futures do settle by delivery.

Is a future riskier than an option?

For the buyer, an option caps loss at the premium, while a future has open-ended losses on both sides. Leverage makes futures unforgiving if the price moves against you.

What is a margin call?

It is a demand to add funds when daily losses pull your account below the required level. If you do not top up, the broker squares off your position.

Can I hold a future forever?

No. Every futures contract has a fixed expiry. To stay in the market you must roll over by closing the near contract and opening the next month's, which has its own cost.

What is the minimum I need to trade a future?

You need the initial margin set by the exchange, typically 10 to 20 percent of the contract value, plus a buffer for mark-to-market swings. The full notional value is not required.

Why do so many futures traders lose money?

Leverage amplifies losses, costs add up, and timing the market is hard. SEBI data shows a large majority of individual derivatives traders end up in the red.
