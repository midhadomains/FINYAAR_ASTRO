Income Statement

Income Statement is a financial statement that reports how much revenue a business earned over a period and the expenses recognised in earning that revenue, arriving at the net profit or loss for the period. In India it is often called the Profit and Loss Account, or simply the P&L, and it covers a stretch of time such as a quarter or a financial year rather than a single day.

What an income statement actually shows

An income statement answers one plain question: over the last three months or the last year, did the business make money or lose it, and why. It starts with revenue at the top and works down through the various costs until it lands on net profit at the bottom. Because it always covers a period, every income statement carries a heading such as "for the year ended 31 March 2026". That period framing is the single biggest difference between this statement and the balance sheet, which is a snapshot of one date.

Main-board equity-listed Indian companies generally report quarterly financial results and audited annual results; SME-exchange reporting follows a half-yearly framework. For a small shopkeeper in Surat or a freelance designer in Bengaluru, the same logic applies even if the format is simpler: sales minus expenses equals what is left over. Reading it well is the first skill of financial literacy, because it tells you whether the core activity of a business is genuinely profitable.

The revenue-to-net-profit flow

The statement is built as a waterfall. A simplified trading-business presentation starts with revenue and subtracts costs to show useful subtotals. Actual statement layouts vary, and other income, exceptional items or discontinued operations can add further lines. The headline logic is captured below.

Reading that flow line by line: Revenue (also called turnover or sales) is the money earned from selling goods or services. Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) is the direct cost of producing what was sold — raw material, factory labour, direct power. Subtracting COGS gives Gross Profit. Next come Operating Expenses such as salaries, rent, marketing and, importantly, depreciation. Revenue minus COGS minus operating expenses gives Operating Profit. In this simplified example it equals EBIT (earnings before interest and tax); in other presentations, non-operating income can make EBIT differ from operating profit. Depreciation may appear in production costs or other operating expenses, so do not deduct it twice. Subtract Interest on borrowings and you get profit before tax; subtract Tax and you finally reach Net Profit, the amount that belongs to the owners.

Reading margins, not just rupees

Absolute profit in rupees can mislead, because a large company will naturally post larger numbers. Margins convert each subtotal into a percentage of revenue to help compare businesses of different sizes, while still allowing for differences in industry, business model and accounting policies. The three most-watched margins are gross margin (gross profit as a share of revenue), operating margin (operating profit divided by revenue) and net margin (net profit divided by revenue).

MarginFormulaWhat it tells you
Gross marginGross profit ÷ Revenue × 100%Pricing power and production efficiency
Operating marginOperating profit ÷ Revenue × 100%Efficiency of the core business
Net marginNet profit ÷ Revenue × 100%What owners keep after everything

A falling gross margin often signals rising input costs or a price war. A healthy gross margin paired with a thin net margin usually points to heavy interest or high overheads. Comparing margins across years and against competitors is how analysts and lenders judge whether profits are durable. The bottom-line net profit also feeds ratios such as ROE, which measures profit against shareholder money.

How it differs from the other two statements

Three commonly studied statements are the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement. A complete reporting package may also include a statement of changes in equity and explanatory notes; requirements vary by entity. The income statement measures profitability over a period. The balance sheet lists assets, liabilities and equity on a single date. The cash flow statement tracks the actual cash moving in and out, which can differ sharply from profit because the income statement uses accrual accounting — it records a sale when it is earned, not when the cash arrives. A company can therefore report a healthy net profit yet run short of cash if customers have not paid. This is why you never judge a business on the income statement alone; you read all three together.

Worked example
In this hypothetical example, Priya Nair manages Sundar Textiles Private Limited, a garment trading company in Kochi. For the financial year ended 31 March 2026 she recorded revenue of ₹80,00,000. Her cost of goods sold was ₹48,00,000. Operating expenses (salaries, shop rent, marketing and depreciation) totalled ₹18,00,000. She paid ₹3,00,000 as interest on a business loan, and assume income-tax expense of ₹2,75,000 for this illustration, rather than a calculation under a specific tax regime. Build her income statement and find the gross, operating and net margins.

Step 1 — Start with revenue and find gross profit. Revenue is ₹80,00,000. Subtract COGS of ₹48,00,000. Gross Profit = ₹80,00,000 − ₹48,00,000 = ₹32,00,000.

Step 2 — Subtract operating expenses to get operating profit (EBIT). Operating Profit = ₹32,00,000 − ₹18,00,000 = ₹14,00,000.

Step 3 — Subtract interest to get profit before tax. Profit before tax = ₹14,00,000 − ₹3,00,000 = ₹11,00,000.

Step 4 — Subtract tax to get net profit. Net Profit = ₹11,00,000 − ₹2,75,000 = ₹8,25,000.

Step 5 — Compute the three margins. Gross margin = 32,00,000 ÷ 80,00,000 = 40%. Operating margin = 14,00,000 ÷ 80,00,000 = 17.5%. Net margin = 8,25,000 ÷ 80,00,000 = 10.3%.

So what? The company earns 40 paise of gross profit on every rupee of sales; assessing that margin requires comparison with relevant peers and prior years. But by the time rent, salaries, interest and tax are paid, about 10 paise per rupee remains as accounting net profit, not necessarily cash available to Priya. If next year her rent rises or the loan interest climbs, that 10% net margin can shrink fast even if sales stay flat. The income statement shows her exactly where money leaks out — and tells her that changes in pricing, sales mix, operating costs and financing costs can each affect profitability.

Check yourself
Q
A company reports revenue of ₹50 lakh, COGS of ₹30 lakh and operating expenses of ₹12 lakh. What is its operating profit and operating margin?
A: Gross profit = 50 − 30 = ₹20 lakh. Operating profit = 20 − 12 = ₹8 lakh. Operating margin = 8 ÷ 50 = 16%.

Key takeaways

  • The income statement (P&L) reports profit or loss over a period, unlike the balance sheet which is a snapshot of one date.
  • It flows top-down: revenue minus COGS gives gross profit, minus operating expenses gives EBIT, minus interest and tax gives net profit.
  • Margins express profit as a percentage of revenue; comparisons still need industry and accounting context.
  • It uses accrual accounting, so reported profit can differ from actual cash — always read it alongside the cash flow statement.
  • Net profit is the number that flows into ratios like ROE and into what owners can reinvest or distribute.

Frequently asked questions

Is the income statement the same as the Profit and Loss Account in India?

Yes. In Indian company law and everyday usage the income statement is called the Statement of Profit and Loss, or simply the P&L Account. The name differs but the content — revenue down to net profit for a period — is identical.

What is the difference between gross profit and net profit?

Gross profit is revenue minus only the direct cost of goods sold, so it reflects production and pricing efficiency. Net profit is what remains after operating expenses, interest and tax are also removed — the final amount that belongs to the owners.

Why can a profitable company still run out of cash?

Because the income statement records sales when they are earned, not when cash arrives. If customers buy on credit and pay late, a firm can show strong net profit on paper while its bank balance runs dry. The cash flow statement and working-capital disclosures help explain this gap; investment spending and loan repayments can also absorb cash.

What does EBIT mean on an income statement?

EBIT means earnings before interest and tax. It equals operating profit in this simplified example, but can differ when non-operating income or expenses are included. Check the calculation and reconcile the measure before comparing companies.

How often must an Indian listed company publish its income statement?

Under the usual main-board equity-listing framework, quarterly results are due within 45 days of quarter-end, except the final quarter; audited annual results are due within 60 days of financial year-end. SME-exchange entities generally report half-yearly. Check applicable rules and any extensions for the entity and period.

Does depreciation appear on the income statement even though it is not a cash outflow?

Yes. Depreciation allocates an asset's depreciable amount over its useful life and affects profit through production costs or other expenses. The depreciation charge itself is non-cash; the asset purchase may still involve cash in the same year. Under the indirect cash-flow method, depreciation included in profit is added back when reconciling operating cash flow.
