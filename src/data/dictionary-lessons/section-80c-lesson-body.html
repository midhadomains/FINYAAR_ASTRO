Section 80C

Section 80C is a provision of the Income Tax Act that lets an individual or Hindu Undivided Family reduce taxable income by up to ₹1.5 lakh in a financial year through approved investments and expenses. It is one of the most widely used tax breaks in India, but it applies only under the old tax regime. Popular Section 80C deductions include the Public Provident Fund, ELSS mutual funds, employee provident fund contributions, life-insurance premiums, and home-loan principal repayment.

What Section 80C is

Section 80C is a deduction, not an exemption or a rebate. It works by subtracting up to ₹1.5 lakh of qualifying spending and investment from your gross total income before tax is calculated. The tax you save therefore depends on your slab: a person in the 30 per cent bracket who fully uses the limit can save up to ₹45,000 in tax before cess, while someone in the 5 per cent bracket can save up to ₹7,500 on the same ₹1.5 lakh, depending on how the deduction changes income across slabs.

The ₹1.5 lakh ceiling is a combined cap. Sections 80C, 80CCC (pension plans) and 80CCD(1) (eligible contribution to the National Pension System) share the same ₹1.5 lakh room, so you cannot claim each separately. The deduction is available to individuals and Hindu Undivided Families, although eligibility for particular items can differ. Because it lowers taxable income at the top of your slab, Section 80C is generally worth more to higher earners who choose the old regime.

Which investments and expenses qualify

Section 80C covers a mix of long-term savings instruments and everyday expenses. Some options build wealth, some protect your family, and some reward eligible spending you may already make, such as repaying a home loan or paying school tuition. The old-versus-new regime catch

This is the point most people miss. Section 80C deductions are available under the old tax regime only. The new tax regime, which is now the default in India, offers a different slab structure but strips away most deductions, including Section 80C. So an eligible investment in ELSS or PPF does not reduce taxable income under the new regime.

Choosing between regimes is therefore an annual calculation for taxpayers who are allowed to switch each year. If your Section 80C claims, together with other old-regime deductions such as eligible home-loan interest and health-insurance premiums, are large, the old regime may leave more money in your pocket. If you claim few deductions, the new regime may win. One more layer sits outside the Section 80C cap: an eligible NPS contribution can provide an additional deduction of up to ₹50,000 under Section 80CCD(1B), over and above the combined ₹1.5 lakh limit, under the old regime.

How to use the limit well

Before rushing to invest fresh money, check what already fills the limit. Your eligible EPF contribution, life-insurance premiums, home-loan principal and children’s tuition fees may consume most of the ₹1.5 lakh without any new outflow. Only the remaining gap needs a deliberate investment.

For that gap, match the instrument to your goal and risk appetite. ELSS carries the shortest statutory lock-in among common 80C investments and offers equity-linked growth, but it comes with market volatility, and gains can attract capital gains tax. PPF and Sukanya Samriddhi offer government-set returns with long lock-ins. A blend that respects your goals usually beats chasing the single highest headline return. Worked example
Riya earns a taxable income of ₹12 lakh before her Section 80C claim and has chosen the old tax regime. Her salary already routes ₹40,000 a year into her EPF account, and she pays an eligible ₹20,000 life-insurance premium. She wants to fill the full ₹1.5 lakh Section 80C limit and understand the tax she saves. Her marginal rate on the affected income is 30 per cent. How should she allocate the remaining room, and what is the benefit?

Step 1 — Count what already counts. Riya does not start from zero. Her eligible EPF contribution of ₹40,000 and life-insurance premium of ₹20,000 both count under Section 80C. Together they use ₹60,000 of the ceiling, leaving a gap of ₹1,50,000 − ₹60,000 = ₹90,000.

Step 2 — Choose instruments for the gap. Riya wants some growth and some safety. She decides to put ₹50,000 into an ELSS fund for equity exposure with a three-year lock-in, and ₹40,000 into her PPF account for government-set returns. This closes the ₹90,000 gap and takes her total Section 80C claim to the full ₹1.5 lakh.

Step 3 — Compute the tax saved. The deduction removes ₹1.5 lakh from Riya’s taxable income. Because all of the affected income falls within her 30 per cent marginal slab in this illustration, the tax saved is 30 per cent of ₹1,50,000, or ₹45,000. Adding the applicable 4 per cent health and education cess, the illustrative saving is ₹45,000 × 1.04 = ₹46,800.

Step 4 — Add the NPS top-up. Riya can go further. If she makes an eligible ₹50,000 NPS contribution under Section 80CCD(1B), it sits outside the combined ₹1.5 lakh cap. At the assumed marginal rate, that produces an additional illustrative saving of ₹15,000 before cess. Her combined deduction becomes ₹2 lakh.

So what? Of Riya’s ₹1.5 lakh Section 80C claim, only ₹90,000 was fresh money, yet the whole eligible amount reduced her taxable income. Counting existing commitments first stopped her from over-investing, while the separate NPS deduction added room the Section 80C limit alone could not provide.

Check yourself
Under which tax regime can you claim Section 80C deductions, and what is the maximum combined amount?
A: Section 80C deductions are available under the old tax regime, up to a combined maximum of ₹1.5 lakh per financial year under Sections 80C, 80CCC and 80CCD(1). The new tax regime does not allow Section 80C deductions.

Key takeaways

  • Section 80C allows eligible taxpayers a deduction within a combined limit of up to ₹1.5 lakh per financial year.
  • It is available under the old tax regime, not the new regime.
  • Qualifying items can include PPF, ELSS, EPF contributions, eligible life-insurance premiums, tax-saver fixed deposits, eligible home-loan principal, NSC, Sukanya Samriddhi and qualifying children’s tuition fees.
  • The actual tax saving depends on how the deduction changes income across the applicable tax slabs.
  • An eligible NPS contribution can add a separate deduction of up to ₹50,000 under Section 80CCD(1B), over and above the combined limit.

Frequently asked questions

Is Section 80C available under the new tax regime?

No. Section 80C deductions apply under the old tax regime. If you opt for the new regime, an eligible 80C investment does not reduce your taxable income.

What is the maximum I can claim under Section 80C?

The ceiling is ₹1.5 lakh per financial year. This is a combined cap that also covers Sections 80CCC and 80CCD(1), so these limits cannot be claimed separately on top of it.

Does home-loan repayment qualify under Section 80C?

The eligible principal portion of a home-loan repayment can qualify under Section 80C, subject to the applicable conditions. The interest portion is considered separately under Section 24, not under Section 80C.

Can I claim NPS in addition to the ₹1.5 lakh?

Yes, if you meet the applicable conditions. A contribution of up to ₹50,000 to NPS can be deductible under Section 80CCD(1B), over and above the combined ₹1.5 lakh limit.

Which Section 80C option has the shortest lock-in?

ELSS mutual funds have the shortest statutory lock-in among common Section 80C investment options, at three years. PPF has a fifteen-year tenure, subject to its withdrawal rules.

Do I need to invest fresh money to use Section 80C?

Not always. Existing eligible commitments such as EPF contributions, life-insurance premiums, home-loan principal and children’s tuition fees may already count. Check these first, then invest only for any remaining gap.
