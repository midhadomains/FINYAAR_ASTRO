Hedging

Hedging is the practice of taking an offsetting position in order to reduce the risk of an existing exposure, so that a loss on one holding is partly or fully cancelled by a gain elsewhere. It is risk management, not profit seeking. A hedge aims to reduce a specified risk and carries costs or trade-offs. A protective put preserves upside after its premium cost; a futures hedge can offset gains as well as losses. In Indian markets, investors hedge shares, portfolios, currency, and gold using tools such as a protective put, index futures, and currency forwards.

What hedging really means

To hedge is to set up a second position whose payoff moves opposite to something you already own. A well-matched hedge is designed to gain when the targeted exposure loses, but differences in timing, size or underlying assets can leave residual risk. The goal is a narrower band of outcomes, not a bigger average return. This is why hedging sits inside the discipline of risk versus return: you are deliberately trading away some expected reward to shrink the danger of a large loss.

A hedge is different from diversification. Diversification spreads money across many unrelated assets so no single one can hurt you badly. Hedging instead targets one specific exposure and buys direct protection for it, usually with a derivative whose value is tied to that exact risk.

Common hedging tools

The most familiar tools are options and futures. A protective put means buying a put option on a share or index you own; the put gains value as the price falls, acting like insurance with a deductible. Selling an index future against a portfolio can offset part of a broad market decline. The hedge size depends on portfolio value and market sensitivity, and differences between the portfolio and index can leave residual losses. A currency forward fixes a future exchange rate for an importer, exporter, or overseas investor. Gold is sometimes held as a diversifier or partial hedge, but its relationship with equities, inflation and the rupee varies; it offers no guaranteed offset.

The cost and trade-off

Every hedge has a price. A protective put costs a premium paid up front, which is lost if the feared fall never happens. A short future has no option premium, but requires margin and can generate daily cash outflows. In a closely matched hedge, futures losses offset gains on the holding when the market rises; an imperfect hedge leaves residual exposure. Worked example
In this hypothetical example, ignoring taxes, transaction costs and financing costs, Anjali, a Mumbai investor, owns 500 shares of a company trading at ₹1,000, a holding worth ₹5,00,000. Results are due in two months and she fears a sharp fall but does not want to sell. Assume available option lots cover exactly 500 shares. She buys a two-month protective put at a strike of ₹950 for a premium of ₹30 per share. Compare her outcome with and without the hedge if the share ends at ₹800, and if it ends at ₹1,150.

Step 1 — Cost of the hedge. The put covers 500 shares at ₹30 each, so the premium is 500 × ₹30 = ₹15,000. This is paid now and is the option premium cost under the stated assumptions.

Step 2 — Share falls to ₹800, no hedge. Her 500 shares are now worth 500 × ₹800 = ₹4,00,000, a loss of ₹1,00,000 against the starting ₹5,00,000.

Step 3 — Share falls to ₹800, with hedge. The put lets her effectively sell at ₹950. For a physically settled stock put held in the money at expiry, she delivers the shares at the strike; the calculation represents that economic outcome. The gross floor value is 500 × ₹950 = ₹4,75,000. Subtract the ₹15,000 premium and her net position is ₹4,60,000. The hedge has cut her loss from ₹1,00,000 to ₹40,000.

Step 4 — Share rises to ₹1,150, with and without hedge. Without the hedge her shares are worth ₹5,75,000, a gain of ₹75,000. With the hedge the put expires worthless, so she keeps the ₹5,75,000 shares but is out the ₹15,000 premium, a net gain of ₹60,000.

So what? The hedge behaves exactly like insurance. In the bad case it saved Anjali ₹60,000 of loss; in the good case it cost her ₹15,000 of upside. These two scenarios do not establish an average return. She paid a known premium to limit the expiry loss relative to her starting holding value to ₹40,000 under the stated assumptions. That trade-off is the whole point of hedging.

Check yourself
Q
If you buy a protective put and the share you own rises strongly, what happens to the put and to your overall return?
A: If the share finishes above the strike at expiry, the put expires worthless because selling at the strike is less favourable than selling at the market price. Your holding still gains, but your net return is reduced by the premium you paid for the put.

Key takeaways

  • Hedging means taking an offsetting position so a loss on one holding is cancelled by a gain elsewhere.
  • It is risk management, not profit seeking; it narrows outcomes rather than raising average return.
  • Common tools are the protective put, short index futures, currency forwards, and a gold allocation.
  • Every hedge has a cost — a premium paid or upside given up on a short future.
  • Hedge only a specific, known exposure over a defined period; do not confuse it with diversification.

Frequently asked questions

Is hedging the same as diversification?

No. Diversification spreads money across many unrelated assets, while hedging buys direct protection against one specific exposure, usually with a derivative tied to that exact risk.

Does hedging guarantee I will not lose money?

No. A hedge targets a particular risk. Premiums, transaction costs and funding requirements still matter, and imperfect hedges can leave significant losses. A matched protective put sets an expiry floor under stated assumptions; not every hedge provides such a floor.

What is a protective put?

It is buying a put option on a share or index you already own. The put gains value as the price falls, working like insurance that sets a floor under your holding for a premium.

Can I hedge an entire equity portfolio?

Selling index futures can reduce broad market exposure, but the size must reflect portfolio value and sensitivity to the index. Differences in composition and price movements can leave residual risk. Futures require margin, daily settlement and expiry management, and can offset gains when markets rise.

Is hedging only for large institutions?

No. Individual investors in India can buy index or stock options and futures through a broker, but derivatives are high risk and demand understanding of margins, expiry, and premiums.

How is a currency hedge used?

An investor or business expecting foreign currency can lock a future exchange rate with a forward or futures contract, so a swing in the rupee does not change the amount they finally receive.
