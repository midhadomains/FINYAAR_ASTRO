NSE

NSE (National Stock Exchange) is India's fully electronic stock exchange, where buy and sell orders from across the country meet in one computerised order book and are matched without a trading floor. It was incorporated in 1992, recognised in 1993 and began operations in 1994. It runs equity, derivatives and debt segments, publishes the Nifty 50, and settles trades through its own clearing corporation, all under SEBI regulation.

Order-driven, anonymous, matched by machine

No dealer quotes you a price at NSE. Every buyer and seller submits an order into a common electronic book, and the exchange matches the best buy against the best sell. The design was deliberate, since orders competing directly produce narrower spreads than a market routed through an intermediary's quote.

Two rules decide who trades first. Price wins, so a bid of ₹482.00 stands ahead of one at ₹481.90; time breaks ties, so among identical bids the earliest fills first. The book is anonymous, which lets an investor in Coimbatore and an institution in Mumbai sit in the same queue on the same terms.

Impact cost, the price the book charges for size

Quantity is where the book asserts itself. The quote on your screen covers only the shares at the front of the queue, and a larger order walks up the levels behind it, paying more at each step. NSE measures that and calls it impact cost.

P with the subscript actual is the weighted average price your order really achieves once every partial fill is counted. P with the subscript ideal is the midpoint of best bid and best offer, the price you would pay if the book were infinitely deep. Dividing the difference by that midpoint and multiplying by 100 states it as a percentage of trade value.

The measure is not academic. A share qualifies for the Nifty 50 only if its impact cost stayed at or below 0.50 per cent for 90 per cent of observations over six months, making tradability a formal entry condition alongside size.

Clearing, and why your trade settles even if the seller fails

Matching creates an obligation; it does not discharge one. NSE Clearing Limited, incorporated in 1995 and clearing from 1996 as the first Indian clearing corporation to offer a settlement guarantee, steps between the two sides as counterparty to each. Your money is owed to it and the shares are owed by it, so a defaulting stranger on the other side is its problem, not yours. Margins and a core settlement guarantee fund back that promise.

Equity settlement runs on a T plus 1 rolling cycle, so money and securities move on the working day after the trade. SEBI has also permitted an optional same-day cycle for a specified list of stocks. Under the direct payout rules, shares are credited straight into your demat account.

How the Nifty 50 is put together

The Nifty 50 tracks fifty large companies and is maintained by NSE Indices Limited, with a base date of 3 November 1995 and a base value of 1000, the index itself having been launched on 22 April 1996. Weights follow free-float market capitalisation, so only shares available to trade count: a promoter holding of 60 per cent leaves 40 per cent of that company's value inside the index.

Entry is rule-based, not editorial. A candidate must trade in the derivatives segment, clear the impact cost test and carry a minimum listing history. The index is reviewed twice a year against cut-off dates at the end of January and July, changes announced about four weeks ahead so index funds can adjust.

Worked example
Kavya Iyer in Coimbatore wants 900 shares of a company whose best bid is ₹481.45 and best offer ₹481.60. She places a limit buy order for 900 shares at ₹482.00. What fills, at what price, and what happens to the rest?

Step 1, read the sell side. The sell orders resting in the book are 300 shares at ₹481.60, 250 at ₹481.90, 200 at ₹482.00 and 400 at ₹482.35. Her limit says ₹482.00 and no more, so only the first three levels are open to her.

Step 2, match from the best price outward. The exchange fills the cheapest offer first, so her order takes all 300 at ₹481.60, then 250 at ₹481.90, then 200 at ₹482.00, giving 750 shares. She pays each seller that seller's own asking price, not one blended price.

Step 3, the price she actually paid. Dividing ₹3,61,355 by 750 gives a weighted average of ₹481.81. The midpoint before she traded was (481.45 + 481.60) divided by 2, or ₹481.53. Her impact cost is therefore (481.81 − 481.53) divided by 481.53, multiplied by 100, which comes to about 0.06 per cent of the value she traded. That is small because this share is liquid and the levels behind the quote are well stocked.

Step 4, the unfilled 150. The balance neither vanishes nor chases the ₹482.35 offer. It rests as a bid for 150 shares at ₹482.00, and since that beats the old best bid of ₹481.45, her order is now the best bid on the screen. It fills if a seller comes down to her price, and expires at the close if none does.

Step 5, the road not taken. Had she sent a market order instead, the exchange would have walked further up the book and taken the last 150 shares at ₹482.35, completing all 900 at a weighted average near ₹481.90.

So what? A limit order controls your price and leaves completion uncertain; a market order controls completion and leaves your price uncertain. Neither is superior, but in a thinly traded share the gap between the two can be several rupees rather than nine paise, which is why checking the depth before sending size is worth the thirty seconds it takes.

Check yourself
Q
Two buy orders for the same share arrive at ₹500. One is for 5,000 shares and arrives at 10:15, the other for 100 shares and arrives at 10:14. Which is filled first?
A: The 100-share order, because prices are equal and time then decides. Size gives no priority at all in the NSE order book.

Key takeaways

  • NSE is an electronic, order-driven, anonymous market with no floor and no dealer quoting prices to you.
  • Orders match by price first and time second, so size confers no advantage in the queue.
  • Impact cost measures what the book charges for quantity, and it is a formal Nifty 50 entry test.
  • NSE Clearing becomes counterparty to both sides and guarantees settlement, backed by margins and a core fund.
  • The Nifty 50 is free-float weighted, rule-based and reviewed twice a year with advance notice.

Frequently asked questions

Can I trade directly on NSE without a broker?

No. Orders reach the exchange only through a trading member registered with NSE and SEBI. You open a trading account with that member and a demat account with a depository participant, and the broker routes your instructions.

Why do the same shares trade on both NSE and the BSE?

Companies may list on more than one exchange, and most large ones do. Prices track each other closely because participants arbitrage any gap within seconds. Buying on one and selling on the other works only if you take delivery first.

What is the difference between the Nifty 50 and the Sensex?

They belong to different exchanges: the Nifty 50 holds fifty NSE-listed companies, the Sensex thirty on the BSE. Both use free-float weighting and overlap heavily, so they usually move together.

What happens if the person selling me shares fails to deliver?

You still receive your shares, because the clearing corporation guarantees settlement. The shortfall is covered through an auction in which the securities are bought from the market, at the defaulting seller's cost.

Are NSE trading hours the same for every segment?

No. The equity market has a pre-open session, continuous trading and a closing session, while currency and commodity segments follow their own schedules, all published by the exchange with the holiday calendar.

Does a rising Nifty 50 mean every listed share is rising?

Not at all. Fifty free-float weighted companies can be pulled up by a few heavyweights while most of the market falls. Breadth measures, counting shares advanced against declined, describe such a day far better.
