EPF (Employees Provident Fund)

EPF (Employees Provident Fund) is a government-backed retirement savings scheme for eligible salaried employees in India, managed by the Employees’ Provident Fund Organisation (EPFO). The employee and employer contribute an applicable share of wages, the EPF balance earns an annually declared rate of interest, and the accumulated corpus is available under the scheme’s retirement, exit and advance rules. It is one of India’s most widely held long-term employment-linked savings products.

What EPF actually is

EPF is a savings vehicle for eligible employees working in establishments covered by the EPF law. Every month an applicable share of wages is set aside, the employer adds its contribution, and the EPF portion grows at an interest rate recommended by the EPFO’s Central Board and approved or notified through the government process. Because contributions happen automatically through payroll, EPF works as a disciplined, low-effort way to build a retirement corpus.

The account framework is administered centrally, so the balance is not intended to remain tied to a single employer. A member can retain the same Universal Account Number and transfer or link member accounts across jobs. Coverage, contribution wage base and higher-wage membership can depend on the statutory ceiling, joining status and scheme rules, so an employee should verify the payroll basis shown in the EPF passbook.

Employee and employer contributions, and the EPS split

The standard contribution rate is commonly 12% of basic wages plus dearness allowance and retaining allowance, where applicable. The employer also contributes at the applicable rate, but part of the employer contribution is diverted to the Employees’ Pension Scheme (EPS). The EPS diversion is generally 8.33% of pensionable wages, subject to the statutory wage ceiling of ₹15,000 per month unless the applicable higher-pension framework changes the treatment. At that ceiling, the usual maximum EPS diversion is approximately ₹1,250 per month. The remainder of the employer’s applicable contribution goes to EPF.

This distinction matters when contributions are calculated on wages above ₹15,000. The employer’s total contribution may be based on the statutory ceiling or a higher wage base under the establishment’s practice and applicable permissions, while the ordinary EPS diversion remains capped. The EPS amount is not part of the EPF lump-sum balance; it supports a separate pension benefit subject to EPS eligibility rules.

How interest compounds, UAN and portability

Interest is calculated under EPFO rules using the running balance and is credited for the year after the rate is approved or notified. Once credited, it becomes part of the corpus and can earn interest in later years, so EPF compounds over a career. The rate is reviewed each year and can change.

Every member is issued or linked to a Universal Account Number (UAN). The UAN is intended to act as a lifetime identifier connecting member accounts across employers. When you switch jobs, quote the same UAN and complete the prescribed transfer or linking process instead of withdrawing merely because employment changed. This helps preserve compounding and service continuity.

Tax treatment and withdrawal rules

EPF is often described as broadly EEE — exempt on eligible contribution, accrual and qualifying withdrawal — but each stage has conditions. An eligible employee contribution can qualify within the combined Section 80C limit under the old tax regime. Interest is generally exempt, but interest attributable to employee contributions above ₹2.5 lakh in a financial year is taxable when the employer also contributes. A higher ₹5 lakh threshold can apply where there is no employer contribution.

A qualifying withdrawal after five years of continuous service is generally tax-free, and transferred service across employers normally counts. Withdrawal before five years can attract tax and TDS unless an exception applies. Partial advances are allowed for specified needs such as housing, medical treatment, marriage or education, subject to EPFO conditions.

Worked example
Ananya, a software analyst in Pune, has basic wages plus DA of ₹40,000 per month. Assume her employer’s policy and the applicable EPF arrangement calculate both 12% contributions on the full ₹40,000 wage base, while the ordinary EPS diversion remains capped at ₹15,000. How much goes into EPF each month, how much into EPS, and what does a simple one-year view look like?

Step 1 — Employee contribution. Under the stated assumption, Ananya contributes 12% of ₹40,000 = ₹4,800 per month. This goes into her EPF account.

Step 2 — Employer contribution and EPS diversion. The employer’s total contribution is also assumed to be 12% of ₹40,000 = ₹4,800. The ordinary EPS diversion is approximately 8.33% of the ₹15,000 ceiling, or ₹1,250. The balance, ₹4,800 − ₹1,250 = ₹3,550, goes into EPF.

Step 3 — Total into EPF each month. EPF credit = ₹4,800 employee contribution + ₹3,550 employer balance = ₹8,350 per month. The separate EPS diversion is about ₹1,250 per month.

Step 4 — Simple annual view before interest. Over 12 months the contribution totals are:

So what? The example shows why checking the wage base matters. If Ananya’s employer capped both contributions at the statutory wage ceiling instead of using the full ₹40,000, the figures would be lower. Her eligible employee contribution can count toward the combined Section 80C limit under the old regime. Keeping the UAN consistent and transferring the balance across jobs helps preserve long-term compounding.

Check yourself
If your applicable contribution wage base is ₹25,000 but the ordinary EPS pensionable-wage ceiling remains ₹15,000, roughly how much of the employer contribution is diverted to EPS?
A: About ₹1,250 under the ordinary capped calculation. EPS is approximately 8.33% of the ₹15,000 pensionable-wage ceiling, not 8.33% of ₹25,000. The remainder of the employer’s applicable contribution goes to EPF, subject to the scheme and any higher-pension arrangement.

Key takeaways

  • EPF is an EPFO-managed employment-linked retirement scheme using contributions calculated on an applicable wage base.
  • Part of the employer contribution is diverted to EPS; the remainder goes to the member’s EPF balance.
  • The EPF interest rate is determined for each year and credited under scheme rules, so it can change.
  • A UAN connects member accounts and supports portability and service continuity across jobs.
  • EPF is broadly tax-favoured, but contribution thresholds, withdrawal timing and other eligibility conditions matter.

Frequently asked questions

Is EPS the same as EPF?

No. EPF is an accumulated savings balance generally available as a lump sum under the withdrawal rules. EPS is a separate pension scheme funded from the employer contribution on capped pensionable wages and can provide a monthly pension when its eligibility conditions are met.

Can I contribute more than 12% to EPF?

An eligible member may contribute more through the Voluntary Provident Fund, subject to payroll and scheme processes. VPF generally earns the notified EPF rate, but interest attributable to employee contributions above the applicable annual tax threshold is taxable.

What happens to my EPF when I change jobs?

Keep the same UAN and transfer or link the previous member account through the prescribed EPFO process rather than treating every job change as a final withdrawal. This preserves the accumulated balance and helps maintain service continuity.

Is EPF withdrawal always tax-free?

No. A qualifying withdrawal after at least five years of continuous service is generally tax-free, with transferred service normally counted. Earlier withdrawals can be taxable or subject to TDS unless an exception applies.

How is the EPF interest rate decided?

The EPFO’s Central Board recommends a rate for each year and the government approves or notifies it through the applicable process. Interest is credited annually according to scheme rules, so the rate can change from year to year.

Can I withdraw EPF before retirement for emergencies?

EPFO permits partial advances for specified purposes such as housing, medical treatment, marriage and education, subject to the purpose-specific service requirements, limits and documentation.
