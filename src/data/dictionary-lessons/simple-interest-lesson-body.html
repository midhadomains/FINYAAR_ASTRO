Simple Interest

Simple Interest is the money a lender earns, or a borrower pays, calculated only on the original principal amount for the full period, without adding earned interest back to the principal. Because the base never changes, the interest amount stays the same in every period, making it the simplest way to price a loan or a deposit.

What is Simple Interest?

Simple Interest is interest charged only on the principal (the sum you originally lent or deposited), and never on the interest that piles up along the way. If you lend a friend ₹10,000 at 10% simple interest a year, you earn a flat ₹1,000 every year, whether the loan runs for one year or ten. The base stays frozen at ₹10,000 forever.

This is the arithmetic most Indians meet first, in the shopkeeper's vyaj (interest) on a hand loan, in short car and two-wheeler loans, and in some flat-rate EMI schemes. It contrasts sharply with Compound Interest, where interest itself starts earning interest. Because nothing gets added back, simple interest grows in a straight line, which makes it easy to compute in your head but usually poorer for a saver over long horizons.

Simple vs Compound: the gap that grows

Simple interest is linear; compound interest is a curve that bends upward. On a principal of ₹1,00,000 at 10% per year, watch how the two drift apart:

YearSimple interest earnedSimple balanceCompound balance (annual)
1₹10,000₹1,10,000₹1,10,000
5₹50,000₹1,50,000₹1,61,051
10₹1,00,000₹2,00,000₹2,59,374

At Year 1 they match exactly. By Year 10 compounding has pulled almost ₹59,000 ahead. This is exactly why long-term instruments like a Fixed Deposit, a Recurring Deposit, or a Mutual Fund built through a SIP compound your money rather than pay simple interest. Simple interest survives mainly in short-tenure loans and in the interest payout option of some deposits, where the interest is handed to you regularly instead of being reinvested.

Step 4: Maturity value. At the end she gets her principal back plus the final year's interest: ₹2,00,000 + ₹16,000 = ₹2,16,000 in the last payout, having already pocketed ₹64,000 earlier. Total cash received across the tenure = ₹2,00,000 + ₹80,000 = ₹2,80,000.

Step 5: Compare with compounding. Had the same 8% compounded annually and been left untouched, her ₹2,00,000 would have grown to ₹2,00,000 × (1.08)5 = ₹2,93,866, giving ₹93,866 of interest.

So what? The simple-interest route leaves Priya about ₹13,866 poorer over five years on the same rate and same money. The catch is that her yearly ₹16,000 payouts are useful only if she reinvests them somewhere, otherwise idle cash quietly loses value to Inflation, which the RBI targets at 4% within a 2-6% band. A simple-interest payout suits someone who needs regular income today; a saver aiming to build a corpus should almost always prefer compounding.

Check yourself
You borrow ₹50,000 for 3 years at 12% simple interest per year. What is the total interest, and how much do you repay in all?

SI = (50,000 × 12 × 3) / 100 = 18,00,000 / 100 = ₹18,000. Total repayment = 50,000 + 18,000 = ₹68,000. The interest is a flat ₹6,000 each year because it is always charged on the original ₹50,000.

Key takeaways

  • Simple interest is charged only on the original principal, so the interest amount is the same every period.
  • The formula is SI = P × R × T / 100, with no exponent, so growth is a straight line.
  • Over long horizons it earns far less than Compound Interest, as the ₹1,00,000 example showed a ₹59,000 gap by Year 10.
  • It is common in short loans, flat-rate schemes and the payout option of some deposits.
  • For building wealth, prefer compounding; use simple interest instruments mainly for predictable regular income.

Frequently asked questions

Is a Fixed Deposit simple or compound interest?

It depends on the option you pick. A Fixed Deposit with the cumulative option compounds interest, usually quarterly, and pays everything at maturity. The non-cumulative or payout option behaves more like simple interest, handing you interest monthly, quarterly or yearly without reinvesting it. Bank FDs are also protected up to ₹5 lakh per bank by DICGC.

Why do banks use simple interest for some loans but compound for savings?

Flat or simple-interest quotes make a loan look cheaper because the headline rate seems low, even though the effective cost can be higher than a reducing-balance loan. On deposits and standard loans, compounding is used because it reflects the true time value of money. Always compare the effective annual rate, not just the advertised number.

Does simple interest affect my CIBIL score?

The interest type itself does not directly move your Credit Score (CIBIL), which ranges from 300 to 900. What matters is whether you repay on time. Whether a loan uses simple or compound interest, missed EMIs and high credit utilisation pull your score down, while consistent repayment lifts it.

How is flat-rate EMI related to simple interest?

A flat-rate EMI charges interest on the full original principal for the entire tenure, exactly like simple interest, even though you keep repaying the principal each month. This makes the effective interest rate almost double the flat rate. A reducing-balance loan, which charges interest only on the outstanding amount, is usually cheaper for the borrower.

Can simple interest ever beat compound interest?

Only over a period of exactly one compounding cycle or less. For a tenure of one year with annual compounding, both give the same result. Beyond that, compound interest always wins for the investor because the earned interest starts earning too. The longer the horizon, the wider the gap.

Where is simple interest still used in India today?

You will find it in many personal, car and two-wheeler loans quoted at flat rates, in informal hand loans, in some post-office and company deposit payout schemes, and in interim or penal interest calculations. It is prized for being transparent and easy to verify, even where it is not the cheapest structure.
