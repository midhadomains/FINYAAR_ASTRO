Health Insurance

Health Insurance is a contract under which an insurer pays eligible hospitalisation and related medical expenses up to the policy limits in return for a premium. A claim may be settled directly through an approved cashless process or reimbursed after payment, subject to pre-authorisation, admissibility, exclusions, deductibles, co-payments and the policy terms.

What it is

A health-insurance policy is a contract issued by an insurer regulated by the IRDAI (Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India). You pay the required premium; in exchange, the insurer covers eligible medical expenses described in the contract. Coverage can include hospitalisation, day-care procedures, pre- and post-hospitalisation expenses, domiciliary treatment or other benefits, but each policy defines its own scope and limits. Unlike term insurance, which provides a death benefit, health insurance is designed to pay eligible medical claims while the insured person is alive. The premium is the price of transferring defined financial risk, not a refundable deposit.

How to choose your cover

There is no universal sum insured. Consider hospital costs where you live, family size and age, medical history, existing employer coverage, emergency savings and whether a base policy plus a super top-up suits you. Compare room eligibility, co-pay, deductible, sub-limits, the maximum 36-month waiting period, restoration rules and exclusions. A restoration benefit can be valuable, but whether it refills cover for the same person or illness and when it activates varies by product. Review the current cashless-provider list and the insurer’s claim process rather than assuming every hospital or expense will be cashless.

Employer cover versus a personal policy

Employer group cover can be valuable and may offer favourable terms, but its amount, covered relatives and benefits are controlled by the group contract. Coverage can end or change when employment ends, subject to the policy’s continuation, migration or portability options. A personal policy can provide continuity independent of one employer. Buying earlier may let waiting periods run while you are younger, but it does not permanently lock the premium: insurers can reprice products according to applicable rules and premiums often rise with age or benefit changes.

Worked example
Rhea has a health policy with an available sum insured of ₹5,00,000, a ₹25,000 deductible and a 10% co-pay. Her hospital bill is ₹4,00,000. Assume the policy treats ₹30,000 of consumables and administrative charges as non-admissible, applies the deductible before the co-pay, and has no other limits. How much does the insurer pay?

Step 1 — Remove non-admissible items. The admissible bill is ₹4,00,000 − ₹30,000 = ₹3,70,000. Rhea bears the excluded ₹30,000.

Step 2 — Apply the deductible. The assumed ₹25,000 deductible leaves ₹3,45,000 for the co-pay calculation.

Step 3 — Apply the co-pay. Rhea’s 10% share of ₹3,45,000 is ₹34,500. The indicative insurer share is ₹3,10,500.

Step 4 — Check the available sum insured. ₹3,10,500 is within the ₹5,00,000 available limit, so the ceiling does not reduce it.

So what? Rhea pays ₹89,500 despite having a ₹5 lakh sum insured because the sum insured is a ceiling, not a promise to pay every part of the bill. Actual treatment depends on the precise policy wording and claim adjudication.

Check yourself
Your policy has an available sum insured of ₹7,00,000 and a 20% co-pay, with no deductible. If the admissible hospital bill is ₹2,00,000 and no other limit applies, how much does the insurer pay?
A: The 20% co-pay is ₹40,000, so the indicative insurer payment is ₹1,60,000. The available sum-insured ceiling does not reduce the claim because the amount is below ₹7,00,000.

Key takeaways

  • The sum insured is a policy limit, not a guarantee that every rupee of a hospital bill will be paid.
  • Deductibles, co-pays, exclusions, sub-limits and non-admissible expenses can reduce the insurer’s payment.
  • IRDAI currently caps waiting periods, including pre-existing-disease waiting periods, at 36 months.
  • Employer cover can change or end with employment, so assess whether separate personal cover is appropriate.
  • Eligible premiums can receive a Section 80D deduction under the old tax regime, subject to conditions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a deductible and a co-pay?

A deductible is an amount borne by the insured as defined in the contract. A co-pay is a percentage of an admissible claim that remains payable by the insured. A policy can contain both.

Does health insurance cover pre-existing diseases?

It can, after the waiting period stated in the policy and subject to disclosure and other terms. Under IRDAI’s current framework, waiting periods cannot exceed 36 months.

Is cashless treatment always available?

No. Cashless treatment requires pre-authorisation and ordinarily follows the eligible network-provider process. The patient may still pay deductibles, co-payments, exclusions, non-admissible expenses or deposits.

Is employer cover enough on its own?

It may not be. Its sum insured and benefits can be limited, and coverage may end or change with employment. A personal policy can provide continuity, but individual needs differ.

How much sum insured should I take?

There is no universal amount. Consider local costs, family ages, medical needs, existing cover, room eligibility, cost-sharing terms and emergency savings.

Do I get any tax benefit?

Eligible premiums can qualify under Section 80D when the old tax regime is used, subject to statutory conditions and limits. The deduction is separate from Section 80C.
