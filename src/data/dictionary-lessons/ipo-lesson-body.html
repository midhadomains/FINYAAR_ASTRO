IPO

IPO stands for Initial Public Offering, the process by which a privately held company sells its shares to the public for the first time and gets listed on a stock exchange such as the NSE or BSE. An IPO lets a company raise capital from public investors while giving early founders and backers a route to sell some or all of their holdings.

What is an IPO?

An IPO (Initial Public Offering) is the first time a company offers its equity shares to the general public. Before an IPO, a company is private and owned by promoters, family, employees and private investors. After a successful IPO, its shares trade freely on a stock exchange and anyone with a demat account can buy or sell them.

Think of Zomato, LIC, Nykaa or Paytm. When each of these companies went public, it invited retail investors, mutual funds and foreign institutions to subscribe to its shares within a price band. Once the offer closed and shares were allotted, they began trading on the NSE or BSE. The process in India is regulated by SEBI (the Securities and Exchange Board of India). The company files a detailed disclosure document called the DRHP (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) for regulatory review.

An IPO can do two jobs at once: it lets the company raise fresh capital to fund growth, and it gives existing shareholders a chance to sell part of their stake through an Offer for Sale (OFS).

How an IPO works in India

An Indian IPO generally follows this sequence:

1. Appoint merchant bankers and file the DRHP with SEBI and the stock exchanges. 2. Regulatory review and public feedback. 3. Announce the price band and offer dates (for example, ₹100–₹105 per share). 4. Open the book-building window, during which investors apply through ASBA or supported UPI flows so the application money is blocked in their bank accounts. 5. Finalise allotment and unblock unused funds. 6. List the shares on the exchange.

Most Indian IPOs use the book-building method, where investors bid within a price band and the final issue price is discovered from demand. The offer document specifies how shares are allocated among eligible investor categories.

Book-built IPOs divide the offer among investor categories. The exact allocation depends on the applicable regulations and the offer's structure, so investors should check the prospectus.

Types of public offers

Not every public offer is a classic IPO. It helps to know the differences:

  • Fresh Issue: new shares are created; the money goes to the company for purposes such as expansion or repaying debt.
  • Offer for Sale (OFS): existing shareholders sell shares; the money goes to them, not the company.
  • FPO (Follow-on Public Offer): an already-listed company offers additional shares to the public.
  • SME IPO: a smaller company lists on an SME platform such as BSE SME or NSE Emerge. These shares may have lower liquidity and higher volatility.
Worked example

Rohan, a salaried professional in Pune, wants to apply for the fictional Bharat Logistics IPO. The price band is ₹95–₹100 per share, and the lot size is 150 shares. Assume the applicable retail limit is ₹2 lakh. Rohan applies at the cut-off price for the maximum whole lots he can fit under that limit. The IPO is oversubscribed 8 times in the retail category. On listing day the stock opens at ₹128. What happens to Rohan's money, and what is his listing gain if he receives one lot?

Step 1 — Cost of one lot. Using the upper band price of ₹100 for the application calculation, one lot of 150 shares costs 150 × ₹100 = ₹15,000.

Step 2 — Maximum lots under the assumed retail cap. ₹2,00,000 ÷ ₹15,000 = 13.33, so Rohan can apply for a maximum of 13 lots, or 1,950 shares, blocking 13 × ₹15,000 = ₹1,95,000 through UPI or ASBA.

Step 3 — Effect of oversubscription. In this example, Rohan receives 1 lot (150 shares) costing ₹15,000. The unused blocked amount of ₹1,80,000 is released. Actual allotment depends on the basis of allotment and applicable rules; an 8x subscription does not guarantee that a particular applicant receives shares.

ItemCalculationAmount
Applied (13 lots)13 × 150 × ₹100₹1,95,000
Allotted (1 lot)150 × ₹100₹15,000
Amount unblocked₹1,95,000 − ₹15,000₹1,80,000
Listing value150 × ₹128₹19,200
Listing gain₹19,200 − ₹15,000₹4,200

Step 4 — Listing gain. His 150 shares are worth 150 × ₹128 = ₹19,200 against a cost of ₹15,000. That is a profit of ₹4,200, or 28% on the allotted amount, before taxes and charges.

So what? Because of oversubscription, Rohan blocked ₹1.95 lakh but only ₹15,000 was invested in this example. A 28% listing gain looks spectacular, but it applies only to one lot and is not guaranteed. Many IPOs list flat or below their issue price. A single lucky allotment does not build wealth; diversification and a long-term view matter more than chasing listing gains.

How to value an IPO

The prospectus helps you judge whether the asking price is reasonable. A common yardstick is the P/E ratio, which compares the issue price with the company's EPS. You can also study its EBITDA margins, debt and implied market capitalization against listed peers. Some companies pay a dividend, while many growth companies reinvest profits. An equity investment carries real risk vs return; unlike an eligible bank fixed deposit, an IPO offers no capital guarantee or deposit-insurance protection.

Check yourself
An IPO offers 2 crore shares at ₹120 each and receives applications for 30 crore shares. What is the issue size and the subscription ratio?

Issue size = 2 crore × ₹120 = ₹240 crore. Subscription ratio = 30 crore ÷ 2 crore = 15x, meaning demand is fifteen times the shares offered.

Key takeaways

  • An IPO is a company's first sale of shares to the public, followed by listing on an exchange such as the NSE or BSE.
  • Issue size = shares offered × issue price; the subscription ratio compares demand with supply.
  • Book-built IPOs allocate shares among categories such as QIB, NII and Retail, according to the offer structure and applicable rules.
  • Application money is generally blocked through ASBA or supported UPI flows, and unused funds are released after allotment.
  • Listing gains are not guaranteed; assess the business, valuation, risks and use of proceeds before applying.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a demat account to apply for an IPO?

Yes. Shares are allotted electronically, so you need a demat account and valid investor details such as a PAN. You can apply through an eligible bank's ASBA facility or a supported UPI-enabled broker flow, where the application amount is blocked until allotment.

Is IPO investment guaranteed profit?

No. An IPO is an equity investment with no capital protection. Shares may list at, above or below the issue price and can fall afterwards. Unlike an eligible bank fixed deposit, an IPO has no deposit-insurance protection.

What is oversubscription and how does allotment work?

Oversubscription means valid bids exceed the shares available. Allotment follows the basis approved for the issue. In a heavily oversubscribed retail category, eligible applicants may receive the minimum lot through a computerised draw, while some applicants receive no shares.

What is the difference between an IPO and an FPO?

An IPO is the first public offer of shares by a company, while an FPO (Follow-on Public Offer) is made by a company that is already listed.

What is a grey market premium (GMP)?

GMP is an unofficial and unregulated indication of the price at which an IPO may trade before listing. It is not endorsed by SEBI, can change rapidly and should not be the basis of an investment decision.

Can I invest in equities without applying to every IPO?

Yes. An index fund, ETF or mutual fund can provide diversified equity exposure. A monthly SIP can help you invest consistently without depending on IPO allotments.
