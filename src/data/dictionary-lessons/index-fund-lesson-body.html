FINYAAR DICTIONARY · MUTUAL FUNDS AND SIP

Index Fund

Index Fund is a type of mutual fund that passively copies a market index such as the Nifty 50 or the Sensex, holding the same stocks in the same weights instead of trying to beat the market. It aims to deliver the index return at very low cost, making it one of the simplest ways for an Indian investor to own the whole market.

What is an Index Fund?

An Index Fund is a mutual fund that mechanically mirrors a benchmark index. If you buy a Nifty 50 index fund, the scheme buys all 50 stocks of the Nifty in exactly the same proportion the index assigns them, so your money rises and falls almost exactly as the Sensex and Nifty do. There is no star fund manager picking winners; the fund simply tracks the index. This is called passive investing.

Think of it like buying a ready-made thali instead of ordering each dish. A Reliance or HDFC Bank shareholder is betting on one company; a Nifty 50 index fund investor owns a slice of India's 50 largest listed companies in one shot. Because there is no expensive research team, the fee (the expense ratio) is tiny, often 0.10% to 0.30% a year versus 1.5% to 2.2% for an actively managed fund.

How an Index Fund works

You invest a rupee amount and receive units priced at the fund's NAV (Net Asset Value), which is published once at the end of each trading day. The fund manager pools everyone's money and buys the index constituents. When the index provider periodically rebalances the index, the fund adjusts its holdings to match.

The manager's only job is to keep the fund glued to the index. The small gap between the fund's return and the index's return is called tracking error (or tracking difference), caused mainly by the expense ratio, cash held for redemptions, and trading costs.

Index Fund vs ETF vs Active Fund

Index funds are one of three ways to get market exposure. An ETF also tracks an index but trades live on the exchange like a share, while an active fund pays a manager to try to beat the market. The table compares them for a typical Indian investor.

For most first-time investors who want a simple monthly SIP without a demat account, the index fund wins on convenience; the ETF wins narrowly on cost if you already trade. Both give instant diversification and predictable behaviour, which lowers volatility compared with betting on a single equity share.

Worked example
Riya, a 30-year-old software engineer in Pune, starts a ₹10,000 monthly SIP in a Nifty 50 index fund. Assume the index compounds at 12% per year. How much wealth does she build in 5 and 10 years, and how much did the low fee actually save her?

Step 1 — Set up the inputs. Monthly SIP P = ₹10,000. Annual return 12%, so monthly rate i = 12% / 12 = 1% = 0.01. For 10 years, n = 10 × 12 = 120 instalments.

Step 2 — Compound the growth factor. (1 + 0.01)120 = 3.3004. Plugging into the SIP formula:

FV = 10,000 × [(3.3004 − 1) / 0.01] × 1.01 = 10,000 × 230.04 × 1.01 ≈ ₹23,23,393.

Step 3 — Do the 5-year checkpoint. For n = 60, (1.01)60 = 1.8167, so FV = 10,000 × [(1.8167 − 1)/0.01] × 1.01 ≈ ₹8,24,864.

Step 4 — Value the low fee. Riya's index fund charges 0.20% a year. A regular active fund charging 1.70% costs 1.50% more annually. On a corpus that averages roughly ₹10 lakh over the decade, that extra 1.5% is about ₹15,000 a year of fees quietly leaking out — money that, left invested, would itself compound into lakhs by retirement.

So what? By simply owning the market cheaply, Riya turns ₹12 lakh of contributions into over ₹23 lakh in ten years, and keeps the fee drag near zero. She never has to guess which stock or which manager will win — the index does the work, and low costs let compounding keep more of her return.

Check yourself
If a Nifty 50 index rises 11% in a year and your Nifty index fund returns 10.7%, what is the tracking difference, and what mostly explains it?

Tracking difference = 10.7% − 11% = −0.3%. The gap is negative and small, explained mainly by the fund's expense ratio plus minor cash drag and trading costs. A well-run index fund keeps this difference close to its fee.

Key takeaways

  • An index fund passively copies a benchmark like the Nifty 50 or Sensex instead of trying to beat it.
  • Its biggest edge is a very low expense ratio, so more of the market return stays with you.
  • You invest at day-end NAV and can run a simple monthly SIP — no demat account needed, unlike an ETF.
  • Tracking error measures how closely the fund follows its index; smaller is better.
  • Index funds give instant diversification across the market, reducing single-stock risk.
  • Best suited to long-term, hands-off investors who value low cost and simplicity.

Frequently asked questions

Are index funds safe in India?

Index funds are regulated by SEBI and hold a basket of listed companies, which reduces single-company and fund-manager selection risk. But they are still market-linked investments, so their NAV can fall when the market falls. Unlike eligible bank deposits, they do not have deposit-insurance protection. Treat them as long-term investments, not guaranteed products.

Which is better, an index fund or an ETF?

Both can track the same index. An index fund is easier for SIP investors because you buy at NAV without a demat account, while an ETF trades live on the exchange and often has a slightly lower expense ratio. If you invest a fixed amount monthly and prefer simplicity, the index fund usually wins.

Can I do a SIP in an index fund?

Yes. A monthly SIP into a Nifty 50 or Sensex index fund is a popular way to invest in India. It spreads purchases across market ups and downs and lets compounding build wealth over time, though returns are not guaranteed.

Do index funds pay dividends?

Most investors choose the growth option, where income from the underlying stocks remains invested and is reflected in the NAV. An IDCW option may also be available, but distributions are not guaranteed and are subject to applicable tax rules.

What return can I expect from an index fund?

No return is guaranteed. An index fund aims to deliver the underlying index's return minus fees and tracking difference. Equity indices can deliver strong long-term returns but may experience sharp declines, so consider the trade-off explained in risk vs return.

How are index funds taxed in India?

Tax treatment depends on the fund's underlying holdings and how long you hold its units. Equity-oriented index funds generally follow equity capital-gains rules, while other index funds may be treated differently. Tax rules can change, so verify the current rates and thresholds before redeeming.
