What the repo rate is and who sets it

“Repo” is short for repurchase agreement. In a liquidity-injecting repo with RBI, an eligible participant obtains short-term funds against eligible securities and agrees to reverse the transaction later. The price difference represents repo interest. The policy repo rate is the benchmark policy rate decided by RBI’s six-member Monetary Policy Committee (MPC).

The MPC’s statutory primary objective is maintaining price stability while keeping the objective of growth in mind. It normally meets on a published schedule and decides by majority vote. The policy rate influences overnight money-market conditions and, through monetary transmission, broader lending, deposit and bond yields. The effect is important but not mechanical: liquidity, competition, risk, funding mix and expectations also shape retail rates.

How a repo change reaches loans and deposits

Since October 2019, scheduled commercial banks covered by RBI’s framework must price specified new floating-rate retail and MSME loans using an external benchmark. When the benchmark changes, the borrower’s rate changes at the contractual reset, subject to the loan terms. The lender may change the EMI, tenure or both. Deposit rates can move in the same broad direction, but they are not automatically tied to repo: banks also respond to liquidity, competition and funding needs. Existing fixed-rate loans and deposits do not reprice merely because repo changes.

How monetary policy addresses inflation

India uses a flexible inflation-targeting framework. For 1 April 2026 through 31 March 2031, the notified headline CPI inflation target remains 4%, with a lower tolerance level of 2% and an upper tolerance level of 6%. Higher policy rates can moderate borrowing and demand, helping reduce persistent price pressure; lower rates can support activity when inflation conditions permit. The effect works with variable lags and cannot directly fix every supply shock, such as a crop failure or imported energy-price surge.

The MPC therefore studies the outlook rather than reacting to one data point. Its resolution explains the rate decision and policy stance, helping households, businesses and markets form expectations. For the wider context, see inflation and the Economics and Macro hub.

The liquidity corridor and reverse repo

The operational framework uses a corridor around the policy repo rate to guide overnight money-market rates. The Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) is the standing absorption facility and floor of the Liquidity Adjustment Facility corridor. The Marginal Standing Facility (MSF) is the standing liquidity-injection facility and ceiling. The policy repo rate sits between them.

A reverse repo also absorbs liquidity: RBI receives funds and provides securities in the reverse leg. Fixed-rate reverse repo remains part of RBI’s toolkit, but since the SDF’s introduction in 2022 it is not the standing floor of the corridor. This distinction matters because older explanations often still describe reverse repo as the floor.

Check yourself
If the RBI cuts the policy repo rate, what usually happens to a repo-linked floating loan at its next reset, and must a bank cut its fixed-deposit rates by the same amount?
A: The repo-linked loan rate normally falls at its contractual reset if the spread and other terms are unchanged, which may reduce the EMI, tenure or both. A bank does not have to cut fixed-deposit rates by the same amount; deposit pricing also depends on liquidity, competition, funding needs and product tenure.

Key takeaways

  • The policy repo rate is the MPC’s benchmark rate for liquidity-injecting repo operations with RBI.
  • Repo-linked floating loans reprice at contractual resets; the EMI, tenure or both may change.
  • Deposit rates and bond yields respond to monetary conditions but are not mechanically determined by repo alone.
  • India’s notified CPI target is 4%, with a 2% to 6% tolerance range, from April 2026 through March 2031.
  • The SDF is the corridor floor and the MSF is the ceiling; fixed-rate reverse repo remains a separate RBI tool.

Frequently asked questions

Who decides the repo rate in India?

RBI’s six-member Monetary Policy Committee decides it by majority vote.

Why does the RBI change the repo rate?

The MPC assesses inflation, growth, liquidity, financial conditions and the outlook. Higher rates generally restrain demand; lower rates generally support activity.

How does repo affect my home-loan EMI?

A repo-linked loan reprices at its contractual reset. The lender may adjust EMI, tenure or both according to the terms and rules.

How do repo and reverse repo differ?

Repo injects liquidity; reverse repo absorbs it. The SDF, rather than fixed reverse repo, is now the standing corridor floor.

Does a repo cut always lower deposit rates?

No. Deposit pricing also reflects each bank’s liquidity, funding needs, competition and deposit mix.
