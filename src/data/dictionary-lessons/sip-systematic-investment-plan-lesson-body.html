FINYAAR DICTIONARY · MUTUAL FUNDS AND SIP

SIP (Systematic Investment Plan)

SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) is a disciplined method of investing a chosen amount into a mutual fund at regular intervals, usually monthly, so units are accumulated over time instead of through one lump-sum purchase. It can turn investing into a recurring habit and give each contribution time to compound.

What is SIP (Systematic Investment Plan)?

A SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) is a facility offered by mutual funds that lets you invest a chosen amount on a recurring schedule. On the instalment date, the amount is generally debited through your registered mandate and used to buy units at the applicable NAV (Net Asset Value). Minimum amounts, available frequencies and processing rules vary by scheme and platform.

Think of a Pune schoolteacher who starts a ₹5,000 monthly SIP in an equity fund. She does not try to predict the market every month. The contribution leaves her salary account on a scheduled date, much like a recurring deposit, except the mutual-fund return is market-linked rather than a stated deposit return. The regular schedule can make long-term investing easier to maintain.

How a SIP works: rupee cost averaging

When you invest the same rupee amount at regular intervals, you buy more units when the NAV is lower and fewer units when it is higher. This mechanism is called rupee cost averaging. It spreads purchases across dates and reduces dependence on choosing one entry point, but it does not guarantee a profit or a lower cost than investing a lump sum.

For example, if the NAV is ₹20 one month and ₹25 the next, two ₹5,000 investments buy 250 units and 200 units. The total ₹10,000 buys 450 units, giving an average acquisition cost of about ₹22.22 per unit. That weighted cost is below the simple average NAV of ₹22.50 because more units were purchased at the lower price. Future returns still depend on what happens to the fund's NAV.

Worked example
Ravi, a 30-year-old IT professional in Bengaluru, starts a ₹5,000 monthly SIP in an equity index fund. Assume, only for illustration, a constant return of 12% per year with contributions at the start of each month. What corpus does the formula estimate over 5, 10, 15 and 20 years?

We use FV = P × [((1+i)n − 1) ÷ i] × (1+i) with P = ₹5,000 and the simplified monthly rate i = 0.12 ÷ 12 = 0.01. Only n changes with tenure.

10-year case (n = 120): (1.01)120 is approximately 3.3004. Therefore, [(3.3004 − 1) ÷ 0.01] × 1.01 is approximately 232.34. FV = 5,000 × 232.34 = about ₹11,61,700. Ravi contributes ₹5,000 × 120 = ₹6,00,000, leaving an illustrated difference of about ₹5.62 lakh.

Earlier instalments have longer to compound, and accumulated returns also earn returns. Actual markets do not deliver a smooth 12% each year, so real results can be materially higher or lower. Starting early can help, but fund choice, costs, asset allocation and investor behaviour still matter.

SIP vs lump sum and fixed deposits

A lump sum puts available capital to work immediately, while a SIP invests it gradually or as income becomes available. If the full amount is already available, investing earlier gives it more time in the market but also exposes it immediately to market movements. A SIP reduces dependence on a single entry date, though it does not remove investment risk.

A fixed deposit has a stated interest rate and maturity terms, while a mutual-fund SIP is market-linked and has no capital guarantee or bank-deposit insurance. Eligible bank deposits may receive deposit-insurance protection subject to current rules. Compare goals, horizon, liquidity, taxes, risk and the fund's expense ratio rather than choosing solely on expected return.

Check yourself
In the illustration, why is the 20-year corpus of about ₹50 lakh more than four times the 10-year corpus of about ₹11.6 lakh even though total contributions only doubled?

Because of the assumed compounding. Earlier instalments grow for longer, and accumulated returns also earn returns. Under a constant positive return, extra time creates non-linear growth. Actual market returns vary, so the figures are illustrative rather than guaranteed.

Key takeaways

  • A SIP invests a chosen amount into a mutual fund at regular intervals and buys units at the applicable NAV.
  • Rupee cost averaging buys more units at lower NAVs and fewer at higher NAVs, but does not guarantee profit.
  • The annuity formula can illustrate future value under constant-return assumptions; real returns fluctuate.
  • A longer horizon gives earlier contributions more time to compound.
  • SIPs are market-linked and do not have the capital guarantee or deposit insurance associated with eligible bank deposits.

Frequently asked questions

Is SIP better than a lump-sum investment?

Neither is universally better. A lump sum puts available money to work immediately, while a SIP spreads purchases across dates and can suit regular income. The outcome depends on market movements, time in the market, asset allocation and investor behaviour.

Can I lose money in a SIP?

Yes. A SIP is an investment method, and the underlying fund remains market-linked. The value can fall below your contributions, especially over short periods, and a long holding period does not guarantee a positive return.

What is the minimum amount for a SIP?

The minimum varies by scheme and platform. Many funds permit relatively small instalments, but investors should check the current scheme documents and transaction rules rather than assume one universal minimum.

Does a SIP give tax benefits?

A SIP does not itself create a tax benefit. An investment in an eligible scheme such as ELSS may qualify under applicable tax rules and tax regimes, with a lock-in applying separately to each instalment. Verify current law before relying on a deduction.

Can I stop or pause my SIP anytime?

Many SIP mandates can be paused or cancelled according to the AMC or platform process. Stopping future instalments does not automatically redeem existing units. Redemption may still be subject to exit loads, lock-ins, taxes and processing rules.

SIP or recurring deposit: which should I choose?

A recurring deposit provides a stated bank interest rate and may suit goals requiring greater predictability. A SIP's outcome depends on the chosen mutual fund and carries market risk. Choose based on goal, horizon, liquidity, risk tolerance and applicable deposit-insurance terms.
