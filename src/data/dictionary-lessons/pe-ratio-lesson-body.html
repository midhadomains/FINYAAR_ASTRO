P/E Ratio

P/E Ratio (Price-to-Earnings Ratio) is a valuation multiple that tells you how many rupees investors are paying for every one rupee of a company's annual earnings per share. It is calculated by dividing the share price by earnings per share and is a widely used starting point for comparing stock valuations.

What is the P/E Ratio?

The P/E Ratio is the price investors pay for each rupee of a company's earnings. If a stock trades at a P/E of 30, the market price is ₹30 for every ₹1 of annual earnings per share. A higher number may reflect expectations of faster growth, stronger business quality or optimism. A lower number may reflect a bargain, slower growth, cyclical earnings or concern about the business.

Think of buying a chai stall in your neighbourhood. If it earns ₹2 lakh a year and the owner asks ₹20 lakh, the asking value is ten times annual earnings—a P/E-like multiple of 10. This does not mean the buyer will literally recover the price in ten years, because future profits, reinvestment, financing and cash distributions may change. The same valuation idea scales to an equity share traded on the NSE or BSE.

The formula can use different earnings figures. Match like with like when comparing companies.

Normal P/E ranges vary widely across sectors and market cycles. Consumer, technology, banking, commodity and utility businesses can deserve very different multiples because their growth, capital needs, cyclicality and accounting profiles differ. Compare a company with relevant peers and its own history rather than relying on a universal market threshold.

Worked example
Bharat Consumer Ltd trades at ₹2,000 per share. Last year it reported profit attributable to equity shareholders of ₹5,000 crore on 100 crore weighted-average shares. Analysts expect next year's profit to grow 20%. Its selected FMCG peers trade at an average P/E of 50. What do the trailing and forward multiples show?

Step 1 — Find EPS. ₹5,000 crore ÷ 100 crore shares = ₹50 per share.

Step 2 — Compute trailing P/E. ₹2,000 ÷ ₹50 = 40. Investors are paying ₹40 for each rupee of trailing annual EPS.

Step 3 — Compute forward P/E. If shares and other assumptions remain unchanged, expected EPS = ₹50 × 1.20 = ₹60. Forward P/E = ₹2,000 ÷ ₹60 = 33.3. The lower forward multiple reflects the assumed earnings growth.

Step 4 — Add context. The company's trailing P/E of 40 is below the selected peer average of 50, but that alone does not prove it is cheap. The gap could reflect different growth, margins, balance-sheet risk, business quality or accounting. Its trailing earnings yield is 1 ÷ 40 = 2.5%.

So what? Bharat Consumer trades at a lower P/E than the selected peers, and the forward P/E falls if the forecast growth arrives. But the valuation still depends heavily on the reliability of that forecast and on whether the peer group is genuinely comparable. P/E raises the question—is the price justified by future earnings, quality and risk?—but does not provide the final answer.

Why the P/E Ratio matters—and its limits

P/E is popular because it is quick and intuitive. It can help investors screen an IPO, compare similar companies and understand how much optimism is embedded in a price. Because it uses earnings after interest, tax and depreciation, it captures items that a metric such as EBITDA excludes.

But P/E has important blind spots. When earnings are zero or negative, P/E is generally not meaningful. One-off gains—such as selling land or a subsidiary—can temporarily inflate earnings and make the multiple look deceptively low. P/E says little about debt, cash conversion or capital intensity and does not show how much profit is distributed as a dividend. A high multiple can also leave a stock sensitive to disappointing results, but volatility depends on many factors beyond P/E.

Check yourself
A stock is priced at ₹900 and its EPS is ₹30. What is its P/E ratio, and what does it mean?

P/E = ₹900 ÷ ₹30 = 30. Investors are paying ₹30 for every ₹1 of annual earnings per share. The number should be interpreted against the company's growth, risks, history and comparable businesses; it is not a literal 30-year payback promise.

Key takeaways

  • P/E Ratio = Share Price ÷ EPS; it shows the price paid for each rupee of earnings per share.
  • A high P/E may reflect growth or quality expectations; a low P/E may reflect value, cyclicality or business risk.
  • Use trailing P/E for reported earnings and forward P/E for forecast earnings—do not mix them unknowingly.
  • Compare a stock with relevant peers and its own history rather than applying one universal threshold.
  • Earnings yield is the reciprocal of P/E, but it is not directly equivalent to a guaranteed deposit rate.
  • P/E ignores debt, cash flow, capital needs and earnings quality, so use it with other measures.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good P/E ratio in India?

There is no single good number. A reasonable P/E depends on the company's sector, growth, cyclicality, financial risk, accounting quality and current market conditions. Compare the company with genuinely similar peers and its own historical range.

Is a high or low P/E better?

Neither is automatically better. A low P/E may indicate value or a business facing declining earnings. A high P/E may be supported by strong growth and quality or may reflect excessive optimism. Investigate why the multiple differs.

Can a company have a negative P/E?

A loss produces negative EPS, so a calculation can yield a negative number, but P/E is normally treated as not meaningful in that situation. Analysts may use measures such as price-to-sales or an appropriate enterprise-value multiple, while considering each metric's limitations.

What is the difference between trailing and forward P/E?

Trailing P/E uses reported earnings from the previous 12 months. Forward P/E uses estimated future earnings, so it incorporates expectations but is only as reliable as the forecast.

How is P/E different from EPS?

EPS measures earnings attributable to each share. P/E divides the share price by EPS to express the valuation multiple. EPS is an input to P/E, not a valuation conclusion by itself.

Does the P/E ratio apply to mutual funds?

A mutual fund unit is valued using NAV rather than its own corporate earnings. An equity fund factsheet may report a portfolio P/E calculated from its holdings, which can indicate a value or growth tilt but should not be used alone.
