Home Loan

Home Loan is a secured, long-tenure loan that a bank or housing finance company gives you to buy, build, or renovate a residential property, where the property itself is pledged as collateral and you repay the amount, plus interest, through monthly instalments over a period that often runs from 10 to 30 years.

What a home loan is

A home loan lets you spread the cost of a house over many years instead of paying the full price upfront. The lender disburses a large principal amount — commonly 75% to 90% of the property value, with you funding the rest as a down payment — and you return it through fixed monthly EMIs. Because the home is mortgaged to the lender until you clear the debt, interest rates are lower than on an unsecured loan such as a credit card. Your eligibility, the sanctioned amount, and the rate offered depend heavily on your income, existing obligations, and your credit score (CIBIL). A strong score can shave a meaningful margin off the rate. Home loans sit at the heart of the loans, credit and debt pillar because they are the largest single borrowing most Indian households ever take on.

A longer tenure lowers the EMI but raises the total interest you pay over the life of the loan.

Tax benefits under the old regime

A home loan carries valuable tax deductions, but only under the old tax regime. The principal you repay each year qualifies for deduction within the Section 80C limit of ₹1.5 lakh a year — a limit shared with PPF, life insurance, and other eligible items. The interest you pay on a self-occupied home is deductible under Section 24(b) up to ₹2 lakh a year. Note that if you eventually sell the property, any profit is taxed as capital gains tax, though reinvestment reliefs may apply. Under the new regime these home-loan deductions are largely unavailable, so the regime you choose changes the real cost of the loan.

How to choose and prepay wisely

Compare lenders on the total interest outgo, not just the headline rate, and check processing fees and the spread over the repo rate. For individual borrowers, floating-rate home loans generally carry no prepayment penalty, so making part-prepayments whenever you have surplus cash is one of the most effective ways to cut lifetime interest. Even a modest annual prepayment early in the tenure can save several lakh rupees and shorten the loan by years. Before locking money into prepayment, weigh it against other goals: if a safe fixed deposit earns less after tax than your loan rate, prepaying the loan is usually the better return.

Worked example
Anjali Deshpande in Pune takes a home loan of ₹50,00,000 at a floating rate of 9% a year for a tenure of 20 years. She wants to know her EMI, and how much a one-time prepayment of ₹5,00,000 at the end of year 5 would save her.

Step 1 — Set up the numbers. Principal P = ₹50,00,000. Annual rate = 9%, so the monthly rate r = 0.09 ÷ 12 = 0.0075. Tenure = 20 years, so n = 240 months.

Step 2 — Apply the EMI formula. Using EMI = P × r(1+r)^n ÷ [(1+r)^n − 1], the factor (1.0075)^240 works out to about 6.009. The EMI comes to roughly ₹44,986 a month.

Step 3 — Find the total cost without prepayment. Over 240 months Anjali pays about ₹44,986 × 240 = ₹1,07,96,640. Of that, ₹50,00,000 is principal, so the total interest is about ₹57,96,640 — more than the amount she borrowed.

Step 4 — Apply the prepayment. At the end of year 5 (60 EMIs paid), her outstanding principal is roughly ₹43,90,000. She pays ₹5,00,000 extra, cutting the balance to about ₹38,90,000. Because there is no prepayment penalty on a floating-rate loan, she keeps the EMI the same and lets the tenure fall instead.

So what? A single ₹5,00,000 prepayment early in the tenure saves Anjali roughly ₹12 lakh in interest and frees her of the loan about three years sooner — a far larger benefit than the ₹5 lakh she put in, because it removes years of compounding interest on the balance. Prepaying early, while the interest share of each EMI is highest, is where the impact is greatest.

Q
Under the old tax regime, what is the maximum interest deduction on a self-occupied home loan in a year, and under which section?
A: Up to ₹2 lakh a year under Section 24(b). Principal repayment is separately deductible within the ₹1.5 lakh Section 80C limit.

Key takeaways

  • A home loan is a secured loan where the property is mortgaged until you repay the principal plus interest through monthly EMIs.
  • Floating rates move with the RBI repo rate; fixed rates stay constant for a period but usually start higher.
  • Under the old regime, principal repayment counts within the ₹1.5 lakh Section 80C limit and self-occupied interest is deductible up to ₹2 lakh under Section 24(b).
  • Floating-rate home loans for individuals generally carry no prepayment penalty, so early prepayment is a powerful way to cut interest.
  • A longer tenure lowers the EMI but sharply raises the total interest paid over the life of the loan.

Frequently asked questions

What decides my home loan interest rate?

For most retail loans the rate is floating and linked to the RBI repo rate plus the lender's spread. Your income, tenure, and credit score also shape the rate you are offered.

Should I pick a fixed or floating rate?

Floating rates are usually cheaper over time and let you prepay without penalty, but the EMI can rise if the repo rate goes up. Fixed rates give certainty at a higher starting cost.

Is there a penalty for prepaying?

For individual borrowers, floating-rate home loans generally carry no prepayment penalty. Fixed-rate loans may charge a fee, so check your agreement first.

What tax benefits does a home loan give?

Under the old regime, principal repayment qualifies within the ₹1.5 lakh Section 80C limit and interest on a self-occupied home is deductible up to ₹2 lakh under Section 24(b). The new regime largely removes these.

How does credit score affect my loan?

A higher credit score signals lower risk and can win you a lower rate and a larger sanctioned amount. A weak score may raise your rate or lead to rejection.

Does a longer tenure make the loan cheaper?

No. A longer tenure lowers the monthly EMI but increases the total interest you pay, because the balance stays outstanding for more years.
