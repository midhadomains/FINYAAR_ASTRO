Bull and Bear Market

Bull and Bear Market are the two labels used for the long upward and downward phases of a stock market. A bull market is a stretch in which prices rise broadly and confidence builds, while a bear market is usually marked when a broad index such as the Nifty 50 or the Sensex falls twenty per cent or more from its recent peak. Those thresholds are market convention, not something SEBI or the exchanges define in a rulebook. The labels matter far less than what each phase does to behaviour, because money is lost in the reaction rather than the fall.

Two phases, and the mood attached to each

A bull market is a long climb spread across most sectors rather than one lucky corner, and it feeds on itself. Profits look easy, first-time investors open accounts in numbers, and companies queue up to list through an IPO. A bear market runs the film backwards: volumes thin, listings are shelved, and the business that looked cheap higher up is now called risky.

Neither phase announces itself. The label arrives only once enough of the move has happened for the number to be crossed, which is why Sensex and Nifty commentary confirms a bear market weeks in and a bull market long after the low is gone.

Where the twenty per cent line comes from

The convention is plain arithmetic. Roughly ten per cent off a recent peak is a correction, twenty per cent or more is a bear market, and about twenty per cent up from the low starts the next bull phase. No Indian regulator sets these numbers: SEBI issues no bear market notification and the exchanges stamp no phase on the ticker.

None of the four labels predicts anything. A twenty per cent slide over eighteen months also feels nothing like the same slide in three weeks.

What pushes a phase along

Four forces do most of the work. Earnings set the base, since broad profit growth explains higher prices on its own. Interest rates set the discount applied to those profits, so an easing cycle at the RBI supports valuations and a tightening cycle compresses them. Liquidity decides who is buying, which in India means foreign portfolio flows on one side and domestic SIP money on the other. Sentiment exaggerates the rest.

Bear phases usually begin when two of them turn together, for instance earnings disappointing while the repo rate climbs. The cause shapes the ending: a rate shock fades when the cycle turns, while collapsing profits must first come back.

Measuring the fall, and why the turn stays invisible

The figure that matters to a portfolio is the drawdown, the distance between the highest level the index has reached and where it sits now.

P subscript peak is the highest closing level touched, P subscript now is the current level, and the answer is the share of that peak given up. Because the denominator is the peak and not your entry price, a drawdown deepens all the way down and closes only when a fresh high is printed.

Climbing back costs more than the fall took: a 20 per cent drop needs a 25 per cent gain, a 50 per cent drop a full 100 per cent. The turn stays invisible, because the lowest close becomes the low only once prices have moved away from it, so waiting for calm means buying back higher. Volatility is worst near the bottom.

Worked example
Rohan Deshpande, a software tester in Pune, runs a ₹10,000 monthly SIP into a Nifty 50 index fund for thirty months. Across that stretch the market peaks, slides into bear territory and recovers to a new high. What does the full cycle do to his money, and what would pausing during the fall have cost him?

Step 1, the path the fund took. For ten months the market climbs, with the scheme NAV averaging 240 and then 250, peaking at 256. Months eleven to twenty are the slide, averaging 220 and then 195, with the lowest NAV at 192. The last ten months recover, averaging 215 and then 250.

Step 2, the drawdown at the worst point. From 256 down to 192 is 64 points on a peak of 256, or 25.0 per cent. That is past the twenty per cent convention, so the newspapers were calling it a bear market while Rohan was still transferring ₹10,000 on the fifth of every month.

Step 3, what each block of instalments bought. Every five-month block puts in the same ₹50,000, so the units it buys depend entirely on the price paid. The cheap blocks quietly do the heavy lifting.

Step 4, the outcome. At a closing NAV of 250 his 1,324.57 units are worth ₹3,31,143, a gain of ₹31,143 or 10.4 per cent on the money put in. The fund itself finished only 4.2 per cent above the level at which he began, so most of the gain came from the price he paid, not the distance the market travelled.

Step 5, the cost of pausing. Had he stopped through the fall, he would have skipped ₹1,00,000 of instalments that bought 483.68 units at the cheapest prices of the cycle, finishing with 840.89 units worth ₹2,10,223 on ₹2,00,000 invested. That is 5.1 per cent instead of 10.4.

So what? The bear phase did not damage Rohan, it supplied 484 of his 1,325 units. The damage comes from instalments never made, and those are decided in the months when stopping feels most sensible.

Check yourself
Q
An index falls from a peak of 25,600 to 19,200. What is the drawdown, and what does the label say about the next six months?
A: The drawdown is 6,400 divided by 25,600, or 25 per cent, which crosses the bear market convention. The label describes what has already happened and says nothing about how much further prices fall.

Key takeaways

  • A bull market is a broad sustained rise; a bear market is a fall of about twenty per cent from the peak.
  • Those thresholds are convention, not a SEBI or exchange definition.
  • Earnings, rates, liquidity and sentiment drive both phases, and the cause shapes the ending.
  • Drawdown runs from the highest level reached, and recovery needs a larger percentage gain.
  • A monthly investor buys the most units in the worst phase, so pausing there does the real damage.

Frequently asked questions

Is a bear market officially declared in India?

No. SEBI, the NSE and the BSE publish no such declaration, because the twenty per cent rule is analyst and press convention, so two commentators can date the same phase differently.

What is the difference between a correction and a bear market?

Only the depth of the fall, with about ten per cent called a correction and twenty per cent a bear market. Most corrections stop short of that line, and nobody knows which one will not.

Should I stop my SIP when the market is falling?

A falling market is when a fixed instalment buys the most units, so stopping removes the cheapest purchases of the cycle. Pause for a real reason such as a job loss.

How long do bull and bear markets last?

Bull phases have historically run longer than bear phases, but the range around that average is enormous. Past durations set no schedule for the current one.

Does every stock fall in a bear market?

No. Companies with stable cash flows often fall far less, while leveraged or richly valued ones fall much more. The index is an average hiding a wide spread.

Can anyone reliably spot the turn?

Only in hindsight, once prices have risen far enough above the low. Because the strongest days of a recovery cluster near that point, waiting for clarity usually means re-entering higher.
